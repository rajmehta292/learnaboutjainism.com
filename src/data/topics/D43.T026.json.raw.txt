Jainism’s core concern is the reduction of harm (ahiṃsā) and passions (kaṣāya), not the policing of identity categories as they are understood today. Classical Jain texts develop a sophisticated taxonomy of bodies and desires—liṅga (sex/gender) and veda (sexual desire)—primarily to regulate monastic discipline and to analyze how attachment operates, rather than to legislate modern sexual orientations or gender identities. [[0]](https://www.dbpia.co.kr/journal/articleDetail?nodeId=NODE12886536)\n\nWithin this framework, Jain authors recognize three genders: male (puṃ‑liṅga), female (strī‑liṅga), and a third or neuter sex (napuṃsaka‑liṅga). They also distinguish three forms of desire: desire for women (puṃ‑veda), desire for men (strī‑veda), and desire for both (napuṃsaka‑veda). These categories allow Jain thinkers to describe what we might now call heterosexual, homosexual, and bisexual desire, but they do so in a premodern idiom that is embedded in concerns about asceticism, celibacy, and the management of desire in monastic communities. [[1]](https://journals.library.unt.edu/index.php/sujjs/en/article/view/311) [[0]](https://www.dbpia.co.kr/journal/articleDetail?nodeId=NODE12886536)\n\nModern scholarship has highlighted that Jainism is unusual among classical Indian traditions in systematically separating biological sex (dravyaliṅga) from psychological sexuality (bhāvaliṅga/veda). This separation implies that external bodily characteristics do not necessarily match internal patterns of desire, opening conceptual space for experiences that resemble contemporary LGBTQ realities. At the same time, the ultimate ideal for all—monk, nun, and layperson—is complete celibacy and detachment, so the tradition does not construct a positive theology of sexual expression, queer or otherwise. [[2]](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/4629615.pdf) [[0]](https://www.dbpia.co.kr/journal/articleDetail?nodeId=NODE12886536)\n\nBecause of this, there are clear source limits. No canonical Jain text speaks directly about “LGBTQ” identities, pride, marriage equality, or legal rights; these are modern categories. Primary sources focus on how different desires can obstruct liberation (mokṣa) and how monastic rules should respond to them. Sectarian debates—among Digambara, Śvetāmbara, and Yāpanīya traditions—concern whether women and certain sexual minorities can attain liberation in a female or third‑sex body, or only after rebirth in a male body. These debates show that gender and sexuality are soteriologically significant, but they do not yield a single, uniform doctrinal verdict that can simply be mapped onto contemporary LGBTQ politics. [[0]](https://www.dbpia.co.kr/journal/articleDetail?nodeId=NODE12886536)\n\nRecent academic work and community discussions attempt to read Jain principles—especially ahiṃsā, anekāntavāda (doctrine of many‑sidedness), and aparigraha (non‑possessiveness)—in ways that affirm the dignity of queer persons while acknowledging the ascetic orientation of the tradition. Scholars such as Leonard Zwilling and Michael Sweet, and more recently Holland McCasland and Yang Youngsun, analyze how third‑sex and veda theories function in classical literature and how they might inform modern ethical reflection. [[1]](https://journals.library.unt.edu/index.php/sujjs/en/article/view/311) [[2]](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/4629615.pdf) [[0]](https://www.dbpia.co.kr/journal/articleDetail?nodeId=NODE12886536) Community‑oriented works and interfaith studies, like Devdutt Pattanaik’s exploration of karmic faiths and queer dignity, extend this conversation but operate at a level of interpretation and application rather than strict exegesis of Jain canon. The first two layers belong to the classical Jain world and must be cited precisely; the latter two are interpretive and often contested. Where sources are silent—on same‑sex marriage, gender transition, pronouns, or legal rights—any strong claim about “what Jainism says” exceeds the evidence and should be framed as a proposal or argument, not as binding doctrine. In such areas, consultation with qualified teachers in living Jain lineages is indispensable, especially when personal life decisions, family obligations, or community roles are involved.\n\nIn summary, Jainism offers conceptual tools—three genders, three desires, and a sharp distinction between body and desire—that can help think about LGBTQ experiences within a karmic and ethical framework. Yet these tools arise from ascetic and soteriological concerns, not from a project of affirming or rejecting modern identities.

  "overview": "In Jainism, cosmological maps are visual and textual representations of the structure of the loka—the inhabited universe—used to teach doctrine, guide contemplation, and situate the soul’s journey within a precise, law‑governed cosmos. Classical Jain texts describe the universe as beginningless and uncreated, composed of six eternal substances (dravya), divided broadly into jīva (living beings) and ajīva (non‑living entities such as matter, motion‑medium, rest‑medium, space, and time). These descriptions are then rendered into diagrams and paintings that function as maps: they show where different classes of beings reside, how karmic conditions correspond to regions of existence, and where liberation (mokṣa) lies beyond the structured universe. [[0]](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Religion%3AJain_cosmology)\n\nOne of the most iconic Jain cosmological maps is the loka‑puruṣa, the “cosmic person,” in which the entire universe is depicted in the shape of a standing human figure—broad at the top, narrow at the waist, and broad again at the base. The lower body contains the seven levels of hell (adho‑loka), the narrow waist marks the middle world (madhya‑loka) where humans, animals, and plants live, and the torso and head contain the many heavens (ūrdhva‑loka). At the very top, just beyond the crown, lies siddha‑śilā, the realm of liberated souls who have transcended the cycle of rebirth. This map is not treated as symbolic fiction but as a precise cosmographic diagram, with measurements, subdivisions, and classifications that are elaborated in technical texts and commentaries. [[1]](https://pantheonica.com/jain-cosmology-loka-cosmic-man-cycles-of-time/) [[2]](https://www.leventhalmap.org/projects/jain-map-chat/) [[0]](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Religion%3AJain_cosmology)\n\nAnother major family of Jain cosmological maps focuses on the middle world, especially the Aḍhāī‑dvīpa (“two‑and‑a‑half continents”) and within it Jambūdvīpa, the “Rose‑Apple Continent.” These maps show concentric continents and oceans, the division of Jambūdvīpa into regions (kṣetras) such as Bhārata and Airāvata, and the placement of Mount Meru at the center. They are closely related to, but distinct from, broader Indian cosmographic traditions; Jain versions emphasize the specific regions where human birth is possible and where tīrthaṅkaras appear, thereby linking geography to soteriology. Painted cloth maps from Gujarat and Rajasthan, often displayed in temples or used in teaching, visually encode numerical ratios, distances, and the repetition of similar land‑ocean patterns, reflecting the mathematical rigor of Jain cosmology. [[2]](https://www.leventhalmap.org/projects/jain-map-chat/) [[3]](https://www.hinduwebsite.com/jainism/jain-cosmology.asp)\n\nTextual cosmological maps are found in works like the Tattvārtha Sūtra and various Saṃgrahaṇī and Pannatti texts, which systematically list the three worlds, the layers of hells and heavens, the continents and oceans, and the classifications of beings inhabiting them. These texts provide the underlying schema that artists and scribes translate into diagrams: charts of the six dravyas, tables of the ten regions of Jambūdvīpa and related continents, and detailed enumerations of the heights and widths of cosmic structures. The maps thus operate at multiple levels—didactic, ritual, and contemplative—helping laypeople and monks alike to visualize the consequences of karma, the rarity of human birth, and the fixed location of liberation beyond the top of the loka. [[3]](https://www.hinduwebsite.com/jainism/jain-cosmology.asp) [[0]](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Religion%3AJain_cosmology)\n\nHistorically, cosmological maps have also served as comparative and polemical tools. Jain authors use precise cosmography to argue for an ordered, self‑running universe without a creator deity, contrasting their eternal, cyclic time‑model with other Indian and non‑Indian cosmologies. Modern scholarship and museum exhibitions highlight how these maps combine technical cosmology with artistic conventions—stylized human forms, mandala‑like layouts, and color coding for different realms—while traditional commentaries stress their ethical function: by seeing the vastness and stratification of the universe, practitioners are encouraged to cultivate ahiṃsā, reduce attachment, and aim for the siddha‑śilā beyond all maps.

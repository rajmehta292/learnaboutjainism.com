  "overview": "Kannada literature is the body of written work in the Kannada language, a major Dravidian language primarily associated with the region of present‑day Karnataka in southwestern India. It is one of India’s officially recognised classical languages, with epigraphic evidence from at least the 6th century CE and a continuous literary tradition from about the 9th century onward. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada_literature) [[1]](https://www.britannica.com/art/Kannada-literature) The corpus includes courtly poetry, religious and philosophical verse, narrative epics, didactic works, folk and oral‑derived texts, drama, novels, short stories, essays, and modern experimental writing, reflecting the social, religious, and political history of the region over more than a millennium. [[2]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/literature-and-writing/kannada-literature)\n\nScholars usually divide the history of Kannada language and literature into three broad phases: Old Kannada (approximately 450–1200 CE), Middle Kannada (1200–1700 CE), and Modern Kannada (1700–present). [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada_literature) [[2]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/literature-and-writing/kannada-literature) Old Kannada literature is dominated by Jain authors writing in verse and campu (mixed prose‑verse) forms, often under the patronage of dynasties such as the Western Ganga, Rashtrakuta, Chalukya, Hoysala, and Yadava kings. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada_literature) [[1]](https://www.britannica.com/art/Kannada-literature) The earliest extant major work, the Kavirajamarga (c. 850 CE), is a treatise on poetics and rhetoric that presupposes an already existing tradition of Kannada poetry and grammar, indicating that earlier works—now lost—must have circulated before the 9th century. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada_literature) [[1]](https://www.britannica.com/art/Kannada-literature) Classical Jain poets such as Pampa, Ponna, and Ranna composed mahakavyas (epics) and puranas that adapted pan‑Indian narrative cycles into a Kannada literary idiom, shaping the language’s high style and establishing models of courtly and ethical discourse. [[3]](https://www.jaincollege.ac.in/blogs/famous-kannada-poets-from-pampa-to-kuvempu-history-timeline) [[1]](https://www.britannica.com/art/Kannada-literature)\n\nFrom the 12th century, the rise of the Virashaiva or Lingayat movement transformed Kannada literary culture. Poet‑saints like Basavanna, Allama Prabhu, Akka Mahadevi, and others composed vachanas—short, intense, often aphoristic devotional and reformist poems in rhythmic prose—critiquing caste hierarchy, ritualism, and social injustice while affirming direct, personal devotion to Shiva. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada_literature) [[3]](https://www.jaincollege.ac.in/blogs/famous-kannada-poets-from-pampa-to-kuvempu-history-timeline) [[4]](https://sathee.iitk.ac.in/ml/sathee-cuet/student-corner/article/languages/kannada/kannada-literature-and-famous-works/) These Lingayat texts, usually meant to be recited or sung, introduced a powerful egalitarian and vernacular voice that stood alongside the more court‑centred Jain tradition. In the later medieval and early modern periods, Vaishnava bhakti currents, especially the Haridasa movement, added another devotional strand: composers such as Purandara Dasa and Kanaka Dasa created Kannada kirtanas and devaranamas that combined music, theology, and social reflection, contributing both to literature and to the development of South Indian classical music. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kannada_literature) [[3]](https://www.jaincollege.ac.in/blogs/famous-kannada-poets-from-pampa-to-kuvempu-history-timeline) [[4]](https://sathee.iitk.ac.in/ml/sathee-cuet/student-corner/article/languages/kannada/kannada-literature-and-famous-works/)\n\nMiddle Kannada literature also saw the emergence of narrative prose and mixed forms. Works like Nemichandra’s \"Līlāvatī\" (1370), often regarded as an early Kannada novel or romance, and Ṣaḍakṣaradeva’s \"Rājaśekharavilāsa\" (1657), a morality tale in verse interspersed with prose, show experimentation with plot, character, and ethical dilemmas beyond purely doctrinal exposition. [[1]](https://www.britannica.com/art/Kannada-literature) [[2]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/literature-and-writing/kannada-literature) Authors such as Sarvajna and Shishunala Sharif contributed aphoristic and folk‑inflected verse that circulated widely and shaped proverbial wisdom in Kannada. [[3]](https://www.jaincollege.ac.in/blogs/famous-kannada-poets-from-pampa-to-kuvempu-history-timeline) These developments reflect a gradual broadening of genres—from exclusively religious and royal contexts toward more diverse social and philosophical concerns.\n\nIn the modern period (19th century onward), Kannada literature absorbed new forms such as the Western‑style novel, short story, realistic drama, and lyric poetry, aided by the printing press and commercial publishing. [[2]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/literature-and-writing/kannada-literature) [[1]](https://www.britannica.com/art/Kannada-literature) Writers engaged with colonialism, nationalism, regional identity, gender, caste, and urbanisation, while also reinterpreting classical themes. Major modern and contemporary authors include Kuvempu, D. R. Bendre, Masti Venkatesha Iyengar, V. K. Gokak, U. R. Ananthamurthy, Girish Karnad, Chandrashekhara Kambar, and many others, several of whom have received the Jnanpith Award, India’s highest literary honour. [[3]](https://www.jaincollege.ac.in/blogs/famous-kannada-poets-from-pampa-to-kuvempu-history-timeline) [[2]](https://www.ebsco.com/research-starters/literature-and-writing/kannada-literature) Modern Kannada literature thus stands at the intersection of deep classical roots—Jain, Lingayat, and Vaishnava—and global literary currents, continuing to evolve while maintaining a strong sense of linguistic and cultural identity.",

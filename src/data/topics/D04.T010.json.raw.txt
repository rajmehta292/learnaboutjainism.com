  "overview": "In Jain philosophy, the paired terms \"bhavya\" and \"abhavya\" describe two fundamental dispositions of the soul with respect to liberation (mokṣa). They do not refer to social status, sectarian identity, or moral worth in a simplistic way, but to whether a particular jīva (soul) is, in principle, capable of attaining right faith (samyagdarśana) and ultimately liberation, or not.\n\n\"Bhavya\" designates a soul endowed with bhavyatva—the capacity for liberation. Such a soul will, at some point in the beginningless cycle of existence, come to possess right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct, thereby entering the path that culminates in mokṣa. The term does not guarantee that liberation will occur in a specific lifetime, nor does it imply that the present conduct of a bhavya soul is already spiritually advanced. Rather, it marks a deep, inherent potentiality of the soul that is independent of the temporary rise or subsidence of particular karmas. In classical Jain ontology, this capacity is counted among the inherent dispositions (pāriṇāmika-bhāva) of the soul.\n\n\"Abhavya\" denotes a soul endowed with abhavyatva—the incapacity for liberation. According to the traditional definition, an abhavya soul will never attain right faith and therefore will never traverse the full path to mokṣa. This is not framed as an external decree or punishment imposed from outside, but as a description of the soul’s own beginningless karmic configuration and dispositions. The Tattvārthasūtra and its commentaries emphasize that bhavyatva and abhavyatva are inherent characteristics of jīva, not dependent on the rise (udaya), subsidence (upaśama), destruction (kṣaya), or destruction-cum-subsidence (kṣayopaśama) of particular karmas.\n\nThe locus classicus for this doctrine is Tattvārthasūtra 2.7, which lists three pāriṇāmika-bhāvas of the soul: jīvatva (being a living soul), bhavyatva (capacity for liberation), and abhavyatva (incapacity for liberation). Sarvārthasiddhi, the early Digambara commentary by Ācārya Pūjyapāda, explains that a soul which will attain right faith is called bhavya, while one that will not is called abhavya. This commentary also clarifies that these three are distinctive, soul-specific characteristics, in contrast to more general qualities like existence (astitva) that are shared by both soul (jīva) and non-soul (ajīva). [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084624.html)\n\nWithin the broader Jain doctrinal system, the distinction between bhavya and abhavya intersects with discussions of karma, guṇasthāna (spiritual stages), and omniscience (kevalajñāna). Only an omniscient being is traditionally said to know with certainty whether a particular soul is bhavya or abhavya; ordinary practitioners are cautioned against making such judgments. The doctrine also raises subtle questions about universalism and spiritual equality: all souls are beginningless, conscious entities, yet not all are described as capable of liberation. Classical authors respond by stressing that the classification is descriptive of karmic reality, not a license for discrimination or neglect, and that ethical obligations—non-violence (ahiṃsā), truthfulness, and compassion—apply universally, regardless of whether a soul is bhavya or abhavya.\n\nModern scholarship and contemporary Jain teachers sometimes highlight tensions and interpretive possibilities around the notion of abhavya. Some emphasize the practical focus on one’s own effort and humility, suggesting that the doctrine functions primarily as a metaphysical category rather than a tool for judging others. Others explore how the concept relates to broader Indian debates about grace, determinism, and free will. Across these discussions, a common thread is the insistence that bhavya and abhavya should not be reduced to social labels or used to justify exclusion, but understood within the technical framework of Jain karma theory and spiritual epistemology.",

  "overview": "Shrimad Rajchandra (1867–1901), also reverently known as Param Krupalu Dev, was a Jain poet, mystic, philosopher, and reformer whose short life left a profound impact on modern Jain spirituality and on figures such as Mahatma Gandhi. Born as Lakshminandan (later Raichandbhai Ravjibhai Mehta) in Vavaniya, in present-day Gujarat, he grew up in a religiously mixed environment: his father and grandfather were Vaishnavas, while his mother was a Śvetāmbara Sthānakavāsī Jain. This background exposed him early to both devotional Hindu traditions and Jain ethics of non‑violence, introspection, and renunciation, shaping his later synthesis of deep inner spirituality with respect for scriptural tradition.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrimad_Rajchandra)\n\nFrom childhood, Shrimad Rajchandra displayed extraordinary intellectual and spiritual capacities. At around seven years of age, while observing a cremation, he is said to have experienced jāti‑smaraṇa‑jñāna—recollection of past lives—which intensified his detachment from worldly pursuits and confirmed for him the continuity of spiritual effort across births. His prodigious memory and concentration were later demonstrated through public performances of avadhāna (multi‑focus memory feats), including śatāvadhāna, where he simultaneously retained and reproduced numerous streams of information. Although these feats brought him fame, he eventually discouraged such displays, emphasizing that genuine spiritual progress lay in inner purification, right perception (samyaktva), and disciplined meditation rather than in miraculous powers.  [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atma_Siddhi) [[2]](https://www.shrimadrajchandratrust.org/shrimad-rajchandraji)\n\nIn his twenties, Shrimad Rajchandra undertook an intense study of Jain scriptures and philosophical treatises, alongside broader Indian religious literature. A pivotal moment in his inner journey was his encounter with Saubhagbhai of Sayla, from whom he received guidance in an esoteric contemplative method remembered as “Sudharas”—remaining still in pure awareness. Their spiritual friendship is preserved in more than 250 letters, in which Shrimad articulates subtle experiences of the soul, the nature of bondage, and the path to liberation. These letters, together with his poems and prose works, form a distinctive corpus that combines scriptural fidelity with experiential testimony, and they have become central to several contemporary lineages devoted to his teachings.  [[2]](https://www.shrimadrajchandratrust.org/shrimad-rajchandraji) [[3]](https://www.atmasiddhi.org/shrimad)\n\nShrimad Rajchandra’s most celebrated composition is the Gujarati treatise \"Ātma‑Siddhi Śāstra,\" a 142‑verse poem that expounds six fundamental truths (satapada) about the soul: that the soul exists, is eternal, is the doer, is the enjoyer of karmic fruits, is capable of liberation, and that there is a path to liberation. Written in accessible yet philosophically rich verse, it emphasizes the necessity of right faith, self‑effort, and the guidance of a true teacher for self‑realization. Among his other important works are \"Mokshamālā\" (a didactic text on the stages of spiritual progress), various devotional and reflective poems, and extensive correspondence that interprets Jain doctrines—such as karma, non‑violence, and renunciation—in a way that speaks to lay seekers engaged in worldly life. For many followers, \"Ātma‑Siddhi\" has attained near‑canonical status as a concise map of the path to liberation within a Jain framework.  [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atma_Siddhi) [[2]](https://www.shrimadrajchandratrust.org/shrimad-rajchandraji)\n\nHistorically, Shrimad Rajchandra is also notable for his influence on Mahatma Gandhi. Gandhi records in his autobiography that Shrimad’s clarity, humility, and experiential conviction deeply impressed him and helped shape his understanding of religion, truth, and non‑violence. Gandhi consulted Shrimad on spiritual questions, read texts recommended by him, and regarded him as one of the three major influences on his life. This relationship illustrates how Shrimad’s Jain perspective—centered on inner transformation, ethical discipline, and compassion—could speak beyond sectarian boundaries and contribute to broader discourses on ahimsa and moral responsibility in modern India.  [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Atma_Siddhi) [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shrimad_Rajchandra)\n\nWithin Jain traditions, Shrimad Rajchandra is often described as an enlightened lay seeker who revitalized attention to the experiential core of dharma at a time when ritualism and sectarian disputes were prominent. He did not establish a new sect in the formal sense, but his writings and example have inspired multiple contemporary communities and institutions that emphasize introspective practice, scriptural study, and devotion to the ideal of the liberated soul (siddha) as the true Guru. These communities typically situate his teachings within the broader Śvetāmbara and Digambara scriptural heritage, while highlighting his insistence that external observances must be grounded in inner realization. As with all modern spiritual figures, interpretations of his status and attainments vary, and responsible study places his works in dialogue with classical Jain āgamas, commentaries, and the guidance of qualified living teachers.\n\nShrimad Rajchandra passed away in 1901 in Rajkot at the age of thirty‑three, after a period of intense inner absorption and declining health. His legacy endures through his texts, letters, and the living traditions that read them as a bridge between ancient Jain wisdom and the spiritual questions of modern seekers. For academic and serious religious study, his writings are best approached alongside primary Jain scriptures and historically grounded scholarship, with attention to the distinction between hagiographical accounts, doctrinal exposition, and personal mystical testimony. This layered approach respects both the devotional reverence many hold for him and the need for careful, source‑based understanding of his place in Jain intellectual and spiritual history.

  "overview": "Yogashataka (often spelled Yogśataka or Yogshatakam) is a seminal Jain treatise on yoga attributed to the Śvetāmbara ācārya Haribhadrasūri, generally dated to around the 8th century CE.\n\nThe title literally means \"One Hundred Verses on Yoga\" (yoga-śataka), reflecting its structure as a concise, systematic presentation of yogic theory and practice in approximately one hundred metrical verses, accompanied in the traditional manuscripts by Haribhadra’s own auto-commentary (svopajñavṛtti). The work belongs to a broader corpus of Haribhadra’s writings on yoga and philosophy, in which he engages with non-Jain systems such as Pātañjala Yoga, Sāṃkhya, and Buddhist thought, while reinterpreting them through a Jain doctrinal lens.\n\nIn Yogashataka, Haribhadra outlines stages of spiritual development, types of yogic practitioners, and the ethical and contemplative disciplines required for liberation. He emphasizes that genuine yoga is inseparable from right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct—the three jewels (ratnatraya) of Jainism—and that any yogic path must be evaluated by its capacity to reduce karmic bondage and cultivate non-violence, self-restraint, and equanimity. The text thus serves both as a comparative map of Indian yogic traditions and as a normative guide for Jain spiritual practice.\n\nHistorically, Yogashataka has been important for several reasons. First, it is one of the earliest systematic attempts by a Jain author to classify and compare multiple yoga systems, often using a neutral and respectful tone while still affirming Jain priorities. Second, it shows how medieval Jain thinkers participated in the wider pan-Indian discourse on yoga, borrowing terminology and conceptual frameworks but re-situating them within Jain metaphysics of jīva (soul), karma, and liberation. Third, the text influenced later Jain yoga literature and commentarial traditions, and modern critical editions—such as those prepared by Muni Puṇyavijaya and others—have made it a key source for scholars studying the history of yoga and interreligious philosophical dialogue.\n\nFrom a practice-oriented perspective, Yogashataka does not present yoga merely as physical postures or breath control. Instead, it treats yoga as a graded discipline of inner transformation, beginning with ethical purification and progressing through increasingly subtle forms of concentration and insight. Haribhadra’s classifications of yogins and his insistence on the primacy of moral and cognitive clarity anticipate later Jain discussions of dhyāna (meditation) and guṇasthāna (spiritual stages). For lay Jains, the text offers a framework for understanding how widely known yogic ideas can be integrated responsibly into Jain soteriology, without compromising core commitments such as ahiṃsā and anekāntavāda.\n\nModern scholarship has highlighted Yogashataka as a crucial bridge text: it connects Jain canonical and post-canonical traditions with the broader history of Indian yoga, and it demonstrates that Jain authors were not isolated but actively engaged in intellectual exchange. Critical editions and studies have also drawn attention to textual variants, the structure of the auto-commentary, and the ways in which Haribhadra adapts non-Jain categories. While some details of dating and manuscript transmission remain debated, the consensus places Yogashataka among the most influential early Jain yoga treatises, alongside works like Haribhadra’s Yogabindu and Yogadṛṣṭisamuccaya.\n\nBecause Yogashataka is both doctrinal and practical, contemporary readers—whether practitioners or scholars—often approach it with dual aims: to understand Jain perspectives on yoga historically, and to explore how its emphasis on ethical clarity, non-violence, and disciplined contemplation might inform modern engagements with yoga beyond purely physical practice.",

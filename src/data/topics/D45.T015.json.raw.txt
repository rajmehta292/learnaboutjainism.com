  "overview": "Genetic engineering is a set of scientific techniques that allow humans to directly modify the genetic material (DNA or RNA) of living organisms. In contemporary biology, this includes methods such as recombinant DNA technology, transgenic modification, CRISPR-Cas gene editing, and synthetic biology. These tools can alter traits in microorganisms, plants, animals, and humans, with aims that range from basic research and agriculture to medicine, such as gene therapy for inherited diseases.\n\nFrom a Jain perspective, genetic engineering is not discussed explicitly in canonical texts, which were composed long before modern biology. However, Jain doctrine offers a detailed ontology of living beings (jīva), non-living substances (ajīva), and karmic processes that can be used to evaluate new technologies. Central to this evaluation is ahiṃsā (nonviolence), understood not only as avoiding physical harm but also as restraining harmful intentions, passions, and carelessness (pramāda). The tradition classifies life forms by the number of senses they possess and recognizes subtle, pervasive forms of life, including one-sensed beings such as plants and microorganisms. This broad recognition of life means that genetic engineering, which often manipulates microorganisms, plants, animals, and human embryos, must be assessed in terms of direct and indirect harm to many kinds of jīvas.\n\nRecent Jain bioethical scholarship has begun to apply these classical principles to contemporary technologies. Works such as Brianne Donaldson and Ana Bajželj’s \"Insistent Life: Principles for Bioethics in the Jain Tradition\" and Donaldson’s article \"Bioethics and Jainism: From Ahiṃsā to an Applied Ethics of Carefulness\" argue that Jain ethics can contribute a nuanced framework for evaluating medical interventions, including reproductive technologies and gene-based therapies. They highlight four key concerns: the pervasiveness of life forms, the role of desires and aversions in motivating technological use, the distinction between direct and indirect modes of harm, and the obligation to reduce harm in one’s specific context.\n\nApplied to genetic engineering, this framework suggests several guiding questions rather than a single, uniform verdict. First, what kinds of beings are affected—microbial, plant, animal, or human—and with how many senses? Second, does the intervention reduce suffering and harmful karmic entanglements, or does it intensify attachment, exploitation, or violence? Third, are there alternatives that involve less harm, such as non-invasive therapies or lifestyle changes? Fourth, how do social structures—economic pressure, inequality, or commercial interests—shape the use of genetic technologies in ways that may increase systemic violence?\n\nWithin Jain communities, views on genetic engineering are diverse and evolving. Some lay Jains and scholars see potential value in carefully regulated medical uses, such as gene therapies that alleviate severe hereditary disease, provided they are pursued with restraint, transparency, and attention to long-term consequences. Others express concern that genetic modification of embryos, animals, or crops may encourage commodification of life, intensify factory farming, or lead to ecological disruption, all of which conflict with the ideal of minimizing harm to all jīvas. There is also awareness that genetic technologies can be used for eugenic or discriminatory purposes, which would be incompatible with Jain commitments to equality of souls and the karmic nature of difference.\n\nBecause Jainism emphasizes personal responsibility and contextual judgment rather than centralized dogmatic rulings, many contemporary authors recommend that decisions about genetic engineering—especially in medicine—be made in consultation with qualified healthcare professionals, informed Jain teachers, and family members, while keeping ahiṃsā and carefulness (apramatta) as central criteria. Canonical texts provide the ethical grammar, but they do not replace the need for modern scientific understanding and professional guidance. For complex cases involving serious illness, reproductive decisions, or long-term ecological impact, a Jain-informed approach will typically combine scriptural reflection, community dialogue, and expert medical advice, rather than relying on a single textual proof.\n\nIt is important to note that there is no universally accepted Jain position on genetic engineering at present. Scholarship and community discussions are ongoing, and different Jain traditions (Digambara, Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka, Sthānakavāsī, Terāpanth) may emphasize different aspects of practice—such as ascetic restraint, lay vows, or social engagement—when thinking about new technologies. This plurality of voices reflects a broader Jain commitment to anekāntavāda, the doctrine of many-sidedness, which encourages recognition of multiple perspectives and cautions against absolutist claims. In the context of genetic engineering, anekāntavāda supports careful, multi-perspectival deliberation that weighs scientific data, ethical principles, and lived experience before acting.\n\nFor anyone personally facing decisions about genetic testing, gene therapy, or reproductive technologies, Jain sources and modern scholarship can offer ethical orientation, but they cannot replace individualized medical, legal, or psychological counsel. Especially where health, pregnancy, or serious illness is involved, consultation with qualified professionals and, where appropriate, experienced Jain teachers or counselors is strongly recommended.

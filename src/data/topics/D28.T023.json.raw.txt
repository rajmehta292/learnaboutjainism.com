  "overview": "Anuttaraupapatikadasa (Prakrit: \"Anuttaraupapātikadaśā\"; often rendered in English as \"Ten Chapters on Those Born in the Highest Heavens\") is a canonical Jain scripture belonging to the Śvetāmbara tradition. It is classified as the ninth Anga (limb) of the twelvefold Jain Āgama corpus and is traditionally said to have been promulgated by Lord Mahāvīra and composed by his gaṇadhara (chief disciple) Sudharmasvāmin.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anuttaraupap%C4%81tikada%C5%9B%C4%81h) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_09_ang_09_anuttaropapatik_sutra_stahanakvasi_003449_003449) The text occupies a central place in narrative and devotional study because it presents exemplary biographies of ascetics whose rigorous spiritual practice leads to rebirth in the highest celestial realms (Anuttara Vimānas) and ultimately to liberation (mokṣa).\n\nThe title itself signals the focus of the work: \"anuttara\" refers to the \"unsurpassed\" or highest heavens in Jain cosmology, while \"upapātika\" denotes beings who arise or are reborn there, and \"daśā\" indicates a set of chapters or conditions.  [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/anuttaropapatikdasha_sutra_004192_004192) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_09_ang_09_anuttaropapatik_sutra_anuttaraovavai_terapanth_003565_003565) The text describes how such souls, through intense tapas (austerities), strict observance of vows, and deep internal transformation, attain birth in these superior realms. After completing their celestial lifespan, they are destined to be reborn in auspicious human circumstances—often in Mahāvideha Kṣetra—where they encounter a living Tīrthaṅkara and finally achieve mokṣa.  [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/anuttaropapatikdasha_sutra_004192_004192) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_09_ang_09_anuttaropapatik_sutra_stahanakvasi_003449_003449)\n\nStructurally, Anuttaraupapatikadasa is divided into three vargas (sections), which together contain a series of chapters (adhyayanas) narrating the lives of approximately thirty-three great souls, though the exact enumeration and ordering can vary slightly across manuscript traditions and sectarian recensions.  [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_09_ang_09_anuttaropapatik_sutra_stahanakvasi_003449_003449) [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/aagam_09_anuttaropapatik_dasha_moolam_evam_vrutti_004109_004109) The first varga is commonly described as having ten chapters, the second thirteen, and the third ten, each centered on particular princes, monks, or laymen whose spiritual journeys exemplify the path from worldly entanglement to ascetic renunciation and celestial rebirth. These narratives are not merely biographical; they function as didactic case studies illustrating the fruits of right faith (samyag-darśana), right knowledge (samyag-jñāna), and right conduct (samyag-cāritra).\n\nWithin the broader Jain literary landscape, Anuttaraupapatikadasa is notable for its vivid portrayal of extreme asceticism and the psychological transformation of its protagonists. The text recounts episodes of prolonged fasting, fearless acceptance of hardship, and unwavering adherence to non-violence and self-restraint, often under testing social and familial circumstances.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Anuttaraupap%C4%81tikada%C5%9B%C4%81h) [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/aagam_09_anuttaropapatik_dasha_moolam_evam_vrutti_004109_004109) Figures such as Abhayakumāra, Dhanyakumāra (Dhanṇa Anagāra), and various sons of King Śreṇika are depicted as moving from royal privilege to mendicant life, thereby demonstrating that spiritual greatness is measured not by birth or wealth but by inner discipline and detachment. These stories have been repeatedly retold in sermons, commentaries, and modern educational materials, making the text a bridge between technical doctrine and accessible narrative.\n\nFrom a doctrinal perspective, the scripture underscores several key Jain themes: the operation of karma, the hierarchy of celestial realms, the superiority of liberation over sensual enjoyment, and the importance of scriptural study. Many protagonists are shown mastering the eleven Angas and practicing careful observance of guptis (restraints) and samitis (carefulness), thereby integrating learning with conduct.  [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/anuttaropapatikdasha_sutra_004192_004192) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_09_ang_09_anuttaropapatik_sutra_stahanakvasi_003449_003449) The text implicitly warns against mistaking heavenly rebirth for the final goal; Anuttara heavens are presented as exalted yet still within saṃsāra, and the narratives emphasize that only the destruction of karmic bondage through right practice leads to the ultimate state of siddhahood.\n\nHistorically, Anuttaraupapatikadasa has been transmitted in Prakrit, with later Sanskrit, Hindi, Gujarati, and English translations and commentaries produced by both Mūrtipūjaka and Sthānakavāsī scholars, as well as Terāpanthī projects of critical editing.  [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_09_ang_09_anuttaropapatik_sutra_anuttaraovavai_terapanth_003565_003565) [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/aagam_09_anuttaropapatik_dasha_moolam_evam_vrutti_004109_004109) Traditional commentaries, such as that of Abhayadeva Sūri, and modern critical editions by Acharya Tulsi, Muni Nathmal (Mahāpragya), and others, have sought to preserve the canonical text while clarifying difficult passages and aligning variant readings. Contemporary English expositions, including illustrated editions, present the gāthās alongside vernacular explanations, making the scripture accessible to youth and lay practitioners while maintaining its status as a revered Anga within the Śvetāmbara canon.",

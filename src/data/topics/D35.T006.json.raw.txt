  "overview": "Digambara Terapanth is a reformist sub‑tradition within the Digambara branch of Jainism that crystallized in the 17th century in the Jaipur region of present‑day Rajasthan, particularly around Sanganer, Amer, and Jaipur. It emerged as a distinct current over against the older Digambara mainstream, which retrospectively came to be called Bispanthi, and is therefore best understood as an internal Digambara reform rather than a separate sect outside the Digambara fold. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digambara_Terapanth)\n\nHistorically, Terapanth grew out of the Adhyatma (“inner spirituality”) movement that began in Agra around 1626 CE (V.S. 1683), associated with lay intellectuals such as Banarasidas and later poets like Dyanatrai. These circles emphasized introspective spirituality, scriptural study, and a critical stance toward what they perceived as ritual excess and institutional domination. Within this climate, prominent merchants Amra Bhaunsa Godika and his son Jodhraj Godika of Sanganer led a group that, between about 1664–1667, openly opposed the authority of Bhattaraka Narendrakirti of Amber, the local representative of the Digambara bhattaraka hierarchy. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digambara_Terapanth)\n\nThe name “Terapanth” is traditionally explained as referring to a group of thirteen (tera) individuals who cooperated to build a new temple and establish a reformed pattern of worship; another interpretive strand reads “tera” as “your” (i.e., the Lord’s) path, but the numerical etymology is the one most commonly cited in historical sources. In the royal capital of Jaipur, King Jai Singh II (1688–1743) recognized the growing division by sponsoring separate temples for Bispanthi and Terapanthi Digambaras, indicating that the distinction had become socially and ritually salient by the early 18th century. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digambara_Terapanth)\n\nDoctrinally, Digambara Terapanth does not introduce new philosophical principles beyond classical Digambara Jainism; rather, it reconfigures practice and authority. Terapanthis affirm core Digambara teachings on karma, non‑violence (ahiṃsā), non‑possession (aparigraha), and the path to liberation, and they accept the same major Digambara scriptural and doctrinal corpus (such as the Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama and the works of Kundakunda) as normative. Their reform lies in insisting on direct engagement with scripture and inner spiritual discipline (adhyātma‑sādhana), while rejecting what they saw as accretions: elaborate ritual offerings, the worship of minor deities, and the mediating authority of bhattarakas in temple life. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digambara_Terapanth) [[1]](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Digambar_Jain)\n\nIn ritual practice, Terapanthis oppose the worship of subsidiary gods and goddesses and restrict devotional focus to the Jinas (Tirthankaras). They are known for rejecting the use of flowers and certain other perishable offerings in temple worship, arguing that such practices conflict with non‑violence and simplicity. Over time, some of these reforms—especially the avoidance of flowers—spread beyond Terapanth circles and influenced broader North Indian Digambara practice, blurring the visible boundary between Terapanthi and non‑Terapanthi communities in some regions. Nevertheless, Terapanthi identity remains marked by a self‑conscious emphasis on scriptural purity, restrained ritual, and lay‑driven spiritual initiatives. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digambara_Terapanth)\n\nSeveral influential lay scholars and authors are associated with the Terapanth movement, including Daulatram Kasliwal and Pandit Todarmal, whose works systematized Digambara doctrine for lay audiences and reinforced the movement’s focus on rational, text‑based exposition of the path to liberation. Todarmal’s son, Gumaniram, later founded a stricter sub‑tradition known as Gumanapantha in the 1770s, sometimes described as “śuddha Terapantha amana” (pure Terapanth tradition), which tightened ritual rules and further minimized external forms of worship. Bakhtaram’s 18th‑century drama “Mithyātva‑khaṇḍana‑nāṭaka” preserves early narrative memories of the founding group and their opposition to bhattaraka control. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digambara_Terapanth)\n\nFrom a historical‑critical perspective, much of what is known about Digambara Terapanth comes from later narrative, community histories, and modern scholarship, and there is some uncertainty about details such as the exact composition of the founding group, the internal diversity of early Adhyatma circles, and the degree to which royal patronage shaped the movement’s trajectory. The broad contours, however—17th‑century origins in the Jaipur region, roots in the Adhyatma movement, opposition to bhattaraka authority, reform of ritual practice, and enduring influence on North Indian Digambara worship—are consistently attested across independent sources. Modern researchers therefore treat Terapanth as a paradigmatic example of an early‑modern Jain reform movement that remained firmly within the doctrinal boundaries of its parent sect while significantly reshaping its institutional and ritual life.",

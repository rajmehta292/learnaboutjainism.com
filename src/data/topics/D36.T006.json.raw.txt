  "overview": "Sthulabhadra (often written Sthulibhadra or Sthūlabhadra) is a pivotal early Jain ācārya of the Śvetāmbara tradition, traditionally dated to the late 4th–3rd century BCE and associated with the courtly and intellectual milieu of Magadha and the Nanda–Maurya transition. He is remembered both as a great ascetic and as a guardian of scriptural memory at a time when famine, political change, and internal weakness threatened the continuity of the Jain canon. [[0]](https://www.jainqq.org/explore/000021/322) [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sthulabhadra)\n\nAccording to Śvetāmbara pattāvalī (lineage lists) and narrative texts, Sthulabhadra was the son of Sakatala, a minister in the Nanda kingdom, and the brother of Śrikaya. When his father died, he refused the post of chief minister and instead chose the path of renunciation, becoming a disciple of the ācāryas Sambhutavijaya and Bhadrabahu. These accounts situate him in Pāṭaliputra, the capital of Magadha, and portray him as moving from a life entangled in courtly pleasures—especially his attachment to the famed dancer Rūpkosā/Kosā—to a life of rigorous asceticism. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sthulabhadra) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/sthulibhadrasuri_249058_249058)\n\nHagiographical narratives emphasize a crucial turning point: after spending years in the company of Rūpkosā, Sthulabhadra experiences a profound inner transformation and renounces worldly life at about thirty years of age. He undertakes severe ascetic discipline under Sambhutavijaya, eventually conquering sensual desire to such an extent that later texts praise him as a \"kāma-vijeta\" (conqueror of passion). His story is frequently used in Jain pedagogy to illustrate how deep attachment can be transformed into detachment and how genuine renunciation requires both inner resolve and guidance from a competent teacher. [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/sthulibhadrasuri_249058_249058) [[3]](https://jainqq.org/explore/006643/2)\n\nIn the Śvetāmbara tradition, Sthulabhadra is closely linked with the preservation of the Āgamas after a devastating twelve-year famine in Magadha. Under Bhadrabahu, he is said to have mastered eleven of the twelve Aṅga Āgamas, while the twelfth, the Dṛṣṭivāda containing the fourteen Purvas, remained only partially transmitted. When famine scattered the saṅgha and weakened the monks’ capacity to maintain strict discipline and accurate oral transmission, Sthulabhadra later convened a council at Pāṭaliputra to recompile the scriptures. Eleven Aṅgas were reconstructed from collective memory, but the Dṛṣṭivāda and its Purvas could not be fully recovered, marking a decisive loss in the tradition’s scriptural corpus. [[0]](https://www.jainqq.org/explore/000021/322) [[3]](https://jainqq.org/explore/006643/2)\n\nA famous episode in later narrative sources describes Sthulabhadra studying the remaining Purvas under Bhadrabahu in Nepal. Having acquired extraordinary yogic powers through this study, he allegedly transformed himself into a lion to impress his nun-sisters. Bhadrabahu, displeased by this display of pride and misuse of spiritual attainment, is said to have curtailed further transmission of the Purvas to him. This story is often interpreted as a cautionary tale about the ethical use of spiritual power and the dangers of subtle ego even in advanced practitioners. [[0]](https://www.jainqq.org/explore/000021/322) [[3]](https://jainqq.org/explore/006643/2)\n\nWithin Śvetāmbara lineage lists, Sthulabhadra appears as an important successor in the chain of teachers from Mahāvīra, sometimes counted as the eighth ācārya in that succession. He is also described as a \"śrutakevalī\"—one who has complete mastery of the scriptures—though this designation is understood differently across sects and modern scholarship. In devotional and liturgical contexts, his name is recited alongside Mahāvīra and Gautama as auspicious, reflecting his enduring status as a model ascetic and guardian of doctrine. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sthulabhadra) [[3]](https://jainqq.org/explore/006643/2)\n\nFrom a historical-critical perspective, Sthulabhadra’s life is known primarily through later texts such as Hemacandra’s 12th‑century \"Parisistaparvan\" and the Śvetāmbara \"Kalpa Sūtra\" tradition, which combine historical memory with didactic and hagiographical aims. These sources situate him in the broader narrative of the early Jain saṅgha’s struggles with famine, migration, and the eventual sectarian differentiation between Digambara and Śvetāmbara communities, though details and dates vary and must be treated with caution. Modern scholarship generally accepts his existence as an early Jain leader but debates the precise chronology and extent of his scriptural knowledge, noting that the loss of the Purvas is a complex process rather than a single event.

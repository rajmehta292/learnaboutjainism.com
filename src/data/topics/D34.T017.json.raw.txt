  "overview": "Central India, in the Jain historical and cultural imagination, refers primarily to the belt of Malwa, Bundelkhand, Avanti, and adjoining regions of present‑day Madhya Pradesh and parts of Uttar Pradesh and Rajasthan. In many Jain narrative and historical sources this area appears under names such as Avanti, Dasharna, Jaijakabhukti, and Malwa, and is remembered as a major centre of Jain communities, temples, scholastic activity, and mercantile networks from at least the early centuries of the Common Era through the medieval period.\n\nJain narrative texts connect Central India to very early sacred geography. Ādipurāṇa, for example, describes how Ṛṣabhadeva (Adinātha), the first Tīrthaṅkara, divided India into fifty‑two janapadas, listing Avanti with its capital at Ujjayinī (Ujjain) as one of them. This embeds the region into a cosmological and mytho‑historical framework, making cities like Ujjain part of the remembered landscape of tīrthas and royal centres associated with Jain dharma. [[0]](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/26906071.pdf?ab_segments=0/basic_search_gsv2/control&initiator=search-results)\n\nFrom roughly the 8th to 12th centuries CE, Central‑Western India—especially Malwa and Bundelkhand—saw a flourishing of Jain religious life under dynasties such as the Gurjara‑Pratīhāras, Paramāras, and Candellas. Epigraphic records from places like Deogarh (Lalitpur), Vidisha, and Osian attest to the construction of Jain temples dedicated to Tīrthaṅkaras such as Śāntinātha, and to organized Jain communities (saṅghas and gacchas) under the guidance of ācāryas. Even when ruling houses were not themselves Jain, they often permitted or indirectly supported temple building and mercantile patronage, allowing Jain institutions to thrive. [[0]](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/26906071.pdf?ab_segments=0/basic_search_gsv2/control&initiator=search-results) [[1]](https://wikiland.org/fr/Jainism_in_Bundelkhand)\n\nBundelkhand, covering the northern part of Madhya Pradesh and the south‑western part of Uttar Pradesh, is repeatedly described in modern and traditional accounts as an ancient centre of Jainism. It hosts numerous tīrthas—Vidisha, Deogarh, Lalitpur, Khajuraho, Kundalpur, Sonagir, Nainagiri, and others—where rock‑cut images, temple complexes, and inscriptions document the presence of Digambara communities, monastic lineages, and lay groups such as Parwar, Golapurva, Golalare, and Terapanthi Jains. These communities maintained strong regional identities while remaining integrated into wider Jain networks through sanghas and ganas. [[1]](https://wikiland.org/fr/Jainism_in_Bundelkhand)\n\nMalwa, with Ujjain as a major urban centre, functioned as a significant headquarters for Jain activity in several periods. Jain pattāvalīs (pontifical genealogies) and later historical works remember ācāryas such as Maghachandra, Amitagati, Mahāsena, Dhanapāla, and others who taught, wrote, and oversaw temple consecrations in and around Ujjain during the Paramāra era. Sculptural and inscriptional evidence from sites like Bhojpur, Dashpur, Karedi, and Ujjain itself shows a rich tradition of Tīrthaṅkara images—Adinātha, Neminātha, Śāntinātha, Pārśvanātha—often accompanied by yakṣa‑yakṣiṇī figures such as Gomukhi, Padmāvatī, and Cakreśvarī. [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/malva_ki_parmarkalin_jain_pratimaye_211726_211726)\n\nUnder the Sultans of Malwa and later the Mughal administration, Jain merchants and officials continued to play notable roles in the regional economy and governance. Records mention Jain treasurers and wazirs, and inscriptions even refer to matrimonial ties between Malwa sultans and Jain families. Travelogues and administrative documents from the Mughal period describe towns like Sironj and Chanderi as having substantial banya (often Jain) populations, indicating that Central India remained a stronghold of Jain mercantile and temple life into the early modern era. [[3]](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/44147014.pdf)\n\nIn the broader history of Jainism, Central India thus appears as a bridge region: geographically between the older heartlands of Magadha and the western centres of Gujarat and Rajasthan, and historically between early narrative sacred geography and later epigraphically documented communities. Its temples, inscriptions, and local Jain communities contribute significantly to our understanding of how Jain dharma was practiced, patronized, and localized across different political regimes, while still remaining connected to pan‑Indian doctrinal and monastic traditions.",

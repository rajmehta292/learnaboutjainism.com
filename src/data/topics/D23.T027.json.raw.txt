  "overview": "Naminatha (also spelled Nāminātha or Nami) is revered in Jain tradition as the twenty‑first Tirthankara of the present avasarpinī (descending time cycle). As a Tirthankara, he is understood not merely as a historical or legendary figure, but as a perfected being who rediscovered and re‑taught the eternal Jain dharma in his era. In canonical lists of the twenty‑four Jinas, Naminatha appears immediately before Ariṣṭanemi (Neminatha) and Pārśvanatha, forming part of the sequence of “intermediate” Tirthankaras whose lives are sketched in brief in early Śvetāmbara sources.\n\nTraditional narrative accounts describe Naminatha’s birth in a royal kṣatriya family, most commonly in the city of Mithilā, with King Vijaya (or Vijay) as his father and Queen Vipravati (Viprāvatī) as his mother. His conception is accompanied by the standard series of auspicious dreams seen by his mother, signaling the descent of a Tirthankara‑soul. As a prince, Naminatha is portrayed as compassionate, intelligent, and inclined toward non‑violence, even while fulfilling worldly responsibilities. Over time, witnessing the impermanence of power, wealth, and relationships, he develops deep vairāgya (dispassion) and resolves to renounce worldly life.\n\nAt a mature age—given in traditional cosmological calculations as many thousands of years, reflecting the vast temporal scales of Jain universal history—Naminatha performs the great renunciation. He abandons his royal status, undertakes the ritual of hair‑plucking, and becomes a mendicant ascetic. In this phase, texts emphasize his practice of severe tapas (austerities), strict adherence to non‑violence (ahiṃsā), truthfulness, non‑possessiveness, and careful restraint of body, speech, and mind. Through prolonged meditation and spiritual discipline, he eventually attains kevala‑jñāna, the state of omniscience in which all substances and their modes are directly known without obstruction.\n\nAfter attaining kevala‑jñāna, Naminatha is described as a kevalin, teaching the path of liberation to both monks and lay followers. His samavasaraṇa (divine preaching assembly) becomes the setting in which he expounds core Jain doctrines: the nature of the soul (jīva), the structure of the cosmos (loka), the workings of karma, and the disciplines necessary for liberation. Although specific sermons are not preserved in detail in the earliest canonical texts, later narrative and didactic literature associates him with the same fundamental teachings attributed to all Tirthankaras—emphasizing non‑violence, restraint, and right faith, knowledge, and conduct.\n\nIn Jain cosmology, Naminatha’s life is framed by precise numerical attributes: his height, lifespan, the duration of his spiritual practices, and the epoch in which he appears. These figures vary slightly across textual traditions but consistently present him as a mahāpuruṣa (great person) whose physical and temporal dimensions far exceed those of ordinary humans. His emblem (lāñchana) is commonly given as the blue lotus, and his associated tree (śrīvṛkṣa) and attendant deities are specified in later iconographic manuals. Such details guide temple iconography and ritual visualization, ensuring that images of Naminatha conform to established tradition.\n\nFrom the perspective of practice, Naminatha functions as an object of devotion (bhakti) and contemplation rather than as a source of new, distinct doctrines. Jain laypeople and monks may recite his name in liturgical lists of the twenty‑four Tirthankaras, meditate upon his qualities of compassion and detachment, and visit temples where his image is installed. Festivals such as Paryuṣaṇa, during which the Kalpa Sūtra is publicly recited in Śvetāmbara communities, often include brief references to Naminatha within the broader narrative of the Tirthankaras. In Digambara traditions, cosmological and narrative texts likewise affirm his place in the canonical sequence of Jinas.\n\nModern scholarship notes that, compared to Mahāvīra, Pārśvanatha, or Neminatha, the historical and textual record for Naminatha is relatively sparse. Early canonical works focus more on the last four Tirthankaras, leaving the intermediate ones—including Naminatha—described in concise formulaic passages. As a result, much of what is popularly known about Naminatha today comes from later narrative compilations, iconographic manuals, and community education materials that systematize the attributes of all twenty‑four Tirthankaras. These sources aim to integrate Naminatha into a coherent devotional and doctrinal framework, while acknowledging that detailed biographical episodes are limited in the earliest strata of the Jain canon.\n\nBecause of this textual situation, careful study of Naminatha involves distinguishing between (a) brief canonical references that establish his existence, sequence, and basic attributes, and (b) later elaborations that fill out his story in line with the general Tirthankara pattern. For serious academic or religious study, it is important to consult verified editions of primary texts and, where possible, traditional commentaries, and to seek guidance from qualified teachers within living Jain lineages when questions of practice or ritual arise.",

  "overview": "Jivabhigama (often found under the fuller title Jivajivabhigama or Jiva‑Jivabhigama) is a canonical Jain scripture belonging to the group of Upanga Agamas, traditionally counted as the third Upanga and the fourteenth text in some Svetambara Agam lists. It is classified as an \"abhigama\"—a treatise of systematic knowledge—whose central concern is the precise understanding of jiva (living, conscious soul) and ajiva (non‑living, non‑conscious entities) as the two fundamental categories of reality. By organizing the vast diversity of beings and substances into rigorously defined classes, the text provides a foundational ontology for later Jain philosophy and practice.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jivajivabhigama_sutra_part_01_004194_004194) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_14_upang_03_jivabhigam_sutra_part_01_stahanakvasi_003454_003454)\n\nThe work opens by situating itself within the broader Agamic tradition, attributing its doctrinal authority to the teachings of the Tirthankaras as transmitted and systematized by sthavira‑bhagavantas (elder monks). It emphasizes that all phenomena in the universe can ultimately be understood through the interplay of jiva and ajiva; other key notions such as punya (merit), papa (demerit), asrava (influx of karma), bandha (bondage), samvara (stoppage), and moksha (liberation) are treated as consequences of the relations between these two basic ontological poles. In this way, Jivabhigama functions not merely as a catalog of beings but as a bridge between metaphysics and soteriology, showing how correct knowledge of the soul and non‑soul underlies right conduct and the path to liberation.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jivajivabhigama_sutra_part_01_004194_004194) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jiva_jiva_bhigam_sutra_229824_229824)\n\nStructurally, the text is organized into nine pratipattis (distinct modes or viewpoints of exposition). The first pratipatti treats both jiva and ajiva together, laying out the basic twofold division of reality and the principal sub‑categories of non‑soul, such as rupi‑ajiva (material, formful) and arupi‑ajiva (immaterial, formless), including dharmastikaya (medium of motion), adharmastikaya (medium of rest), akashastikaya (space), kala (time), and various forms of pudgala (matter). Subsequent pratipattis focus increasingly on jiva, presenting multiple, non‑contradictory classifications of worldly souls (samsarasamapannaka) according to movement (sthavara and trasa), gender (female, male, neuter), realm of existence (hell‑beings, animals, humans, gods), and other attributes. This multi‑perspectival method exemplifies anekantavada, the Jain doctrine of manifold viewpoints, by showing how the same soul can be described validly in different ways depending on the chosen angle of analysis.  [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jiva_jiva_bhigam_sutra_229824_229824) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_14_jivajivabhigam_sutra_hindi_anuwad_034681_034681) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_14_upang_03_jivabhigam_sutra_part_01_stahanakvasi_003454_003454)\n\nWithin the broader Jain intellectual history, Jivabhigama is especially important for its detailed treatment of samsari jivas—the souls bound in the cycle of birth and death. It distinguishes them from siddha jivas (liberated souls) and then elaborates the many conditions, capacities, and limitations of embodied beings. By clarifying the difference between self and non‑self (bheda‑vijnana), the text aims to cultivate right knowledge (samyag‑jnana) as a prerequisite for right faith and right conduct. Later commentarial traditions and modern expositions draw heavily on Jivabhigama when explaining Jain cosmology, ethics of non‑violence toward all life forms (including earth, water, fire, and air‑bodied beings), and the technical vocabulary of karma theory. Although preserved and interpreted somewhat differently across Svetambara sub‑traditions (including Murtipujaka and Sthanakavasi lineages), its core doctrinal role as a systematic guide to the nature of jiva and ajiva remains widely acknowledged.

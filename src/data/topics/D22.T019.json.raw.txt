  "overview": "In Jain studies, the term \"meter\" refers primarily to poetic and liturgical prosody—chandas—rather than the modern SI unit of length. Jain acharyas adopted and extended the broader Indian science of prosody (chandashastra) to structure Prakrit and Sanskrit verse used in scriptures, commentaries, stotras, and didactic poetry.\n\nIn classical Indian poetics, chandas is defined as a regulated arrangement of syllables and morae (mātrā), governed by rules of quantity (laghu–guru), pauses (yati), and rhythmic movement (gati). Jain prosodists emphasize that meter is not merely decorative: it preserves doctrinal content, aids memorization, and creates aesthetic delight (rasa) that supports right faith (samyak-darśana). The etymology of \"chandas\" is linked to roots meaning \"to please\" and \"to cover\", indicating that metrical form both delights the listener and envelops meaning in a patterned linguistic body.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/chando_ratnamala_002289_002289) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/chandonushasanam_229276_229276)\n\nJain literature distinguishes Vedic meters from laukika (non-Vedic or conventional) meters, focusing mainly on the latter for narrative, philosophical, and devotional works. Within laukika meters, Jain authors classify patterns by syllable-count (mātrika or jāti-chandas), letter-count (varṇika or akṣara-chandas), and sometimes by metrical feet (gaṇa-chandas). Core technical concepts include the measurement of short syllables (laghu, one mātrā) and long syllables (guru, two mātrās), the role of long vowels, anusvāra, visarga, and consonant clusters in making a syllable heavy, and the use of prastāra (systematic tabulation) to derive and analyze metrical schemes.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/chando_ratnamala_002289_002289) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jain_chand_shastra_parampara_210637_210637)\n\nWithin Jain Prakrit literature, the gāthā meter occupies a central place. Many canonical and post-canonical texts—such as narrative collections and doctrinal verses—are composed in gāthā, whose structure is treated systematically in specialized Jain prosodic treatises. These works show how Prakrit phonology, with its particular vowel system and consonant clusters, interacts with quantitative meter. Medieval Jain acharyas used Prakrit meters not only for literary elegance but also for sectarian dialogue and reconciliation, crafting formulaic verses that emphasize shared ethical and soteriological foundations across traditions.  [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/chandonushasanam_229276_229276) [[3]](https://horizon-jhssr.com/articles/authors/pdf-version-18/JHSSR-1318-2025.pdf)\n\nHistorically, Jain contributions to the science of meter form a distinct strand within Indian chandashastra. Jain prosodic texts engage with earlier authorities such as Pingala while introducing original classifications and terminologies. They are closely connected to Jain grammar, poetics, and commentarial traditions, and they reflect the intellectual environments of both Digambara and Śvetāmbara communities. Modern scholarship on Indian metrics highlights how Jain treatments of meter participate in the wider Sanskrit and Prakrit prosodic discourse, while preserving sect-specific concerns such as doctrinal clarity, non-violence in language, and accessibility for lay audiences.

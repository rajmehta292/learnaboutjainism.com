  "overview": "Mulachara (Mūlacāra), often translated as “Fundamental Conduct,” is a major Digambara Jain scripture that systematically sets out the code of life for mendicant monks and, by extension, provides a framework for understanding ideal ascetic discipline in the Jain tradition. It is generally dated to around the 2nd century CE and is composed in a Prakrit dialect closely related to Jain Sauraseni, reflecting an early North Indian linguistic milieu, even though the text circulated widely in later South Indian Digambara communities.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulachara)\n\nThe work is traditionally attributed to Acharya Vattakera, a prominent teacher of the Mulasangha, and is regarded in Digambara circles as an Āgama-sthānīya grantha—meaning a text that, while not part of the Śvetāmbara canonical Āgamas, holds near-canonical authority for defining monastic conduct. Later Sanskrit commentators such as Vasunandi explicitly name Vattakera as the author, and modern critical studies tend to support this attribution, while also documenting earlier manuscript traditions that occasionally associate the text with the more famous Acharya Kundakunda.  [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/mulachar_229189_229189) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/mulachar_ka_samikshatmak_adhyayana_002088_002088)\n\nMulachara is divided into twelve chapters and contains over 1,200 verses (gāthās). These chapters cover the twenty-eight fundamental virtues (mūlaguṇas) of monks, detailed rules of renunciation (pratyākhyāna), daily discipline from waking to resting (samayācāra), fivefold conduct (pañcācāra: faith, knowledge, conduct, austerity, and energy), purification of alms (piṇḍa-śuddhi), the six essential duties (ṣaḍ-āvaśyaka), the twelve contemplations (dvādaśānuprekṣā), and other topics that together form a comprehensive manual of ascetic life. The text insists on rigorous observance of nudity (acelakatā), non-possession, vigilance in movement and speech, and strict regulation of food intake, presenting these not as external formalities but as supports for inner purification and progress toward liberation (mokṣa).  [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/mulachar_ek_parichay_229846_229846) [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/mulachar_purvardha_001838_001838)\n\nHistorically, Mulachara has also functioned as a bridge between Digambara and Śvetāmbara textual worlds. Comparative philological work has shown that many verses in Mulachara closely parallel or even duplicate passages found in Śvetāmbara texts such as the Dasavaikalika and other Prakīrṇakas, suggesting that both major Jain sects once shared a broader ascetic textual tradition that later differentiated. This shared layer is important for understanding Jain history: Mulachara is not merely a sectarian replacement for the lost Digambara Ācārāṅga, but part of a wider, early Jain discourse on mendicant discipline.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Mulachara) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/mulachar_ka_samikshatmak_adhyayana_002088_002088)\n\nIn contemporary Digambara practice, Mulachara remains a key reference for defining the ideal life of nude monks (munis) and, in adapted form, for clothed ascetics and lay followers who study its teachings. Commentaries, translations, and modern introductions—such as those by Vasunandi, Gyanmati Mataji, and Dr. Phool Chand Jain Premi—continue to interpret its prescriptions in dialogue with changing historical, social, and ethical contexts. For serious students, Mulachara offers both a window into early Jain ascetic ideals and a demanding blueprint for the disciplined pursuit of non-violence, truthfulness, non-stealing, celibacy, and non-possession at the highest possible intensity.",

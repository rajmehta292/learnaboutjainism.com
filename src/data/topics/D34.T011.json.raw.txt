  "overview": "Western India, in the context of Jain history and culture, primarily refers to the regions of Gujarat, Rajasthan, and Maharashtra, with occasional inclusion of parts of Madhya Pradesh and coastal Karnataka depending on historical period and administrative usage. For Jain traditions, Gujarat and Rajasthan in particular became major strongholds from the early centuries of the Common Era onward, while Maharashtra developed important pilgrimage sites and monastic centres. These regions together form one of the densest concentrations of Jain communities, temples, and educational institutions in the world.  [[0]](https://jainworld.com/library/jain-books/books-on-line/jainworld-books-in-indian-languages/antiquity-of-jainism/jainism-in-western-india/) [[1]](https://jainworld.jainworld.com/jainbooks/explain/e3.htm)\n\nFrom a Jain historical perspective, Western India gained prominence after the gradual migration of Jain communities from the eastern Gangetic heartland (Magadha and Videha) toward the west, a process often dated to around the 3rd century BCE. The decline of Jain influence in Magadha and the rise of new political powers there coincided with the expansion of Jain merchants and ascetics into Saurashtra, Gujarat, and Rajasthan. Over time, these regions became centres of Śvetāmbara Jainism, while southern India emerged as a stronghold of Digambara Jainism. The famous council at Valabhi (in present-day Gujarat), held in the 5th century CE, is traditionally remembered as the occasion when the Śvetāmbara canon was first systematically written down, underscoring Western India’s role in textual preservation and institutional consolidation.  [[0]](https://jainworld.com/library/jain-books/books-on-line/jainworld-books-in-indian-languages/antiquity-of-jainism/jainism-in-western-india/) [[2]](https://www.jainheritagecentres.com/jainism/history/history-of-jainism/)\n\nPolitically, Western India hosted a succession of dynasties that offered significant patronage to Jain communities. The Rāṣṭrakūṭas, Caulukyas (Solankis) of Anahilavāḍa-Pāṭan, Gurjara-Pratīhāras, Cāhamānas (Chauhans), Guhilas of Mewar, and later regional houses in Gujarat and Rajasthan supported temple construction, endowments, and scholastic activity. Under rulers such as Siddharāja Jayasiṃha and Kumārapāla of Gujarat—strongly influenced by the polymath ācārya Hemacandra—Jainism enjoyed a period of remarkable architectural and literary flourishing. Merchant communities, ministers, and local elites also played a decisive role, funding temple-cities like Śatruñjaya (Palitana), Girnar, and Mount Abu, which became iconic pilgrimage centres for Śvetāmbara Jains and, in some cases, for Digambaras as well.  [[0]](https://jainworld.com/library/jain-books/books-on-line/jainworld-books-in-indian-languages/antiquity-of-jainism/jainism-in-western-india/) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/some_early_jaina_temples_in_western_india_250310_250310) [[1]](https://jainworld.jainworld.com/jainbooks/explain/e3.htm)\n\nCulturally and socially, Western India’s Jain communities contributed substantially to the region’s ethos of non-violence, vegetarianism, and commercial networks. Jain merchants were active in inland and maritime trade, linking Western India to North India, the Deccan, and overseas markets. Their economic strength enabled sustained patronage of temples, libraries (bhandāras), and scholastic lineages. Over centuries, Western India became home to major Jain centres of learning, including Pāṭan, Ahmedabad, Jaisalmer, and other towns where manuscripts were copied, preserved, and commented upon. The architectural heritage—ranging from early rock-cut caves and structural temples to the highly ornate marble complexes at Dilwara (Mount Abu) and Ranakpur—illustrates the evolution of Jain art and ritual space in dialogue with broader regional styles.  [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/some_early_jaina_temples_in_western_india_250310_250310) [[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Jainism)\n\nReligiously, Western India is especially associated with Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka traditions, though Sthānakavāsī and Terāpanthī communities also have a strong presence, particularly from the early modern period onward. The region’s Jain practice includes temple worship of tīrthaṅkara images, observance of major festivals such as Paryuṣaṇa and Mahāvīr Jayantī, and support for itinerant monks and nuns who travel between towns and villages. At the same time, Western India has been a site of internal reform movements—iconoclastic and non-image-worshipping currents, scriptural re-evaluations, and modern educational initiatives—shaped by colonial encounters, nationalist politics, and global diasporic connections. Today, Western India remains a central hub for Jain pilgrimage, scholarship, and community life, while also serving as a launching point for Jain diasporas to East Africa, the United Kingdom, North America, and beyond.  [[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/History_of_Jainism) [[1]](https://jainworld.jainworld.com/jainbooks/explain/e3.htm)\n\nIn comparative perspective, Western India’s Jain heritage cannot be separated from the wider religious and cultural landscape of the region. Jain temples often stand alongside Hindu, Buddhist, and later Islamic and Sikh sites, and Jain thinkers have historically engaged with Vedānta, Nyāya, Buddhist philosophy, and modern social thought. The region’s multilingual environment—Gujarati, Rajasthani dialects, Marathi, and earlier Prakrit and Sanskrit—has shaped Jain literary production, from canonical recensions and commentaries to narrative literature and ethical manuals. For students of Jainism, Western India thus offers a crucial case study in how a non-Vedic śramaṇa tradition adapted to changing political orders, economic structures, and cultural currents while maintaining a distinctive commitment to ahiṃsā, anekāntavāda, and disciplined ascetic and lay practice.",

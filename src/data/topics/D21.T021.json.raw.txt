  "overview": "In Jain traditions, what many scholars and museums call “mandalas” are primarily cosmological diagrams that map the structure of the universe (loka) and the path of the soul within it. These diagrams include circular maps of the middle world (madhya-loka) with concentric continents and oceans, vertical depictions of the cosmic person (loka-puruṣa), and complex temple floor plans and wall paintings that encode doctrinal ideas in geometric form. They are not merely decorative art: they function as visual scriptures, teaching aids, and ritual supports that condense vast cosmological and ethical systems into a single ordered image.  [[0]](https://www.leventhalmap.org/projects/jain-map-chat/) [[1]](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/711314)\n\nThe best-known Jain cosmological mandalas are maps of the “two-and-a-half continents” (aḍhāi-dvīpa or adhaidvipa). At the center lies Jambūdvīpa, the Rose-Apple Continent, with Mount Meru as its axis. Around it are ring-shaped oceans and continents—Lavaṇa-samudra (Salt Ocean), Dhātakīkhaṇḍa-dvīpa, Kālodadhi (Black Water Ocean), and Puṣkaradvīpa—often rendered as concentric circles filled with fish, aquatic beings, mountains, rivers, and human settlements. In the four corners or borders, artists frequently place temples with Jinas, attendants, and worshippers, visually linking cosmology to devotion. These cloth paintings (paṭas) and later printed diagrams are used in teaching, ritual display, and sometimes circumambulation, allowing laypeople and monks to “walk” the map of the universe and reflect on their place within it.  [[2]](https://www.davidrumsey.com/luna/servlet/detail/RUMSEY~8~1~281766~90054435) [[1]](https://www.metmuseum.org/art/collection/search/711314)\n\nAnother major Jain mandala form is the depiction of the universe as the cosmic person, loka-puruṣa. Here the three worlds are mapped onto a standing human-like figure: hells (adho-loka) in the lower body, the middle world (madhya-loka) as a narrow band at the waist, the heavens (ūrdhva-loka) rising through the torso, and the realm of liberated souls (siddha-śilā) at the crown. This image is deeply mathematical and doctrinal: texts specify dimensions, subdivisions, and symmetries, and the diagram embodies the principle that the universe is beginningless, self-ordered, and governed by law rather than a creator deity. For practitioners, contemplating such a mandala reinforces key Jain insights—especially the vastness of saṃsāra, the rarity of human birth, and the urgency of ethical discipline (ahiṃsā, self-restraint) as the only means to ascend from lower realms to liberation.  [[0]](https://www.leventhalmap.org/projects/jain-map-chat/) [[3]](https://smarthistory.org/jain-cosmology/) [[4]](https://pantheonica.com/jain-cosmology-loka-cosmic-man-cycles-of-time/)\n\nWithin temples, especially in Śvetāmbara mūrti-pūjaka settings, geometric floor designs and altar layouts also function as mandala-like structures. Yantras and auspicious diagrams may be placed beneath or around images of the Jinas, organizing space according to cosmological and karmic principles. While these are less standardized than the great cosmological maps, they share the same logic: symmetry, repetition, and precise proportion express the ordered nature of reality and the soul’s potential for purification. Digambara traditions likewise preserve cosmological diagrams in manuscripts and paintings, though terminology, numerical details, and iconographic conventions can differ from Śvetāmbara usage; careful comparison of editions and commentaries is required to avoid overgeneralizing from one sect to all Jains.  [[3]](https://smarthistory.org/jain-cosmology/)\n\nModern scholarship often groups Jain cosmological diagrams under the broad category of “mandalas” because they resemble Hindu and Buddhist sacred circles. Yet Jain examples are distinctive in several ways. They emphasize cartographic accuracy according to scriptural cosmology, use extremely large numbers and precise measurements, and foreground ethical and soteriological messages rather than deity-centered worship. They are also embedded in specific ritual and pedagogical contexts: displayed in temples and homes, studied with reference to canonical and commentarial texts, and interpreted by teachers who connect each ring, mountain, and region to doctrines of karma, rebirth, and the gunasthānas (stages of spiritual development). Where translations or reproductions are based on unverified OCR or secondary summaries, details of labeling and proportion can be uncertain, so serious study of Jain mandalas relies on verified editions (Tier A), traditional commentaries (Tier B), and curated museum documentation (Tier D) rather than uncorroborated images (Tier E–F).\n\nBecause these diagrams encode complex doctrine, they also reveal areas of disagreement and interpretive diversity. Śvetāmbara and Digambara authors agree on the broad three-world structure but may differ on names of continents, exact counts of heavens and hells, and some measurements; museum labels sometimes harmonize these differences without noting them. Scholars debate how early certain visual conventions arose and whether some diagrams were primarily didactic, ritual, or meditative. Translation of technical cosmological vocabulary—such as loka-ākāśa, aloka-ākāśa, and specific mountain and ocean names—can be imprecise, and some English renderings (“world of mortals,” “cosmic man”) are interpretive rather than literal. For practice decisions—such as using a mandala in meditation, ritual vows, or children’s education—Jain ontology guidelines recommend consulting qualified teachers within a living lineage, since an image alone cannot convey the full ethical and karmic implications of the cosmology it represents.",

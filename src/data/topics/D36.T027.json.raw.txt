  "overview": "Acharya Shri Shantisagar (Satgauda/Satgonda; c. 1871–1872 to 1955) is widely regarded as the pioneering modern Digambara Jain acharya who revived the rigorous, nude ascetic tradition in the twentieth century after a long period of decline. Born into a devout Digambara Jain Patil family in the Belgaum (now Belagavi) district of Karnataka—most sources place his birth in Belgul/Yelgula near Bhoj village—he grew up in an agrarian and trading environment shaped by strong religious values, especially his mother Satyavati’s emphasis on non‑violence, truthfulness, and ethical conduct. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shantisagar) [[1]](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-99-2485-1_5) From childhood he displayed physical strength, a calm temperament, and a marked inclination toward temple worship, scriptural study, and meditation, which later became the foundation of his monastic discipline. [[1]](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-99-2485-1_5) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/acharya_shantisagarji_maharaj_249010_249010)\n\nHis early life included a brief child marriage, common in his social context, but the death of his young wife and his own deep sense of detachment led him to embrace lifelong celibacy. As a young adult he managed his family’s cloth business with notable honesty, using the shop as a place for self‑study and informal religious discourse. Pilgrimages—especially to major Jain sites such as Sammed Shikhar and Girnar—intensified his resolve to renounce worldly life. During these journeys he adopted personal vows like eating only once a day and renouncing ghee and oil, which foreshadowed the austerities of his later ascetic career. [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/maha_tapasvi_acharya_shri_shantisagarji_maharaj_250405_250405) [[4]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Shantisagar)\n\nShantisagar’s formal entry into the Digambara monastic order occurred in the second decade of the twentieth century. He first received kṣullaka and then ailaka dīkṣā (intermediate ascetic stages) under senior monks such as Devendrakirti (Devappa Swami) and Ādisagar (Ankalikar), eventually becoming a full Digambara muni around 1919–1920. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shantisagar) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/maha_tapasvi_acharya_shri_shantisagarji_maharaj_250405_250405) In 1922, at Yarnal village in Karnataka, he was given the monastic name “Shantisagar,” meaning “Ocean of Peace,” reflecting both his tranquil disposition and his aspiration toward inner equanimity. His subsequent elevation to the rank of acharya made him the first major Digambara preceptor of the twentieth century, and he was later honored with the title “Charitra Chakravarti” (Emperor of Conduct) for his exemplary adherence to monastic vows and his role in re‑establishing strict ascetic standards. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shantisagar) [[4]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Shantisagar)\n\nOne of Shantisagar’s most historically significant contributions was the public reassertion of nude Digambara asceticism in regions where it had nearly disappeared. Accounts from modern scholarship describe how, at a time when colonial legal frameworks and social norms discouraged public nudity, he nonetheless led processions and traveled widely across India, including North India, as a nude monk. This was not merely symbolic; it was understood within Digambara theology as a restoration of the ideal of complete non‑possession and radical renunciation. His example inspired a new generation of Digambara munis and acharyas, and by the late twentieth and early twenty‑first centuries, the number of Digambara monks had grown dramatically compared to the near‑extinction reported in the early 1900s. [[1]](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-99-2485-1_5) [[4]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Shantisagar)\n\nShantisagar’s life is also remembered for his intense scriptural engagement and ethical teaching. Biographical texts and community narratives portray him as deeply versed in classical Digambara works (such as those of Kundakunda and other acharyas) and as a teacher who emphasized ahimsa (non‑violence), aparigraha (non‑possession), and disciplined daily practice for both monks and laypeople. While these biographies are devotional and hagiographical in tone, they consistently link his authority to his lived practice rather than to institutional power. Modern academic studies highlight that his charisma and moral rigor helped re‑legitimize Digambara monasticism in the eyes of both lay Jains and the broader public, especially in regions where reformist or less ascetic forms of Jainism were gaining prominence. [[1]](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-99-2485-1_5) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/acharya_shantisagarji_maharaj_249010_249010)\n\nShantisagar concluded his life through sallekhana (also called samādhi‑maraṇa), the ritual fast unto death, at Kunthalgiri in 1955, after a prolonged period of gradually reducing food intake and intensifying spiritual focus. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shantisagar) [[4]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Shantisagar) Within Digambara tradition, this is interpreted as a final act of non‑violent withdrawal from embodied existence, undertaken with full awareness and equanimity. From a modern ethical and medical perspective, sallekhana is a subject of ongoing debate and requires careful, case‑specific guidance from qualified religious and medical authorities; Shantisagar’s own fast is described in sources as voluntary, deliberate, and supported by a community that viewed it as the culmination of his ascetic path. His legacy continues in the monastic lineages and lay communities that trace their revival of strict Digambara practice to his example, and in the many biographies, commemorative conferences, and scholarly studies that treat him as a central figure in the history of modern Jainism.\n\nBecause most detailed accounts of Shantisagar’s life come from twentieth‑century hagiographical biographies (community texts) and later scholarly syntheses, there are minor discrepancies in dates (1871 vs. 1872), place‑names (Belgul/Yelgula/Yarnal), and specific narrative details. These variations reflect differences in regional memory, calendar systems (Vikram Samvat vs. Common Era), and editorial choices rather than fundamental disagreement about his role: all major sources concur that he was a key Digambara acharya who revived nude monasticism and shaped the modern Digambara landscape.",

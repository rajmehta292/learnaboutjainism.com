  "overview": "Sthānakavāsī (often spelled Sthanakavasi or Sthanakvasi) is a major non‑image‑worshipping sub‑tradition within Śvetāmbara Jainism, emerging in western India between the fifteenth and seventeenth centuries. The name literally means “hall‑dweller” or “meeting‑house resident,” indicating the community’s preference for congregational spaces (sthanak) rather than ornate temples with images. As a Śvetāmbara group, Sthānakavāsīs share core Jain doctrines—ahiṃsā (non‑violence), anekāntavāda (non‑one‑sidedness), karma theory, and the pursuit of mokṣa—but they interpret the canonical tradition in a way that excludes image‑worship and emphasizes strict ascetic discipline and lay ethical reform.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sth%C4%81nakav%C4%81s%C4%AB) [[1]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Sthanakavasi)\n\nHistorically, the doctrinal roots of Sthānakavāsī thought are traced to the lay reformer Loṅkā Śāh (Lonka, Lumpaka) in fifteenth‑century Gujarat. Working as a copyist of manuscripts, Loṅkā gained unusual access to Śvetāmbara canonical texts and concluded that the oldest scriptures neither prescribe temple construction nor image‑worship. He argued that lavish temple rituals, donations for building projects, and the breaking of flowers and other offerings violated ahiṃsā and distracted from the path of liberation. Loṅkā’s critique targeted the caityavāsī (temple‑dwelling) monks and the Tapāgaccha establishment, accusing them of lax discipline and scripturally unsupported practices. Although Loṅkā himself did not found a lasting monastic order, his iconoclastic reading of the canon became the intellectual precursor for later non‑image‑worshipping lineages.  [[2]](https://philtar.ac.uk/encyclopedia/jainism/sthan.html) [[3]](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/451551)\n\nThe specifically Sthānakavāsī mendicant traditions crystallized in the sixteenth and seventeenth centuries, when reformers known as the pañca muni (“five monks”) and later figures such as Lava of Surat organized ascetic communities that rejected image‑worship, insisted on strict observance of the accepted scriptures, and adopted distinctive external disciplines such as the permanent mouth‑cloth (muhpattī). These groups differentiated themselves from earlier Loṅkā‑inspired movements (Lonkāgaccha and its sub‑branches) by establishing durable monastic lineages and congregational structures. By the seventeenth century, “Sthānakavāsī” designated Śvetāmbara ascetics and laity who performed religious activities in simple halls, focused on scriptural study, meditation, confession (pratikramaṇa) without images, and ethical preaching, rather than elaborate temple ritual.  [[1]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Sthanakavasi) [[3]](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/451551)\n\nDoctrinally, Sthānakavāsīs accept thirty‑two of the Śvetāmbara Āgamas and reject thirteen texts that they regard as later accretions or as supporting image‑worship. They argue that the authentic teachings of Mahāvīra and the early tradition emphasize internal worship (bhāva‑pūjā)—cultivation of right faith, knowledge, and conduct—rather than external ritual directed toward images. For Sthānakavāsīs, building temples and installing images entails harm to innumerable subtle life‑forms through quarrying stone, construction, and ritual offerings; therefore, such practices contradict ahiṃsā and aparigraha (non‑possessiveness). Their scriptural hermeneutics highlight passages on non‑violence, ascetic restraint, and meditation, while downplaying or reinterpreting later ritual developments.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sth%C4%81nakav%C4%81s%C4%AB) [[2]](https://philtar.ac.uk/encyclopedia/jainism/sthan.html)\n\nIn terms of visible practice, Sthānakavāsī monks and nuns wear white robes like other Śvetāmbara ascetics but are distinguished by the continuous use of the square muhpattī, a cloth mouth‑shield tied behind the ears. This is understood as an outward expression of ahiṃsā, minimizing the inhalation or harm of tiny organisms in the air. They typically avoid temple‑based rituals, do not perform image‑worship, and emphasize itinerant preaching, scriptural exposition, and lay moral reform. Lay Sthānakavāsīs attend discourses in meeting halls, observe fasting and confession practices, and participate in community organization that is less centered on temple management and more on ethical discipline and study.  [[2]](https://philtar.ac.uk/encyclopedia/jainism/sthan.html) [[3]](https://soas-repository.worktribe.com/OutputFile/451551)\n\nThe Sthānakavāsī tradition has also played a role in the broader landscape of Jain sectarian history. From within Sthānakavāsī circles emerged the Terāpanth (“followers of the thirteen precepts”), founded by Ācārya Bhikṣu in the eighteenth century, which further systematized non‑image‑worshipping Śvetāmbara practice under a single ācārya’s authority. Modern Sthānakavāsī teachers have contributed to the global dissemination of Jain ethics, especially ahiṃsā, through interreligious dialogue and peace movements. At the same time, debates continue—both within Jain scholarship and among sectarian communities—about the historical influences on Loṅkā’s iconoclasm (including possible interactions with Islamic aniconism), the precise dating of early non‑image‑worshipping movements, and the status of different canonical texts. These disagreements highlight that Sthānakavāsī identity is shaped by both scriptural interpretation and historical reform, and that its claim to represent the “original” Jain path is contested by other Jain traditions.

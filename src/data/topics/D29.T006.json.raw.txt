  "overview": "Mahādhavalā (often written as Mahadhavala) is the name given to the final and most extensive section of the Dhavalā commentary composed by Ācārya Vīrasena on the Digambara Jain canonical text Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama. In Digambara tradition, Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama is regarded as the foremost preserved scripture (āgama) derived from the ancient oral transmission of Mahāvīra’s teachings, especially portions of the fifth Aṅga (Vyākhyā Prajñapti / Viahapannatti) and the twelfth Aṅga (Dṛṣṭivāda / Diṭṭhivāda). Mahādhavalā, as the concluding part of this massive commentary project, represents a culmination of technical exegesis on karma theory, cosmology, and doctrinal classification, and is counted among the siddhānta texts that safeguard remnants of the lost Aṅgas in the Digambara lineage.\n\nThe Dhavalā commentary as a whole is said to comprise around 72,000 ślokas on Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama, with Mahādhavalā designating its last section. Ācārya Vīrasena, working within the scholastic environment of early medieval South India, received Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama and related materials such as Kāśyaprabṛhita through a lineage of teachers and produced detailed Sanskrit–Prakrit commentaries that systematize earlier doctrinal strands. Mahādhavalā, together with Dhavalā and Jayadhavalā (the commentary on Kāśyaprabṛhita), is therefore central to the Digambara “siddhānta” corpus: Dhavalā and Mahādhavalā on Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama, and Jayadhavalā on Kāśyaprabṛhita.  [[0]](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Religion%3AShatkhandagama)\n\nDoctrinally, Mahādhavalā is especially important for its treatment of karma as a complex, multi‑layered reality involving innumerable varieties, binding conditions, and modes of fruition. It elaborates on categories already present in Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama—such as the classification of living beings (jīvasthāna), the structure of karmic influx (āsrava), bondage (bandha), and the stages of spiritual development (guṇasthāna)—and integrates them with sophisticated logical and epistemological discussions. Later Digambara authors and commentators draw heavily on Mahādhavalā when explaining technical points about dravya (substance), guṇa (quality), paryāya (mode), and pramāṇa (means of valid knowledge), so that the text functions both as scriptural exegesis and as a doctrinal encyclopedia.\n\nFrom a textual‑historical perspective, Mahādhavalā is preserved in palm‑leaf manuscripts and modern printed editions that often present the original Prakrit sūtras of Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama followed by Vīrasena’s mixed Sanskrit–Prakrit commentary. Critical editions prepared in the twentieth century—such as the Amravati series of Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama with Dhavalā—have made portions of Mahādhavalā available to scholars, though the sheer size and technical density of the work mean that only selected parts have been translated into modern languages.  [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/shatkhandagama_pustak_01_001395_001395) [[2]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Shatkhandagama_Pustak_03/001397)\n\nIn contemporary Jain studies, Mahādhavalā is approached as a key witness to early Digambara scholasticism and as a bridge between the more aphoristic style of earlier teachers (e.g., Kundakunda) and the expansive, systematizing commentarial tradition. It is not typically used as a beginner’s text for lay practice; instead, it serves advanced students, monastic scholars, and researchers interested in the historical development of karma theory, cosmology, and epistemology. Because of its technical nature and its status as part of a lineage‑bound siddhānta, guidance from qualified teachers and access to reliable editions are considered essential for serious study.\n\nGiven its canonical weight and doctrinal complexity, Mahādhavalā is best understood not as an isolated book but as the final movement in a larger symphony of Digambara scriptural preservation: the oral teachings transmitted through Dharasena, written down by Puṣpadanta and Bhūtabali in Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama, and then expansively interpreted by Vīrasena in Dhavalā and Mahādhavalā.

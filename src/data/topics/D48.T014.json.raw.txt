  "overview": "In Jain traditions, \"original educational fiction\" refers to narrative works that are consciously crafted as fictional or semi-fictional stories, yet designed primarily to teach doctrine, ethics, and philosophical reflection rather than to provide mere entertainment. These texts use invented plots, stylized characters, and imaginative settings as vehicles for conveying core Jain ideas such as ahiṃsā (non‑violence), karma theory, right faith, and the path to liberation. Rather than being peripheral, such narratives form a major strand of Jain literary culture across Prakrit, Sanskrit, Apabhraṃśa, and later vernaculars.\n\nFrom early canonical and commentarial periods onward, Jain authors distinguished between stories that simply excite the senses and those that guide the listener or reader toward dharma. Later surveys of Jain narrative literature describe classifications such as akathā (aimless or non‑edifying tales), kathā (morally purposeful narratives), and vikaṭhā (improper stories that stimulate sensuality), alongside fourfold schemes like artha‑kathā (wealth‑oriented), kāma‑kathā (desire‑oriented), dharma‑kathā (religious), and miśra‑kathā (mixed). Within these systems, original educational fiction corresponds most closely to dharma‑kathā and its subtypes—stories that expose the flaws of irreligion, evoke detachment, or lead to inner equanimity—while still employing the creative freedom of literary art.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jain_katha_sahitya_ek_samikshatamaka_sarvekshan_229645_229645)\n\nMedieval Jain authors developed highly sophisticated forms of educational fiction. Somadeva Sūri’s Sanskrit champu work *Yaśastilaka* (959 CE) exemplifies this: it narrates the spiritual journey of prince Yaśodhara through romance, political intrigue, and multiple rebirths, yet the narrative frame is densely interwoven with systematic expositions of Jain metaphysics, ethics, cosmology, and soteriology. The text alternates ornate prose and verse, demonstrating that fictional storytelling can coexist with rigorous doctrinal instruction and courtly literary aesthetics.  [[1]](https://dhwani.ink/works/yasastilaka-somadeva-suri.html) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jain_vidwan_ke_sandarbha_me_somadevasuri_krut_yashstilaka_champoo_me_210862_210862)\n\nAnother major tradition of educational fiction is the *Dharmaparīkṣā* (“Examination of Religion”), a didactic and often satirical narrative designed to convince audiences of the correctness of Jain teachings over rival systems. First composed in Apabhraṃśa and later adapted in Sanskrit and various vernaculars, the *Dharmaparīkṣā* tradition uses fictional debates, tests, and narrative frames to critique Brahmanical ritualism and other doctrines, while guiding readers toward Jain understandings of dharma. Its repeated adaptation over centuries shows how educational fiction could be reshaped for new linguistic, regional, and social contexts while retaining its pedagogical core.  [[3]](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/10/5/308) [[4]](https://brill.com/display/title/74066)\n\nModern scholarship on Jain narrative literature highlights that these works are not merely ancillary to doctrinal treatises but central to how Jain communities have transmitted values, negotiated identity, and engaged in inter‑religious polemic. Educational fiction allows complex ideas—such as the workings of karma, the dangers of attachment, or the subtleties of right conduct—to be embodied in memorable stories that can be recited, dramatized, and adapted for different audiences, including laypeople, youth, and courtly elites. At the same time, traditional classifications and commentarial discussions caution against narratives that encourage sensuality or violence, underscoring that the fictional form must remain aligned with the ethical and spiritual aims of Jain dharma.

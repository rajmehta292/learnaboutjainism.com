  "overview": "Svetambara Murtipujaka worship (mūrtipūjā) is the central liturgical and devotional pattern of the largest Śvetāmbara Jain sub‑tradition, often called Derāvāsī (“temple‑dweller”) or Mandir Mārgī (“follower of the temple path”). It is distinguished from Sthānakavāsī and Terāpanthī Śvetāmbara groups by its use of images (mūrti) of the Tīrthaṅkaras as focal points for veneration, meditation, and ethical reflection. In this tradition, the temple (derāsar, mandir) is understood not as a place where a deity grants worldly boons, but as a carefully ordered environment that supports right faith (samyag‑darśana), right knowledge (samyag‑jñāna), and right conduct (samyag‑cāritra) through disciplined ritual, scriptural recitation, and contemplative practice.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murtipujaka)\n\nAt the heart of daily worship is chaityavandana, a structured sequence of temple‑oriented prayers, circumambulations (pradakṣiṇā), prostrations (vandana), and meditative stillness (kāyotsarga). Performed in or facing a Jina temple, chaityavandana weaves together core canonical and post‑canonical sutras: the Navkār (Namokār) Mantra, praise of the twenty‑four Tīrthaṅkaras (such as Logassa), repentance texts like Iriyāvahiyam (Iriyāpathikī), forgiveness formulas (Khamāsamaṇa / Khamemi Savva‑Jīve), and resolve‑taking passages such as Karemi Bhante. The aim is not merely to “honor an idol,” but to bow to the Pañca‑Parameṣṭhī (Arihants, Siddhas, Ācāryas, Upādhyāyas, and Sādhus), confess faults, renew ethical vows, and cultivate universal friendliness (maitrī) and non‑violence (ahiṃsā).  [[1]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/6187/chaitya-vandan-vidhi-and-sutras) [[2]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/14048/chaityavandan-vidhi-details)\n\nTemple worship is framed by strict rules of purity and restraint. Devotees bathe, wear clean and simple clothing, and enter the shrine with awareness and humility. Physical actions—such as approaching the shrine, circumambulating the Jina image, and performing full‑body prostrations—are undertaken with care to minimize harm to living beings and to align body, speech, and mind with dharma. The image itself is not regarded as a sentient god but as a perfected, liberated being’s representation, serving as a visual support for contemplating the qualities of omniscience, non‑attachment, and compassion. In many communities, worship is ideally performed daily, often morning and evening, and is intensified on festivals like Paryuṣaṇa, Mahāvīra Jayantī, and temple‑specific observances.  [[2]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/14048/chaityavandan-vidhi-details) [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murtipujaka)\n\nA distinctive feature of Svetambara Murtipujaka practice is the elaborate ritual of installing images, known as pratishthā. Historical manuscripts such as Pratishthā‑kalpa (Anjanśalākā‑vidhi), drawing on earlier works like Nirvāṇa‑kalikā and Ācāra‑dinkara, describe complex procedures for consecrating Tīrthaṅkara mūrtis. Contemporary community education often distinguishes between “simple pratishthā,” which renders an image darśanīya (worthy of respectful viewing and prayer), and “prāṇa‑pratishthā” with Anjan‑śalākā, which makes the image pūjanīya (worthy of full ritual worship) and associates it with daily aṣṭaprakārī pūjā (worship with eight substances), ārtī, and maṅgal‑dīvo. These distinctions are rooted in later ritual developments and traditional belief rather than in the earliest canonical Āgamas, and modern teachers frequently emphasize that the ultimate focus remains the qualities of the Jina, not any literal “indwelling life” in stone or metal.  [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/anjan_shalaka_pran_pratistha_ceremony_shwetambar_sect_199998_199998)\n\nWithin the Svetambara Murtipujaka worldview, image‑worship is defended as a powerful aid to spiritual progress when practiced with right understanding. Critics such as the fifteenth‑century reformer Loṅkā Śāh argued, on the basis of scriptural study, that temple construction and mūrtipūjā risked subtle forms of violence and attachment, helping to inspire non‑image‑worshipping currents like Sthānakavāsī and Terāpanth. Murtipujaka authors and practitioners respond by stressing that the rituals are framed by repentance, forgiveness, and vows of restraint, and that the material offerings (flowers, lamps, rice, sandal paste) are symbolic supports for inner virtues rather than attempts to please a deity. The tradition thus situates its worship within the broader Jain commitment to ahiṃsā and aparigraha, while acknowledging historical debates and regional variations in practice.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Murtipujaka) [[1]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/6187/chaitya-vandan-vidhi-and-sutras)\n\nBeyond the core temple liturgy, Svetambara Murtipujaka worship includes rich visual and artistic traditions that extend the experience of darśana and pilgrimage. One notable example is the tirtha‑paṭa, large cartographic paintings of sacred sites such as Mount Śatruñjaya, regarded as incarnations (avatāra) of those places and used to facilitate emotional or mental pilgrimages (bhāva‑yātrā) for devotees unable to travel physically. Displayed in temples and homes, these images integrate monuments, landscapes, spiritual figures, and narrative scenes, reinforcing the community’s connection to tīrthas and the aspiration for liberation (mokṣa). Such art forms, together with temple icons, ritual implements, and architectural layouts, form a coherent devotional ecosystem in which seeing, bowing, reciting, and remembering are all oriented toward the same goal: the gradual purification of karma and the realization of the Jina’s path.

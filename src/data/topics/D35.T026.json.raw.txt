  "overview": "In Jainism, “canon differences” refers primarily to the divergent understandings of authoritative scripture between the two major traditions: Śvetāmbara and Digambara. Both trace their origins to the teachings of the Tīrthaṅkaras—especially Lord Mahāvīra—and both affirm that the earliest doctrinal corpus was transmitted orally by the Gaṇadharas (chief disciples) as the dvādaśāṅgī (twelvefold canon) and the fourteen Pūrvas. Yet, over centuries marked by famines, migrations, and declining memorization, each tradition developed its own account of how much of this early corpus survived, and which texts can still be regarded as canonical in a strict sense.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_schools_and_branches) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/history_of_canonical_literature_of_jainas_006541_006541)\n\nŚvetāmbara sources maintain that substantial portions of Mahāvīra’s teachings were preserved in Prakrit and later written down after a series of councils. The most decisive event in this narrative is the council at Vallabhi (in present-day Gujarat), traditionally dated around the 5th century CE and presided over by Devarddhi Kṣamāśramaṇa. There, surviving material was collected, systematized, and committed to writing as the Āgamas, usually counted as eleven Aṅgas plus associated Upāṅgas and other canonical categories. Śvetāmbara authors acknowledge that some texts—especially the fourteenth Pūrva and the twelfth Aṅga, Dṛṣṭivāda—were already lost, but they nonetheless affirm a positive canon of 45 (or 32, depending on classification) Āgama texts that continue to shape monastic discipline, lay ethics, and ritual life.  [[2]](https://www.hinduwebsite.com/jainism/jaincanon.asp) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/history_of_canonical_literature_of_jainas_006541_006541)\n\nDigambara authorities, by contrast, hold that the original canon in its strict sense did not survive. According to their account, the Pūrvas and Aṅgas were gradually lost, especially after the time of Bhadrabāhu, the last śrutakevalī (one who fully knew the scriptures). The twelve-year famine in Magadha and the subsequent migration of monks to the south are seen as pivotal moments: the disruption of the teaching lineages and the decline in memorization meant that no fully reliable textual record of Mahāvīra’s words remained. As a result, Digambaras do not recognize the Śvetāmbara Āgamas as canonical; instead, they treat later doctrinal works—such as those of Kundakunda, Samantabhadra, and others—as authoritative expositions of Jain truth, while still acknowledging that these are not identical with the lost original canon.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_schools_and_branches) [[2]](https://www.hinduwebsite.com/jainism/jaincanon.asp)\n\nThese differing canonical claims are embedded in broader sectarian histories. Śvetāmbara narratives emphasize councils at Pāṭaliputra, Mathurā, and Vallabhi, where monks attempted to stabilize and codify the tradition. Digambara narratives stress the impossibility of perfectly preserving the original teachings after the famine and the subsequent need to rely on doctrinal reconstruction rather than on a fixed scriptural corpus. Both traditions agree that the earliest scriptures were in Prakrit (especially Ardhamāgadhī), and both accept certain later works—most notably the Tattvārthasūtra—as common ground. Yet they disagree on whether any text can be said to be the direct, continuous canon from Mahāvīra’s time.  [[2]](https://www.hinduwebsite.com/jainism/jaincanon.asp) [[3]](https://www.sanatanadecode.com/jainism-svetambara-vs-digambara/)\n\nFrom a technical perspective, “canon differences” therefore involve several layers: (1) historical claims about loss and preservation of the dvādaśāṅgī and Pūrvas; (2) sectarian lists of canonical texts (Āgamas for Śvetāmbaras, Siddhānta and doctrinal treatises for Digambaras); (3) linguistic and regional variations in transmission (Prakrit vs. Sanskrit, north vs. south); and (4) differing hermeneutical attitudes toward later compositions and commentaries. Modern scholarship, drawing on manuscript history and philology, generally confirms that the Jain canon was shaped over many centuries and that no surviving text can be treated as a verbatim record of Mahāvīra’s sermons. At the same time, it recognizes that both Śvetāmbara and Digambara corpora preserve early layers of doctrine and practice, and that comparative study of these corpora is essential for understanding the evolution of Jain thought.  [[2]](https://www.hinduwebsite.com/jainism/jaincanon.asp) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/history_of_canonical_literature_of_jainas_006541_006541)\n\nFor students of Jainism, appreciating canon differences is not merely a matter of listing texts. It requires attention to how each tradition conceptualizes authority: Śvetāmbaras tend to speak of a closed canon of Āgamas, supplemented by commentaries and later works, whereas Digambaras emphasize the loss of the original canon and the centrality of doctrinal treatises and philosophical syntheses. Both, however, insist that true authority ultimately lies in right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct, and that scriptures—whatever their sectarian form—are valuable insofar as they guide beings toward liberation (mokṣa) rather than serve as ends in themselves.\n\nBecause these questions touch on lineage, monastic discipline, and ritual practice, any binding decision about which texts a particular community should treat as canonical belongs to qualified living teachers within that lineage. A lay or academic overview can clarify historical and doctrinal patterns, but it cannot replace the guidance of an ācārya or saṅgha when it comes to concrete practice and vows.",

  "overview": "In Jain tradition, Ayodhya (also called Vinita or Saketa in some texts) is not only a major North Indian city but a primordial sacred landscape woven into Jain cosmology and universal history. It is described as the royal capital of the Ikṣvāku dynasty, the lineage from which many of the Tirthankaras of the current time-cycle are said to have arisen, and thus functions as a paradigmatic city of dharma, kingship, and spiritual renunciation. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishabhanatha) [[1]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Rishabhanatha)\n\nMost Jain narrative sources—especially the Kalpa Sūtra and later universal histories such as Hemacandra’s \"Triṣaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacarita\"—identify Ayodhya as the birthplace of the first Tirthankara, Ṛṣabhanātha (Ādinātha). He is born there to King Nābhi and Queen Marudevī, inaugurates organized social life, and eventually renounces his universal monarchy to become an ascetic, making Ayodhya both the cradle of civilization and the starting point of the Jain path in this cosmic era. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishabhanatha) [[1]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Rishabhanatha) [[2]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Rishabhanatha) The city is portrayed as exquisitely planned, prosperous, and ethically ordered, symbolizing the ideal conditions under which right faith (samyagdarśana) can arise.\n\nAyodhya is also associated with other early Tirthankaras. Traditional lists state that Ajitanātha, the second Tirthankara, and Abhinandananātha, the fourth Tirthankara, are likewise born in Ayodhya to Ikṣvāku kings and queens, reinforcing the city’s status as a repeated locus of Tirthankara descent. [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Abhinandananatha) [[4]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Ajitanatha) In these narratives, Ayodhya is the stage on which royal life, ethical dilemmas, and the decisive turn toward renunciation unfold: princes and kings raised in its palaces eventually abandon power, illustrating the Jain teaching that even the most refined worldly pleasures are impermanent and ultimately unsatisfactory.\n\nFrom a sectarian perspective, both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions venerate Ayodhya as a tīrtha (pilgrimage site) connected to the early Tirthankaras, though details of chronology, dream-omens, and specific episodes can differ between textual lineages. Digambara universal histories often emphasize the immense cosmic timescales and the grandeur of Ayodhya’s royal courts, while Śvetāmbara sources such as the Kalpa Sūtra highlight auspicious dreams, ritual celebrations, and the city’s role in the narrative cycle recited during Paryuṣaṇa. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Rishabhanatha) [[1]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Rishabhanatha) Modern Jain communities continue to remember Ayodhya primarily through these scriptural associations rather than through contemporary political debates, seeing it as a reminder that spiritual transformation can begin even in the heart of worldly power.\n\nIn comparative religious discourse, Ayodhya is widely known as the birthplace of Rāma in Hindu tradition, and this has shaped modern public memory of the city. Jain texts, however, situate Ayodhya within a broader, multi-religious sacred geography in which the same city can host different narratives of dharma and liberation. [[2]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Rishabhanatha) For Jains, Ayodhya’s significance lies less in territorial claims and more in its role as a narrative and ethical archetype: a place where kings become ascetics, where social order is first taught, and where the possibility of crossing saṃsāra—the endless cycle of rebirth—was first demonstrated by Ṛṣabhanātha and other Tirthankaras.",

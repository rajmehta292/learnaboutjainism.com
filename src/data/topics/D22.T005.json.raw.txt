  "overview": "Bhaktamara Stotra (Sanskrit: भक्तामरस्तोत्र, bhaktāmara-stotra) is one of the most celebrated Jain devotional hymns, traditionally attributed to Acharya Manatunga and composed in classical Sanskrit, most likely in the Gupta or post‑Gupta period (around 6th–7th century CE). [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakt%C4%81mara_Stotra) [[1]](https://en.bharatpedia.org/wiki/Bhaktamara_Stotra) The title is commonly explained as deriving from “bhakta” (devotee) and “amara” (immortal or celestial), suggesting a hymn in which the devotion of the worshipper is so profound that even the gods are moved to reverence. [[2]](https://thejainreligion.in/bhaktamar-stotra-meaning) [[1]](https://en.bharatpedia.org/wiki/Bhaktamara_Stotra) In the Jain tradition, Bhaktamara occupies a central place among stotras (hymns of praise) and stavans (devotional songs), and is recited daily in many Digambara and Śvetāmbara communities.\n\nThe stotra is addressed to Lord Rishabhanatha (Adinatha), the first Tirthankara of the current time‑cycle, and extols his omniscience, compassion, purity, and liberating power. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakt%C4%81mara_Stotra) [[2]](https://thejainreligion.in/bhaktamar-stotra-meaning) The opening verses establish Rishabhanatha as the refuge for beings drowning in saṃsāra, whose feet are adorned by the jeweled crowns of celestial beings. The poet repeatedly emphasizes his own limited capacity to describe the infinite qualities of the Tirthankara, yet insists that sincere devotion itself grants the right to praise. This tension between the boundlessness of the Jina’s virtues and the humility of the devotee is a defining feature of the text’s theology and poetics.\n\nManuscript traditions differ regarding the total number of verses. Digambara lineages generally transmit 48 verses, while many Śvetāmbara manuscripts preserve 44 verses; some older references mention 52. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakt%C4%81mara_Stotra) [[3]](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/5361489) The last verse explicitly names Manatunga as the author, a common practice in Sanskrit stotra literature. Over centuries, Bhaktamara has inspired derivative compositions such as Kalyāṇamandira Stotra and Svayambhū Stotra, and has been richly illustrated in manuscripts, paintings, and temple panels—for example, the verse‑by‑verse panels at Sanghiji temple in Sanganer. [[3]](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/5361489) These artistic and liturgical developments show how the hymn became a key medium through which lay Jains encountered doctrinal ideas about omniscience, karma, and liberation in an accessible, emotionally resonant form.\n\nA famous legend, preserved in texts like Merutunga’s Prabandha‑cintāmaṇi and later community narratives, recounts that Acharya Manatunga was imprisoned and chained by a king (often identified with Bhoja). [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Bhakt%C4%81mara_Stotra) [[3]](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/5361489) [[1]](https://en.bharatpedia.org/wiki/Bhaktamara_Stotra) As he composed and recited each verse of Bhaktamara, one chain broke or one door opened, until he was finally freed upon completing the hymn. While this story is hagiographical rather than historical, it has profoundly shaped devotional reception: many Jains regard the stotra as possessing special protective and liberative efficacy, capable of removing obstacles, illness, and fear when recited with faith and right intention. In some traditions, each verse is associated with a specific yantra (mystical diagram) and particular ritual applications, integrating the hymn into broader mantra‑śāstra and tantric‑influenced practices. [[3]](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/5361489) [[2]](https://thejainreligion.in/bhaktamar-stotra-meaning)\n\nFrom a doctrinal perspective, Bhaktamara is not a systematic philosophical treatise but a poetic condensation of key Jain themes. It repeatedly highlights the Jina’s kevala‑jñāna (perfect knowledge), his complete freedom from passions and karmic bondage, and his role as a ford‑maker (tīrtha‑kara) who establishes the path across saṃsāra. The hymn also reflects the multi‑religious environment of its time: verses mention deities such as Śiva, Brahmā, and Buddha, acknowledging their prominence while subordinating them to the Jina’s superior status. [[3]](https://en-academic.com/dic.nsf/enwiki/5361489) This suggests composition in a period when Purāṇic Hindu and Buddhist cults were flourishing, yet Jain authors were articulating a distinct devotional language centered on the Tirthankara. Modern scholarship generally classifies Bhaktamara as a primary devotional text (Corpus Tier A for the Sanskrit base, with Tier B commentarial traditions and Tier C academic studies), and notes ongoing debates about its exact dating and the historical identity of Manatunga.\n\nIn contemporary Jain practice, Bhaktamara Stotra is recited in temples, homes, and educational settings across Digambara and Śvetāmbara communities. It is often taught to children as a foundational prayer, printed with translations and commentaries in Gujarati, Hindi, and other languages, and chanted collectively during special observances. [[2]](https://thejainreligion.in/bhaktamar-stotra-meaning) [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/bhaktamar_stotra_007113_007113) While devotional claims about miracles and protection are widespread, responsible teachers emphasize that the hymn’s true benefit lies in cultivating śraddhā (faith), bhakti (devotion), and reflection on the qualities of the Jina, which in turn support right view and ethical conduct. For questions of ritual prescription, health, or life‑decisions (such as fasting practices or vows), traditional Jain guidance insists that laypeople consult qualified monks, nuns, or learned householders rather than treating the stotra itself as a substitute for human counsel.",

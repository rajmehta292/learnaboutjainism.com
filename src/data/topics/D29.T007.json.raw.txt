  "overview": "Dharasena is a pivotal Digambara Jain acharya remembered primarily as the oral transmitter of portions of the early Jain canon that later became the basis of the Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama (Shatkhandāgama), the foundational scripture of the Digambara tradition. According to Digambara narrative, he lived several centuries after the nirvāṇa of Mahāvīra, at a time when the capacity of monks to retain the vast canonical corpus purely by memory was believed to be declining. Alarmed by the gradual loss of scriptural knowledge, Dharasena undertook the task of preserving at least part of the tradition by teaching selected sections of the canon to two capable disciples, Puṣpadanta and Bhūtabali.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satkhandagama) [[1]](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Religion%3AShatkhandagama)\n\nTradition locates Dharasena in the Candra-guphā (Moon Cave) on Mount Girnar in present-day Gujarat, where he is said to have practiced intense austerities and scriptural contemplation. From this retreat he summoned Puṣpadanta and Bhūtabali and orally taught them portions of the fifth Aṅga, Viahapannatti (Vyākhyā-prajñapti), and the twelfth Aṅga, Diṭṭhivāda (Dṛṣṭivāda). These teachings were then systematized and reduced to sūtra form by his disciples, resulting in the six-part composition later known as the Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama. In Digambara memory, Dharasena thus stands at the crucial junction between the largely oral transmission of the early canon and the emergence of written doctrinal compendia.  [[1]](https://handwiki.org/wiki/Religion%3AShatkhandagama) [[2]](https://jainworld.jainworld.com/JWHindi/Books/shatkhandagam/6INTRODUCTION%20TO%20SATKHANDAGAMA.pdf)\n\nChronological reconstruction is complex and not entirely uniform across sources. Some succession lists place Dharasena after Lohārya (Lohachārya), the twenty-eighth acharya after Mahāvīra, suggesting that he flourished roughly in the first century CE, while other traditional pattāvalīs (lineage lists) compress multiple acharyas into a shorter span and even associate Dharasena with a work called Joni-pāhuḍa around six hundred years after Mahāvīra’s nirvāṇa. Modern scholarship generally treats these dates as approximate, recognizing that they reflect sectarian memory rather than securely verifiable historical chronology.  [[2]](https://jainworld.jainworld.com/JWHindi/Books/shatkhandagam/6INTRODUCTION%20TO%20SATKHANDAGAMA.pdf) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/shatkhandagama_pustak_01_001395_001395)\n\nDoctrinally, Dharasena is associated with advanced knowledge of karma theory and subtle categories of the soul’s bondage and liberation. The portions of Viahapannatti and Diṭṭhivāda he transmitted—later embedded in the Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama and its extensive Dhavalā commentary—deal with the classification of karmas, the structure of the soul’s states (jīvasthāna), and the logic of spiritual progression. Although he is not portrayed as the author of these texts, his role as a transmitter and interpreter is central: without his initiative, Digambara tradition holds that these strands of the early canon might have disappeared entirely.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Satkhandagama) [[2]](https://jainworld.jainworld.com/JWHindi/Books/shatkhandagam/6INTRODUCTION%20TO%20SATKHANDAGAMA.pdf)\n\nIn the broader history of Jain literature, Dharasena’s importance lies less in personal biography and more in his function within the lineage of scriptural preservation. The later acharyas Virasena and Jinasena, who composed the Dhavalā, Mahādhavalā, and Jayadhavalā commentaries, explicitly acknowledge the antiquity and authority of the material stemming from Dharasena’s oral teaching. Thus, Dharasena forms a key link in a chain that runs from Mahāvīra’s original discourses, through the twelve Aṅgas, to the Digambara siddhānta (established doctrinal corpus) as it is known today.

  "overview": "Tattvārtha epistemology refers to the theory of knowledge articulated in the Tattvārthasūtra (also called Tattvārthādhigamasūtra), a foundational Jain philosophical text traditionally attributed to Umāsvāti/Umāsvāmi and accepted by both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions as a concise synthesis of Jain doctrine.\n\nWithin this text, epistemology is primarily treated in the first chapter, often titled “Right Faith and Knowledge” (samyagdarśanajñānācāra). Verse 1.6 states that knowledge of the fundamental categories (tattva)—such as soul (jīva), non-soul (ajīva), influx (āsrava), bondage (bandha), stoppage (saṃvara), shedding (nirjarā), and liberation (mokṣa)—is attained by means of pramāṇa and naya, that is, valid cognition and standpoints. Here, pramāṇa designates comprehensive, valid knowledge, while naya denotes partial, standpoint-based apprehension of reality. This dual emphasis reflects the Jain commitment to both the possibility of truth and the perspectival, many-sided character of its expression.  [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084589.html)\n\nThe Tattvārthasūtra defines pramāṇa in a characteristically Jain way. Verse 1.10 identifies the five kinds of knowledge—sensory (mati), scriptural (śruta), clairvoyant (avadhi), telepathic (manaḥparyāya), and omniscient (kevala)—as the two types of pramāṇa: direct (pratyakṣa) and indirect (parokṣa). Direct pramāṇa includes immediate, non-mediated forms of awareness (especially higher, supra-sensory knowledges), while indirect pramāṇa covers mediated cognition such as inference and scriptural testimony. The text explicitly rejects theories that treat mere contact between senses and objects, or the senses themselves, as pramāṇa, arguing that such accounts cannot explain knowledge of subtle, distant, or hidden entities, nor omniscience. Knowledge itself—when valid—is pramāṇa.  [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084593.html) [[2]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369638859_Development_of_Jaina_Pramanasastra_in_the_Commentaries_of_Tattvarthasutra/fulltext/642602cd92cfd54f843dcc25/369638859_Development_of_Jaina_Pramanasastra_in_the_Commentaries_of_Tattvarthasutra.pdf)\n\nNaya, introduced in 1.6 and elaborated in 1.33, is described as partial standpoints or aspects of pramāṇa. The Tattvārthasūtra lists seven naya: naigama (figurative), saṃgraha (generic), vyavahāra (practical or systematic), ṛjusūtra (straight or linear), śabda (verbal), samabhirūḍha (conventional), and evaṃbhūta (specific). Each naya captures one determinate aspect or mode of a substance without contradiction, and together they illustrate how any statement about reality is conditioned by a particular focus or intention. The text further distinguishes dravyārthika naya (standpoint focusing on substance and general attributes) from paryāyārthika naya (standpoint focusing on modes and particular states), thereby integrating epistemology with the Jain ontology of substances and their ever-changing modes.  [[3]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084616.html)\n\nTattvārtha epistemology thus operates on two interlocking levels. At the level of pramāṇa, it classifies and evaluates types of knowledge, insisting that valid cognition is possible and gradable—from ordinary sensory awareness up to kevalajñāna, the omniscience of the liberated soul. At the level of naya, it articulates how any finite knower’s statements are necessarily partial, conditioned by standpoint, language, and practical purpose. This framework underlies later Jain developments of anekāntavāda (the doctrine of non-one-sidedness) and syādvāda (the doctrine of conditioned predication), even though those terms are not systematized in the Tattvārthasūtra itself.\n\nCommentarial literature—such as Pūjyapāda’s Sarvārthasiddhi and many subsequent Digambara and Śvetāmbara expositions—expands the brief sūtras into a sophisticated pramāṇa-śāstra. These works analyze perception, inference, testimony, and other means of knowledge; debate rival Indian schools (Nyāya, Sāṃkhya, Mīmāṃsā, Buddhist traditions); and refine distinctions between svārthapramāṇa (knowledge for oneself) and parārthapramāṇa (knowledge for others, especially in the form of words). They also clarify that naya are subdivisions or aspects of pramāṇa, not independent means of knowledge, and that comprehensive pramāṇa is “sakalādeśa” (covering all aspects), whereas naya is “vikalādeśa” (partial and relative). In this way, Tattvārtha epistemology becomes a bridge between canonical classifications of knowledge and later Jain logical theory.  [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084589.html) [[2]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/369638859_Development_of_Jaina_Pramanasastra_in_the_Commentaries_of_Tattvarthasutra/fulltext/642602cd92cfd54f843dcc25/369638859_Development_of_Jaina_Pramanasastra_in_the_Commentaries_of_Tattvarthasutra.pdf)\n\nIn modern scholarship, Tattvārthasūtra is recognized as the earliest systematic Sanskrit presentation of Jain philosophy, with its epistemological sections serving as a cornerstone for the development of Jain logic and theory of knowledge. Researchers highlight three dimensions of this development: conceptual (defining pramāṇa and naya), analytical (classifying types of knowledge and standpoints), and logical (articulating inferential structures and criteria of validity). The text’s concise sūtras, together with their extensive commentaries, continue to inform contemporary Jain studies and comparative epistemology, especially discussions on perspectivism, pluralism, and the relationship between omniscience and ordinary cognition.

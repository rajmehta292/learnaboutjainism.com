  "overview": "In Jain philosophy, “Miśra” (Sanskrit: मिश्र, literally “mixed”) is the technical name of the third guṇasthāna, one of the fourteen graded stages of spiritual development through which a soul (jīva) progresses toward liberation (mokṣa). It designates a state in which right and wrong world-views coexist in a mixed, unstable form: the soul has some inclination toward true faith (samyak-darśana) yet remains significantly entangled in delusion (mithyātva). Because of this mixture, Miśra-guṇasthāna is considered part of the extroverted or outwardly oriented condition of the soul (bahirātmā), still far from the stable, introspective spiritual maturity of the higher stages.  [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/essay/traces-of-mysticism-in-jainism-study/d/doc1502257.html) [[1]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/gunasthana)\n\nWithin the canonical ladder of guṇasthānas, Miśra follows Mithyātva (complete wrong belief) and Sāsādana or Sāsvādana-samyak-dṛṣṭi (the “tasting” or fleeting experience of right belief). In Mithyātva-guṇasthāna, the soul is thoroughly deluded, unable to distinguish dharma from adharma, or a true guru from a false one. In the second stage, the soul briefly “tastes” right faith but cannot stabilize it. Miśra-guṇasthāna marks the next nuance: the soul now entertains both right and wrong attitudes simultaneously, neither fully committed to truth nor fully surrendered to falsity. This mixed condition is crucial in Jain metaphysics because it shows how karmic delusion (mohaniya-karma) can manifest in subtle gradations rather than a simple binary of belief and disbelief.  [[2]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc212556.html) [[3]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/gunasthana)\n\nFrom the standpoint of karma theory, Miśra-guṇasthāna is characterized by the continued dominance of deluding karma, especially those obscuring right faith, but with a partial weakening that allows glimpses of correct understanding. The soul may respect Jain teachings, tirthankaras, and ethical ideals, yet still harbor strong attachment to non-Jain or non-dharmic views, or vacillate between confidence and doubt. This ambivalence affects conduct: vows (vrata) and restraints (saṃyama) are not yet firmly adopted, and the soul remains vowless (avirata) in practice, even if it occasionally admires or contemplates the path of renunciation. The transition from Miśra to the fourth guṇasthāna, Avirata-samyag-dṛṣṭi (vowless right belief), involves the decisive stabilization of right faith and the abandonment of mixed attitudes, though full ethical discipline still lies ahead.  [[1]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/gunasthana) [[4]](https://grokipedia.com/page/gunasthana)\n\nTraditional Jain authors emphasize that Miśra-guṇasthāna is not a permanent identity but a dynamic karmic state that can be transcended or lost. Because karmic influx (āsrava) and bondage (bandha) continue strongly at this stage, regression to Mithyātva or fluctuation between the first three guṇasthānas is possible. Only when the soul firmly establishes right faith in the fourth stage does it begin the more stable ascent through partial and complete vows, subtle purification of passions (kaṣāya), and eventually omniscience (kevala-jñāna). In this sense, Miśra is a delicate threshold: it reveals the soul’s capacity to recognize truth while still being pulled by long-standing karmic habits and social conditioning. The careful analysis of Miśra in karma texts and guṇasthāna treatises illustrates the Jain commitment to mapping inner spiritual states with great precision, so that practitioners can understand the nuanced psychology of belief, doubt, and transformation on the path to liberation.

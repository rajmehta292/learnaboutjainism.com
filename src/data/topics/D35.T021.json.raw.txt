  "overview": "Modern Digambara lineages are the outcome of a long historical evolution in which older ascetic traditions, bhattaraka seats, regional communities, and reform movements have interacted from roughly the seventeenth century to the present. In contrast to the more widely studied Śvetāmbara gacchas, Digambara paramparās are often remembered through paṭṭāvalīs (genealogical lists) of ācāryas and bhattarakas, local temple histories, and community memory rather than a single centralized canonical record. [[0]](https://hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/journals/nidan/article/download/28632/28012/58298) [[1]](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Mula_Sangh)\n\nAt the structural level, most modern Digambara lineages trace themselves back to the Mūlasaṅgha, the ancient monastic “root community” that became the umbrella for several major gaṇas (troops or lineages), especially the Balātkāragaṇa and the Kāṣṭhāsaṅgha. These gaṇas developed complex networks of bhattaraka seats—settled monastic leaders who oversaw temples, scriptural copying, and ritual life—across Karnataka, Maharashtra, Rajasthan, Gujarat, and North India. Even where the original nude ascetic orders declined, these bhattaraka lineages preserved Digambara identity and continuity, and many present-day communities still recognize their authority or at least their historical role. [[0]](https://hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/journals/nidan/article/download/28632/28012/58298) [[2]](https://grokipedia.com/page/balatkara_gana)\n\nWithin this broader framework, modern Digambara practice is often organized around sub-sects that combine doctrinal emphases with distinct ritual styles. The three major sub-sects commonly listed in community education are Bīsapaṅtha, Terāpaṅtha (Digambara), and Tāraṇa-paṅtha (also called Samaiya-paṅtha), alongside smaller groups such as Gumanapaṅtha and Totapaṅtha. Bīsapaṅthis generally affirm the traditional bhattaraka system, maintain rich temple ritual including flowers, incense, and ārati, and are numerically dominant in Maharashtra, Karnataka, much of South India, and parts of Rajasthan and Gujarat. Terāpaṅthis emerged in North India in the late seventeenth century as a reform movement that criticized bhattaraka conduct and certain ritual practices; they emphasize more restrained worship, avoid many “sacitta” offerings, and historically reduced the role of bhattarakas in their religious life. [[3]](https://jainworld.com/library/jain-books/books-on-line/jainworld-books-in-indian-languages/antiquity-of-jainism/the-digambara-sub-sects/) [[1]](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Mula_Sangh)\n\nTāraṇa-svāmī-paṅtha, centered historically in Bundelkhand, represents an aniconic Digambara lineage that rejects image worship and focuses on the textual corpus of Tāraṇa Svāmī, whose compositions are treated as central objects of devotion and study. This lineage illustrates how Digambara identity can be maintained without the iconographic and ritual patterns typical of Bīsapaṅtha and Terāpaṅtha, while still affirming Digambara views on asceticism, scripture, and liberation. [[0]](https://hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/journals/nidan/article/download/28632/28012/58298) [[3]](https://jainworld.com/library/jain-books/books-on-line/jainworld-books-in-indian-languages/antiquity-of-jainism/the-digambara-sub-sects/)\n\nThe Balātkāragaṇa Mūlasaṅgha lineages, once spread across both South and North India, have undergone significant transformation in the modern period. Historical research shows that many seats remembered as bhattaraka successions were, for long stretches, actually headed by lower-ranking ācāryas or maṇḍalācāryas before gaining autonomous status. Political changes under the Sultanate and Mughal empires, shifts in lay patronage, and migrations of merchant communities repeatedly reshaped the geography of these lineages, leading to the rise and decline of centers in places such as Delhi, Jaipur, Idar, Surat, Karanja, and Latur. Today, only a few bhattaraka seats—most prominently at Humcha/Humbaj and other Karnataka centers—remain active, while many North Indian seats have lapsed, leaving their legacy primarily in inscriptions, paṭṭāvalīs, and temple traditions. [[0]](https://hasp.ub.uni-heidelberg.de/journals/nidan/article/download/28632/28012/58298) [[2]](https://grokipedia.com/page/balatkara_gana)\n\nModern Digambara lineages also intersect with broader questions of authority and reform. Some communities continue to look to living bhattarakas and ācāryas for guidance in matters of ritual, temple administration, and scriptural interpretation, while others emphasize lay-led organizations, scholarly study, and pan-Jain institutions. The diversity of practice—from fully image-centered worship to aniconic textual devotion, from strong bhattaraka leadership to decentralized lay initiatives—means that “Digambara” today refers not to a single uniform lineage but to a family of related traditions that share core doctrinal commitments (such as the ideal of nude asceticism, the authority of texts like the Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama, and specific views on liberation) while differing in institutional form and regional culture. [[3]](https://jainworld.com/library/jain-books/books-on-line/jainworld-books-in-indian-languages/antiquity-of-jainism/the-digambara-sub-sects/) [[1]](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Mula_Sangh)\n\nBecause much of the detailed lineage history is preserved in regional paṭṭāvalīs, inscriptions, and local chronicles, there remain gaps, disagreements, and uncertainties in reconstructing exact successions and dates. Modern scholarship has begun to address these issues by critically editing genealogical texts, comparing them with epigraphic evidence, and situating Digambara lineages within wider social and political contexts. For a practitioner or student, it is therefore important to distinguish between (1) primary doctrinal texts that articulate Digambara philosophy, (2) lineage records that legitimize particular seats or teachers, and (3) modern community narratives that may simplify or idealize complex historical developments. Consultation with qualified scholars and lineage-holding ācāryas is recommended when questions of formal affiliation, initiation, or temple governance arise.",

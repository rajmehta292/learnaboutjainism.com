  "overview": "Labdhisāra (also spelled Labdhisar or Labdhi Sāra) is a technical Digambara Jain treatise attributed to Ācārya Nemicandra Siddhānta Cakravartin, a major authority on karma theory and Jain mathematics around the early second millennium CE. It is closely connected with the broader karmic doctrinal corpus that includes texts like Gommatasāra, Trilokasāra, and Kṣapaṇasāra, and is often studied in tandem with them in advanced Digambara curricula. The work focuses on the subtle structures of karmic bondage, the gradations of spiritual capacity (labdhi), and the precise conditions under which a soul becomes fit for the first stage of upaśama-samyaktva (the initial subsiding-right-faith). [[0]](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Labdhis%C4%81ra_of_Nemicandra_Siddh%C4%81nta.html?id=B0DkAAAAMAAJ) [[1]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Labdhisar/001606)\n\nIn Jain technical usage, “labdhi” refers to specific attainments or capacities of the soul that arise from particular karmic configurations. Labdhisāra systematically analyzes these attainments in relation to the guṇasthānas (fourteen stages of spiritual development) and the complex taxonomy of karma—covering nature (prakṛti), duration (sthiti), intensity (anubhāga), and quantity (pradeśa). The text describes how, prior to the emergence of right faith, a mithyādṛṣṭi (deluded) soul passes through distinct labdhis that prepare it for the transition to upaśama-samyaktva, and how various karmic species are progressively reduced or transformed during the “prāyogya-labdhi” period. [[2]](https://nikkyjain.github.io/jainDataBase/shastra/03_%E0%A4%95%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%A3%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%A8%E0%A5%81%E0%A4%AF%E0%A5%8B%E0%A4%97/01_%E0%A4%B2%E0%A4%AC%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%A7%E0%A4%BF%E0%A4%B8%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%B0--%E0%A4%A8%E0%A5%87%E0%A4%AE%E0%A4%BF%E0%A4%9A%E0%A4%82%E0%A4%A6%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B0-%E0%A4%86%E0%A4%9A%E0%A4%BE%E0%A4%B0%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AF/html/010.html)\n\nLabdhisāra is notable for its highly symbolic and quasi-mathematical treatment of karma. Later scholarship has highlighted how Nemicandra uses numerical sequences, gradations of time (from palya to koṭi-koṭi-sāgara), and combinatorial patterns to express the diminishing of karmic duration and intensity. Modern studies, such as Laxmi Chandra Jain’s analysis, emphasize that the text encodes an “advanced symbolic and mathematical theory of karma and beyond,” where karmic processes are modeled through ratios, logarithmic-like reductions, and structured series that reflect the soul’s journey from heavy bondage toward increasing purification. [[0]](https://books.google.com/books/about/The_Labdhis%C4%81ra_of_Nemicandra_Siddh%C4%81nta.html?id=B0DkAAAAMAAJ)\n\nTraditionally, Labdhisāra is transmitted with important commentaries. Among these are the Samyagjñāna-candrikā of Paṇḍit Todarmal, Jeevatattva Dīpikā by Keśava Varṇi, and other regional scholastic glosses that unpack its dense Prakrit and Sanskrit terminology. These commentaries explain the five principal labdhis, the thirty-four kinds of “bandhāpasaraṇa” (reductions or cuttings-off of karmic bondage), and the specific karmic species bound in different destinies (human, animal, celestial, and hellish) as the soul approaches right faith. They also situate Labdhisāra within the larger Digambara doctrinal framework, showing how its analysis of labdhi complements Nemicandra’s other works on cosmology and karma. It does not function as a manual of ritual or lay ethics, but rather as a technical map of the inner transformations that must occur for a soul to become capable of genuine right faith and subsequent progress through the guṇasthānas. Because of its complexity, it is usually studied by advanced students, monks, and scholars, often with guidance from established commentarial traditions. Modern readers encounter it both through traditional editions with Prakrit-Sanskrit text and Hindi or regional-language translations, and through academic studies that explore its mathematical symbolism and its place in the intellectual history of Jainism.

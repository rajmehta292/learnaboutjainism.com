  "overview": "Chaityavandana (often spelled Chaitya‑Vandan or Chaityavandan) is a central daily devotional practice in the Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka Jain tradition, performed in the presence of a Jina temple (chaitya) or Jina image, or mentally when one cannot be physically present. It is not merely a sequence of recited formulas, but a structured discipline of inner purification, reverence, confession, and resolve to live with self‑restraint (samyam). Through bowing to the Pañca‑Parameṣṭhī—Arihants, Siddhas, Āchāryas, Upādhyāyas, and Sādhus—remembering the 24 Tīrthaṅkaras, and acknowledging one’s faults, the practitioner re‑orients their life toward the path of liberation.\n\nIn practice, Chaityavandana is closely linked with samayika (meditative equanimity) and pratikramana (ethical self‑review). A typical Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka sequence begins with śuddhi (purification: bathing, clean clothing, removal of leather items) and sitting or standing facing the Jina image. The practitioner recites the Navkār (Namaskāra) Mantra, performs kāyotsarga (standing or sitting motionless like a statue), and then proceeds through a series of canonical Prakrit sutras: Iriyāvahiyam (Iriyāpathikī) for repentance of harm done while moving; Tassa Uttari and related repentance formulas; Khamāsamaṇa/Khamemi for universal forgiveness; Karemi Bhante for resolving to observe non‑violence and other vows; Logassa for praise and remembrance of the 24 Tīrthaṅkaras; and specific Chaityavandana passages that salute all chaityas, Jina temples, and holy places. The ritual often concludes with auspicious verses and the phrase “Micchāmi Dukkaḍaṃ”—“May the evil of my actions be fruitless.”  [[0]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/6187/chaitya-vandan-vidhi-and-sutras) [[1]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/8461/chaitvandan-process-explained-in-english)\n\nThe bodily postures (mudrās) used in Chaityavandana are themselves part of the discipline. Yoga‑mudrā (hands joined near the chest), Muktasukti‑mudrā (hands cupped and raised near the forehead), and Jina‑mudrā (standing in kāyotsarga) are employed at different points to express humility, concentration, and detachment. The practitioner is encouraged to keep the eyes on the idol or its feet, the mouth softly reciting sutras, and the mind focused on three supports (ālambana): the image of Bhagavān, the words of the sutras, and their meanings. In this way, Chaityavandana becomes “internal worship” (bhāv‑pūjā), not just external ritual, cultivating reverence, repentance, and the aspiration to become like the Jina—utterly free from attachment and aversion.  [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/chaityavandan_vidhi_229251_229251) [[3]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/18185/how-to-do-chaityavandan)\n\nHistorically and doctrinally, Chaityavandana is embedded in a broader ritual and interpretive tradition. Texts such as “Chaityavandan Vidhi” and “Chaityavandanādi Bhāṣya Trayam” systematize the procedure, explaining the sequence of sutras, the meaning of each line, and the correct physical comportment. They divide temple worship into multiple aspects (sometimes described as “doors”), including mangalācaraṇa (benedictory verses), niśidhi and pradakṣiṇā (proper places and methods for circumambulation), prāṇāmika (forms of prostration), and various triads (pujā‑trika, upayoga‑trika, mudrā‑trika) that describe the devotee’s focus and mental state. These commentarial works aim to ensure that Chaityavandana is performed with understanding and right intention, not as mechanical recitation.  [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/chaityavandanadi_bhashya_trayam_004260_004260)\n\nAcross Jain traditions, the exact form and terminology of Chaityavandana can vary. Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka communities typically use a fixed set of Prakrit sutras and stotras in a recognizable order, often taught in childhood and reinforced through community education. Sthānakvāsī and Terāpanth Śvetāmbara groups, which emphasize non‑image worship, may retain many of the same core sutras (Navkār, Iriyāvahiyam, Khamemi, etc.) within pratikramana and samayika, but the explicit temple‑centered “Chaityavandana” format is less central. Digambara practice, while sharing the same foundational orientation of bowing to the Pañca‑Parameṣṭhī and confessing faults, tends to focus on different stotras and pūjā‑procedures, and does not always use the term “Chaityavandana” in the Śvetāmbara sense. Thus, “Chaityavandana” as a named daily temple‑veneration rite is most characteristic of Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka usage, though its underlying aims—reverence, repentance, and resolve—are widely shared.

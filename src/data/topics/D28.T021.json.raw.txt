  "overview": "Upasakadasa (Prakrit: Uvasagadasao; often rendered as Upasakdashang Sutra or Upasakdasha) is a central canonical text of the Svetambara Jain tradition and is traditionally counted as the seventh Anga (limb) of the Jain Agamas. It belongs to the Dharmakathanuyoga (narrative-exemplary) strand of the canon, using stories of lay followers to illustrate doctrinal points, ethical ideals, and the practical path of householders (śrāvakas) devoted to the Tirthankaras—especially Mahavira.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_07_ang_07_upashak_dashang_sutra_002733_002733) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/upasakdashang_sutra_004202_004202)\n\nThe core structure of Upasakadasa consists of ten narrative units, each focused on one exemplary lay disciple (upāsaka) of Mahavira. These ten are presented as paradigmatic householders whose lives demonstrate how laypeople can integrate the twelve vows of a śrāvaka (five anuvratas, three guṇavratas, and four śikṣāvratas), cultivate right faith (samyaktva), and progress spiritually while remaining engaged in worldly responsibilities. The text emphasizes that Mahavira had a very large lay following—tradition gives numbers in the hundreds of thousands—but selects ten figures whose conduct under extreme trials makes them models for future generations.  [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/upasakdashang_sutra_004202_004202) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/anandadidas_uvasagkathao_229271_229271)\n\nIn its present form, Upasakadasa is much shorter than the vast length attributed to it in traditional accounts. Some sources speak of an original text of more than eleven lakh (1.1 million) verses, with only a small fraction—around eight hundred verses—surviving due to historical calamities such as famines and the resulting loss of memorized material among monks. These traditional claims highlight both the perceived importance of the work and the awareness of textual loss within the Jain community. Modern critical editions and commentarial traditions work with the extant text, while acknowledging the legendary scale of the original.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_07_ang_07_upashak_dashang_sutra_002733_002733) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/aagam_07_upasak_dasha_moolam_evam_vrutti_004107_004107)\n\nEach of the ten chapters (adhyayanas) centers on a specific householder: Anand, Kamdev, Chulani-pitā (or Chulnipiya), Suradev, Chullashatak (Kshudrashatak), Kundakolik, Saddalputra, Mahashatak, Nandini-pitā, and Lantiy-pitā (Śalhi-pitā). Their stories share a common pattern: a prosperous and respected layperson encounters Mahavira, receives instruction, adopts the lay vows with carefully defined limits on wealth, consumption, and behavior, and then undergoes severe trials (upasargas) often instigated by deities or hostile forces. Through these ordeals—ranging from terrifying apparitions and threats to family members, to disease, financial ruin, and philosophical challenges—the upāsaka’s steadfastness, discernment, and commitment to nonviolence, truth, and restraint are tested and refined.  [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/upasakdashang_sutra_004202_004202) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/aagam_07_upasak_dasha_moolam_evam_vrutti_004107_004107) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/anandadidas_uvasagkathao_229271_229271)\n\nDoctrinally, Upasakadasa serves several functions. It concretizes the abstract framework of lay vows by showing how they are applied in specific situations, including the recognition and confession of atichāras (infractions) and the need for prāyaścitta (repentance). It illustrates the acquisition of limited clairvoyant knowledge (avadhi-jñāna) by lay followers, the karmic consequences of their actions, and their post-mortem destinies—often rebirth in the first heaven (devaloka) for vast spans of time, followed by eventual liberation in Mahāvideha Kṣetra. The text thereby links ethical discipline to the broader Jain cosmology and soteriology, making clear that lay practice, while distinct from monastic discipline, can be a genuine path toward spiritual advancement.  [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/upasakdashang_sutra_004202_004202) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/aagam_07_upasak_dasha_moolam_evam_vrutti_004107_004107)\n\nHistorically and philologically, Upasakadasa occupies an important place in the development of Jain narrative literature. It is closely related to later retellings and condensations such as Anandadidas Uvasagkathao, which summarize or reframe the ten lay stories for new audiences, and it has attracted extensive commentarial attention from Acharyas like Abhayadeva Suri. These commentaries unpack difficult Prakrit terms, explain doctrinal nuances, and situate the stories within broader scriptural classifications (Angas, Upangas, Mula-sutras, Cheda-sutras) and analytical frameworks (the four anuyogas). Modern scholarship and community education materials continue to draw on Upasakadasa when teaching lay ethics, the nature of faith and doubt, and the dynamics of testing and perseverance in the spiritual life.  [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/aagam_07_upasak_dasha_moolam_evam_vrutti_004107_004107) [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/upasakdashangasutram_009331_009331) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/anandadidas_uvasagkathao_229271_229271)\n\nFrom a practice-oriented perspective, Upasakadasa is not a legal code but a narrative guide. It does not prescribe detailed contemporary social regulations; instead, it offers paradigms of how householders can respond to suffering, temptation, and philosophical confusion without abandoning nonviolence, truthfulness, non-stealing, sexual restraint, and non-possessiveness. The text repeatedly shows that genuine lay discipleship involves both inner transformation—cultivating right faith and insight—and outer discipline—setting concrete limits on wealth, consumption, and harmful activities. For modern readers, the work invites careful interpretation in dialogue with qualified teachers, especially when considering how ancient ascetic ideals intersect with present-day health, family responsibilities, and social contexts.\n",

  "overview": "Pathshala adaptation refers to the ways in which traditional Jain religious schools for children and youth have been restructured, relocated, and reimagined in response to modern social, educational, and technological conditions. Historically, Jain learning took place in proximity to mendicant communities, temple complexes, and family spaces, with instruction centered on memorization of basic prayers, narratives of Tirthankaras, and practical rules of conduct for laypeople. In the late twentieth and early twenty-first centuries, migration, urbanization, and global diasporic life have prompted Jains to create new institutional forms—weekend pathshalas, temple-based classes, community-center programs, and fully digital platforms—to preserve identity while accommodating secular schooling and busy family schedules. [[0]](https://openaccess.wgtn.ac.nz/articles/thesis/Keeping_the_tradition_alive_An_analysis_of_contemporary_Jain_religious_education_for_children_in_India_and_North_America/17134841)\n\nIn India, both Digambara and Śvetāmbara communities have developed structured curricula for children that combine classical topics—Navatattva (nine fundamentals), karma theory, vows (vrata), and stories of exemplary laypeople—with contemporary themes such as environmental ethics, non-violent conflict resolution, and civic responsibility. These programs often operate as part-time schools, meeting weekly or during holidays, and are frequently organized and taught by lay volunteers, especially women, rather than by resident monks or nuns. This shift has subtly reallocated pedagogical authority within Jain society: mendicants remain doctrinal exemplars, but lay teachers and curriculum committees now mediate how children encounter scripture, ritual, and ethics in practice. [[0]](https://openaccess.wgtn.ac.nz/articles/thesis/Keeping_the_tradition_alive_An_analysis_of_contemporary_Jain_religious_education_for_children_in_India_and_North_America/17134841) [[1]](https://www.academia.edu/110683728/Keeping_the_tradition_alive_An_analysis_of_contemporary_Jain_religious_education_for_children_in_India_and_North_America)\n\nIn North America and other diaspora contexts, pathshala adaptation has taken on additional layers. Communities composed of Jains from multiple sectarian backgrounds—Digambara, Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka, Sthānakavāsī, Terāpanthī, and others—often design “pan-Jain” curricula that emphasize shared principles such as ahiṃsā (non-violence), anekāntavāda (doctrine of many-sidedness), and aparigraha (non-possessiveness), while downplaying or carefully balancing differences in ritual practice and doctrinal detail. English becomes the primary medium of instruction, with selective use of Hindi, Gujarati, or other languages, and complex philosophical topics are simplified to be accessible to children raised in multicultural environments. These adaptations help sustain Jain identity but also reshape how sectarian boundaries and classical metaphysics are understood by younger generations. [[0]](https://openaccess.wgtn.ac.nz/articles/thesis/Keeping_the_tradition_alive_An_analysis_of_contemporary_Jain_religious_education_for_children_in_India_and_North_America/17134841) [[1]](https://www.academia.edu/110683728/Keeping_the_tradition_alive_An_analysis_of_contemporary_Jain_religious_education_for_children_in_India_and_North_America)\n\nDigital initiatives represent a further stage of pathshala adaptation. Projects such as “Digital Jain Pathshala” and similar platforms offer video courses, interactive games, online quizzes, and downloadable textbooks that blend traditional narratives and doctrinal content with contemporary educational design. They aim to make Jain principles “easy to learn, relevant, and applicable in daily life,” using multimedia resources and remote access to reach children who may not live near a temple or organized community. While these platforms often consult sadhus, paṇḍits, and scholars for content, they also operate within the logic of digital media—short attention spans, entertainment-oriented aesthetics, and algorithmic distribution—which can encourage the simplification or universalization of complex doctrines such as karma, cosmology, and the stages of spiritual development (guṇasthāna). [[2]](https://digitaljainpathshala.org/about-us/) [[3]](https://digitaljainpathshala.org/) [[4]](https://vidyajournal.org/index.php/vidya/article/view/799)\n\nScholarly analysis of contemporary Jain religious education has highlighted both continuity and transformation. Studies of pathshalas in India and North America show that these schools play a crucial role in maintaining ritual familiarity (such as recitation of the Navkār Mantra, participation in pūjā, and observance of Paryuṣaṇa or Daśalakṣaṇa), while also introducing new forms of leadership and pedagogy. Laywomen, in particular, have emerged as key organizers and teachers, shaping curricula, selecting stories, and framing ethical lessons in relation to modern concerns like career choices, gender equality, and ecological responsibility. At the same time, the physical and social distance between children and mendicants has widened, and the authority to interpret texts and practices is increasingly shared among lay leaders, printed manuals, and digital content creators. [[0]](https://openaccess.wgtn.ac.nz/articles/thesis/Keeping_the_tradition_alive_An_analysis_of_contemporary_Jain_religious_education_for_children_in_India_and_North_America/17134841) [[1]](https://www.academia.edu/110683728/Keeping_the_tradition_alive_An_analysis_of_contemporary_Jain_religious_education_for_children_in_India_and_North_America)\n\nFrom a doctrinal perspective, pathshala adaptation raises important questions about how core Jain teachings are transmitted and contextualized. Classical texts emphasize the importance of right faith (samyag-darśana), right knowledge (samyag-jñāna), and right conduct (samyag-cāritra) as the three jewels of the path to liberation. Modern curricula often translate these into age-appropriate themes—developing trust in non-violence, learning basic categories of living beings (jīva) and non-living substances (ajīva), and practicing small vows such as truthfulness and dietary discipline. When done carefully, such adaptation can honor the spirit of the tradition while making it livable in contemporary settings. However, when doctrinal nuance is lost or ethical teachings are detached from their metaphysical framework, children may receive a more generic “values education” that is Jain-inspired but less anchored in the specific soteriological aims of the tradition. [[4]](https://vidyajournal.org/index.php/vidya/article/view/799) [[0]](https://openaccess.wgtn.ac.nz/articles/thesis/Keeping_the_tradition_alive_An_analysis_of_contemporary_Jain_religious_education_for_children_in_India_and_North_America/17134841)\n\nOverall, pathshala adaptation is best understood as a dynamic process rather than a single model. It involves negotiation between scriptural ideals and practical constraints, between sectarian particularity and pan-Jain unity, and between embodied ritual life and digital mediation. For families and communities, the challenge is to design educational spaces that remain faithful to verified primary texts and living lineages, while also responding to the realities of migration, secular schooling, and technological change. In this sense, pathshala adaptation is not merely a pedagogical innovation but a significant chapter in the ongoing history of Jain self-understanding and transmission.",

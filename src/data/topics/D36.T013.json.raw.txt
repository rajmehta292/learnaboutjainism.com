  "overview": "Jinabhadra, commonly referred to as Vācanācārya Jinabhadragaṇī Kṣamāśramaṇa, is a major Śvetāmbara Jain ascetic and commentator active in the late sixth and early seventh century CE, roughly 520–623 CE. He is remembered primarily for his Prakrit and Sanskrit exegetical works on the Jain canon, especially the Āvaśyaka literature, and for his role in the preservation and restoration of key scriptural texts. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinabhadra) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/bhashya_aur_bhashyakar_211598_211598)\n\nFrom an historical perspective, Jinabhadra appears in inscriptions and colophons as a leading figure of the Nirvṛttikula lineage, bearing titles such as “Vacanācārya” (teacher of scripture) and “Kṣamāśramaṇa” (ascetic devoted to forbearance). Inscriptions from Akkota (Ankotta) dating to approximately 550–600 CE mention “Jinabhadra Vacanācārya of the Nirvṛttikula,” linking him to image consecrations and suggesting his authority within a monastic community. [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/bhashya_aur_bhashyakar_211598_211598) [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinabhadra) He is also associated with the scholastic center of Vallabhi during the reign of the Maitraka king Śilāditya I (Śaka 531/609 CE), where manuscript evidence places copies of his Viśeṣāvaśyakabhāṣya. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinabhadra) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/bhashya_aur_bhashyakar_211598_211598)\n\nTextually, Jinabhadra is credited with restoring the Mahāniśītha, a canonical text said to have been damaged by termites in Mathurā, an episode that underscores his role in granthoddhāra—the recovery and stabilization of scripture. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinabhadra) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/bhashya_aur_bhashyakar_211598_211598) His corpus includes Prakrit works such as Bṛhatsaṅgrahiṇī, Bṛhatkṣetrasamāsa, Viśeṣaṇavatī, Viśeṣāvaśyaka, Dhyānaśataka, Jītakalpa and its bhāṣya, along with an unfinished Sanskrit commentary on the Viśeṣāvaśyaka. These texts focus on ritual obligations (āvaśyakas), monastic regulations, cosmological and geographical summaries, and meditative practice, and they became foundational for later Śvetāmbara scholasticism. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinabhadra) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/bhashya_aur_bhashyakar_211598_211598) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/visheshavashyaka_mahabhashya_sswopagnya_tikanu_astitva_230236_230236)\n\nWithin the Āvaśyaka tradition, Jinabhadra’s Viśeṣāvaśyakabhāṣya occupies a central place. It comments on the first adhyayana of the Āvaśyakasūtra and, in its extant form, comprises thousands of gāthās (verses). Later authors praise him as a “yugapradhāna” (leader of his age) and a uniquely skilled interpreter of the Agamas, emphasizing his mastery of both Jain doctrine and rival philosophical systems. [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/bhashya_aur_bhashyakar_211598_211598) [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinabhadra) Modern scholarship notes that his treatment of episodes such as the “Debate with the Gaṇadharas” (a narrative attached to the Āvaśyakasūtra) helped give that material a quasi-autonomous status within the tradition, shaping how Mahāvīra’s omniscience and pedagogical authority were understood. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jinabhadra) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/visheshavashyaka_mahabhashya_sswopagnya_tikanu_astitva_230236_230236)\n\nThere is some complexity in the transmission of his works. A long-standing belief held that his self-commentary (svopajña ṭīkā) on the Viśeṣāvaśyaka Mahābhāṣya had been lost, but later research in manuscript collections showed that the text survives, though incomplete. Jinabhadra is said to have composed the commentary up to the discourse of the sixth Gaṇadhara before his death; the remaining portion was completed by another teacher, Kottaryavādī Gaṇi Mahattar. [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/visheshavashyaka_mahabhashya_sswopagnya_tikanu_astitva_230236_230236) This layered authorship illustrates how later acharyas extended and stabilized his exegetical project while still acknowledging his primacy.\n\nIn community education and later historiography, Jinabhadra is sometimes conflated with other figures named Jinbhadrasūri, including a fifteenth-century Kharatargaccha leader renowned for founding numerous manuscript libraries. [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/anek_gyanbhandaro_ke_sansthapak_jinbhadrasuri_210042_210042) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/bhashya_aur_bhashyakar_211598_211598) Careful attention to dates, sectarian affiliation, and colophon titles is therefore essential: the sixth–seventh century Vacanācārya Jinabhadragaṇī Kṣamāśramaṇa belongs to the early Śvetāmbara exegetical milieu centered on Vallabhi and Mathurā, whereas the later Jinbhadrasūri is a medieval Kharatargaccha organizer of gyanbhandāras. Distinguishing these figures allows a clearer appreciation of Jinabhadra’s specific contribution: the systematic interpretation and preservation of the Āgamas at a formative moment in Śvetāmbara history.\n\nBecause surviving information about Jinabhadra’s personal life is sparse and mediated through inscriptions, colophons, and later commentaries, modern reconstructions must remain cautious. Dates, travel itineraries, and details of discipleship can vary across sources, and some attributions of texts may reflect later scholastic tendencies to cluster important works around a few celebrated names. Where manuscript evidence and early references converge—such as the Nirvṛttikula affiliation, the Vallabhi connection, and the authorship of Viśeṣāvaśyakabhāṣya and Jītakalpabhāṣya—his profile as a major commentator is secure, but finer biographical details are best treated as provisional and open to refinement by ongoing manuscript and epigraphic research.\n",

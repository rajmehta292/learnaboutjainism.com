  "overview": "Pramāṇa–naya integration is a central methodological insight of Jain epistemology and logic, expressing how comprehensive, valid knowledge (pramāṇa) and partial, standpoint-based cognition (naya) cooperate rather than compete. In classical Jain thought, pramāṇa designates those cognitions that grasp an object in its fullness and yield non-contradictory, reliable awareness, while naya refers to one-sided, context-bound approaches that highlight only certain aspects of the same object. The integration of these two—rather than the privileging of one over the other—embodies the Jain commitment to anekāntavāda (many-sidedness of reality) and is operationalized through nayavāda (the doctrine of standpoints) and syādvāda (conditioned predication). [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_epistemology) [[1]](https://www.academia.edu/167008575/Epistemological_Concept_of_Jainism_A_Comprehensive_Overview)\n\nFrom the canonical and early scholastic period onward, Jain authors insist that any claim to truth must be evaluated both as pramāṇa and as naya. Tattvārthasūtra, a foundational text accepted by both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions, states that right knowledge arises through pramāṇa and naya, indicating that valid cognition is not merely a matter of isolated certainty but of systematically coordinated perspectives. Later commentators such as Akalaṅka, Samantabhadra, Haribhadra, and Hemacandra elaborate this into a sophisticated theory: pramāṇa provides the criterion of validity (avisaṃvādi-jñāna), while nayas articulate the legitimate but limited ways in which reality can be approached—substance-wise, mode-wise, practical, nominal, and so on. Integration means that each naya is assessed within the horizon of pramāṇa, and pramāṇa itself is understood as the ordered totality of properly related nayas. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_epistemology) [[1]](https://www.academia.edu/167008575/Epistemological_Concept_of_Jainism_A_Comprehensive_Overview)\n\nTechnically, Jain philosophers distinguish between two broad kinds of pramāṇa—direct (pratyakṣa/aparokṣa) and indirect (parokṣa)—and then classify naya as a logical-epistemic tool that structures indirect knowledge and its expression. Mati-jñāna (sensory-cognitive knowledge) and śruta-jñāna (scriptural knowledge) are indirect and heavily dependent on standpoints; avadhi, manaḥparyāya, and kevala-jñāna are direct and progressively less standpoint-bound. Yet even scriptural and inferential pramāṇas are not rejected as merely relative; instead, their relativity is acknowledged and disciplined through explicit nayas, which prevent the absolutization of a single angle of vision. In this way, pramāṇa–naya integration guards against dogmatism (ekānta) while still affirming that there is determinate truth accessible to the soul as karmic obstructions are removed. [[2]](https://philosophy.institute/indian-philosophy/jain-epistemology-multifaceted-knowledge/) [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_epistemology)\n\nSyādvāda—the doctrine that any predication about a complex reality must be qualified by “in some respect” (syāt)—is often described in modern scholarship as the practical synthesis of anekāntavāda and nayavāda. Akalaṅka and later logicians show that each syād-vacana (qualified statement) implicitly relies on a specific naya and is justified as pramāṇa only when its standpoint and conditions are made explicit. Thus, pramāṇa–naya integration is not merely a conceptual pairing but a rule of reasoning: one must state from which standpoint a claim is made, recognize other legitimate standpoints, and then coordinate them to approximate the many-sided real. This coordination is never arbitrary; it is guided by the hierarchy of pramāṇas and by the ethical orientation of right faith (samyag-darśana), right knowledge (samyag-jñāna), and right conduct (samyag-cāritra). [[3]](https://link.springer.com/article/10.1007/s10781-024-09575-7) [[1]](https://www.academia.edu/167008575/Epistemological_Concept_of_Jainism_A_Comprehensive_Overview)\n\nHistorically, Digambara and Śvetāmbara authors agree on the basic structure of pramāṇa and naya but differ in details of classification and terminology. Some Digambara works emphasize the logical formalization of nayas and their role in debate, while Śvetāmbara scholastics often highlight their pedagogical and hermeneutical function in interpreting scripture. Modern interpreters sometimes read pramāṇa–naya integration as a form of radical relativism, but classical texts resist this: they maintain that while every finite cognition is standpoint-bound, the ordered system of pramāṇas and nayas aims at convergence on the same real, culminating in kevala-jñāna, which transcends all partiality. Where the sources are silent or ambiguous—for example, on the exact number and subtypes of nayas in early strata—later commentaries propose different schemes, and contemporary scholarship notes these disagreements rather than smoothing them out. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_epistemology) [[4]](https://philosophy.institute/religions-of-the-world/jainism-epistemological-perspective/)\n\nIn modern application, pramāṇa–naya integration has been invoked to support intellectual humility, interreligious dialogue, and pluralistic ethics. Community education materials often present it as a way to respect multiple viewpoints while still seeking well-grounded, responsible conclusions. This pedagogical use sometimes simplifies technical distinctions, and translations of key terms (such as rendering naya simply as “view” or “opinion”) can obscure their precise logical role. Careful study of primary texts and reviewed commentaries is therefore essential to avoid projecting contemporary relativist or absolutist frameworks onto Jain epistemology. For decisions involving health, law, or family obligations, classical Jain authors themselves defer to qualified human authorities and practical wisdom; pramāṇa–naya integration is a tool for understanding and dialogue, not a substitute for professional or lineage-based guidance.",

  "overview": "In Jain studies, a \"Jain manuscript\" refers not simply to any written text, but specifically to a hand-produced physical artefact that transmits Jain teachings, ritual manuals, narrative literature, scholastic treatises, and related knowledge across generations. It is a material witness to the Jain scriptural and intellectual tradition, created by scribes using established conventions of layout, script, colophons, and often ornamentation. Historically, these manuscripts were preserved in temple and monastic libraries (gyanbhandars or bhandars), where they functioned both as objects of study and, in many communities, as revered embodiments of \"jnana\" (knowledge) worthy of ritual respect.\n\nFrom roughly the first millennium onward, the dominant physical form of Jain manuscripts in western India was the palm‑leaf bundle, later joined and gradually superseded by paper manuscripts. A palm‑leaf manuscript is made from carefully treated leaves of specific palm species—especially the talipot palm (śrītāḍa) and palmyra palm (kharatāḍa)—which are cut into long, narrow strips, typically about 20–50 cm in length and 3–5 cm in width. These strips are dried, boiled, burnished (often with substances such as turmeric), trimmed, and then inscribed with a stylus or ink before being assembled into a bundle of dozens of leaves, pierced and strung together between protective wooden covers. [[0]](https://www.dsource.in/course/study-jain-manuscripts/process-making/surfaces/palm-leaf-manuscripts) [[1]](https://lis.academy/preservation-conservation-of-library-material/understanding-palm-leaf-manuscripts/) In Jain contexts, such bundles frequently contain canonical texts, commentaries, and ritual works, and their wooden covers (patli) may be richly painted with scenes from the lives of the Tirthankaras, teachers, and donors.\n\nThe term \"Jain manuscript\" therefore denotes a combination of content, material, and institutional setting. Content-wise, these manuscripts transmit a wide spectrum of Jain literature: Śvetāmbara canonical works such as the Kalpasūtra, narrative texts, philosophical treatises, grammatical and astrological works, and Digambara scriptures like the Śaṭkhaṇḍāgama and its associated texts. Materially, they may be palm‑leaf, paper, or occasionally other media cut to the palm‑leaf format; the long, narrow format itself became so standard that even birch‑bark sheets were trimmed to imitate it. [[1]](https://lis.academy/preservation-conservation-of-library-material/understanding-palm-leaf-manuscripts/) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/mudbidrina_jain_bhandarna_prachin_tadpatriya_chitro_230206_230206) Institutionally, Jain manuscripts are typically associated with temple or monastic repositories—gyanbhandars in places such as Patan, Jaisalmer, and Mudbidri—where they are catalogued, conserved, and sometimes ritually worshipped as part of practices like jñānapūjā (worship of the books of wisdom). [[3]](https://www.metmuseum.org/ru/essays/jain-manuscript-painting) [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jaisalmer_ke_mahattvapurna_gyanbhandar_210557_210557)\n\nHistorically, the production and preservation of Jain manuscripts were closely tied to patterns of patronage and community organization. Prosperous Jain merchant communities in Gujarat and Rajasthan commissioned large numbers of manuscripts, including illustrated copies of key texts, for temple libraries. Kings such as Kumarapala of the Caulukya (Solanki) dynasty are recorded as sponsoring the copying and distribution of canonical works like the Kalpasūtra and founding multiple monastic libraries in Patan. [[3]](https://www.metmuseum.org/ru/essays/jain-manuscript-painting) In later centuries, colophons show a shift toward lay middle‑class patrons—traders and bankers—who funded copying projects and sometimes added detailed notes about their towns, lineages, and devotional intentions. These colophons, preserved in repositories such as the Jinabhadrasūri Gyanbhandar at Jaisalmer and the Mudbidri Bhandar, are themselves crucial historical sources for understanding the geography, social networks, and intellectual life of Jain communities. [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/mudbidrina_jain_bhandarna_prachin_tadpatriya_chitro_230206_230206) [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jaisalmer_ke_mahattvapurna_gyanbhandar_210557_210557)\n\nFrom a technical perspective, scholars define a Jain manuscript by several features: the use of scripts such as Devanagari, Jaina Nāgarī, or regional variants; the presence of colophons specifying date, place, scribe, patron, and sometimes lineage; the adherence to established folio dimensions and ruling patterns; and, in many cases, the inclusion of miniature paintings or decorated margins. The manuscript is not merely a carrier of text but a crafted object whose material choices (palm‑leaf versus paper, pigments, binding style) reflect regional practices, available resources, and the intended ritual or scholastic use. Modern conservation and cataloguing efforts—by Jain monks, traditional scholars, and academic institutions—treat these manuscripts as tier‑A and tier‑B sources for reconstructing Jain textual history, while also acknowledging that many survive only in fragile, partially damaged form, requiring careful philological and material analysis.\n\nIn contemporary usage, \"Jain manuscript\" can refer both to the historical artefacts housed in gyanbhandars and museums, and to the broader tradition of hand‑written Jain textual transmission. However, it is important to distinguish these manuscripts from later printed editions and digital reproductions: while the latter disseminate the same texts, they lack the specific material, artistic, and ritual dimensions that define the manuscript tradition. For serious study of Jain literature, scholars often begin with manuscript evidence—collating multiple copies, weighing variant readings, and situating each manuscript within its local history—before producing critical editions and translations. In this way, the definition of a Jain manuscript is inseparable from its role as a primary witness to the evolving Jain canon, commentarial traditions, and lived religious practice.",

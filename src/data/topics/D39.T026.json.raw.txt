  "overview": "Ecological interdependence, in a Jain context, is most succinctly expressed by the aphorism “parasparopagraho jīvānām” – “all life is bound together by mutual support and interdependence.” This principle, drawn from the Tattvārtha Sūtra 5.21, states that the very function of jīvas (souls, living beings) is to assist one another. It is not merely a moral exhortation but a metaphysical description of reality: every living being, from one-sensed organisms to highly developed humans, exists within a web of mutual influence, dependence, and responsibility.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasparopagraho_Jivanam)\n\nFrom the standpoint of Jain ontology, the universe is populated by countless jīvas and by ajīva (non-sentient substances such as matter, space, time, motion, and rest). Ecological interdependence arises because jīvas and ajīva are constantly interacting: living beings rely on earth, water, fire, air, and vegetation for survival, while their actions in turn transform and sometimes harm these elements. Mahāvīra is remembered as teaching that one who neglects or disregards the existence of earth, air, fire, water, and vegetation disregards their own existence, which is entwined with them. This statement frames ecology not as a modern scientific discovery but as a spiritual insight into the shared destiny of all forms of life and environment.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasparopagraho_Jivanam) [[1]](https://infogalactic.com/info/Parasparopagraho_Jivanam)\n\nJain ecological interdependence is closely tied to ahiṃsā (non-violence) and anekāntavāda (the doctrine of many-sidedness). Ahiṃsā extends beyond human relations to include plants, microorganisms, animals, and the elemental life of earth, water, fire, and air. Because every being has its own perspective and intrinsic worth, anekāntavāda encourages humility in judgment and restraint in action. Ecological harm—pollution, habitat destruction, overconsumption—is understood as a form of violence that disrupts the delicate balance of mutual support among beings. Thus, ecological ethics in Jain thought emerge naturally from metaphysics: to injure another being is to injure the network that sustains oneself.\n\nModern interpreters of Jainism have highlighted how parasparopagraho jīvānām anticipates the scientific notion of ecology. Ecology, defined as the study of relationships between organisms and their environment, finds a conceptual parallel in the Jain view that all aspects of nature belong together in both physical and metaphysical relationships. Life is seen as a “gift of togetherness, accommodation and assistance” in a universe filled with interdependent constituents.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parasparopagraho_Jivanam) [[2]](https://jainworld.com/library/jain-books/books-on-line/jainworld-books-in-indian-languages/jainism-a-religion-of-asceticism-new/ecology-and-spirituality-in-jain-tradition/) This perspective encourages a holistic sense of “home” (oikos) that includes not only human society but also animals, plants, and the elemental environment.\n\nIn practical terms, ecological interdependence informs Jain disciplines such as dietary restraint, careful use of resources, and attention to the unseen lives affected by everyday actions. Traditional teachings urge practitioners not to harm, command, own, or exploit other creatures, emphasizing that each life form has inherent dignity.  [[2]](https://jainworld.com/library/jain-books/books-on-line/jainworld-books-in-indian-languages/jainism-a-religion-of-asceticism-new/ecology-and-spirituality-in-jain-tradition/) Lay vows and monastic rules—such as minimizing travel, avoiding unnecessary destruction of plant life, and filtering water—are concrete expressions of awareness that even small actions ripple through the web of life. While these practices arose in pre-industrial contexts, contemporary Jain communities and scholars have extended them to issues like climate change, biodiversity loss, and sustainable living.\n\nEcological interdependence also has a social dimension. Jain thinkers such as Samantabhadra and Jinasena have articulated visions of sarvodayavāda (the uplift of all beings) and social equality grounded in interconnectedness.  [[2]](https://jainworld.com/library/jain-books/books-on-line/jainworld-books-in-indian-languages/jainism-a-religion-of-asceticism-new/ecology-and-spirituality-in-jain-tradition/) Seeing other people as connected with oneself fosters compassion across caste, class, religion, and nationality, and this same logic applies to non-human beings and ecosystems. Religion, in this view, is tasked with removing bitterness and violence between all forms of life, making ecological harmony a spiritual as well as ethical goal.\n\nAt the same time, Jain sources acknowledge the complexity and tension inherent in living within an interdependent world. Complete non-harm is impossible; even breathing and walking affect microscopic life. Ecological interdependence therefore calls for vigilant awareness, minimization of harm, and honest recognition of karmic consequences rather than perfectionism. Interpretations differ among sects and teachers regarding how far traditional rules should be extended to modern technologies and global environmental crises, and contemporary applications often require dialogue with scientific ecology and environmental ethics. Nonetheless, the core insight remains stable: one’s own well-being cannot be separated from the well-being of the wider community of life, and spiritual progress is inseparable from ecological responsibility.",

  "overview": "The Tamil script (தமிழ் அரிச்சுவடி, Tamiḻ ariccuvaṭi) is an abugida belonging to the Brahmic family of writing systems and is used primarily to write the Tamil language, one of the major Dravidian languages of South Asia. Today it is employed by Tamil speakers in India, Sri Lanka, Malaysia, Singapore and diasporic communities worldwide, and it also serves as the script for several minority languages such as Saurashtra, Badaga, Irula and Paniya. Structurally, Tamil script is written from left to right and encodes syllables through consonant signs modified by vowel diacritics, a characteristic feature of Brahmic abugidas. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_script)\n\nHistorically, the Tamil script developed from Tamil-Brahmi (also called Tamili or Damili), a regional variant of the Brahmi script used in southern India between roughly the 3rd century BCE and the 1st century CE. Tamil-Brahmi inscriptions—found on cave beds, potsherds, coins, seals and rings—constitute the earliest known written records of Old Tamil and appear across present-day Tamil Nadu, Kerala, Karnataka, Andhra Pradesh and Sri Lanka. These inscriptions are crucial not only for the history of the script but also for reconstructing early social and religious life in the Tamil country, including the presence of Jain communities whose rock-cut caves and stone beds often bear Tamil-Brahmi legends. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil-Brahmi) [[2]](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-81-322-3756-3_2.pdf)\n\nFrom Tamil-Brahmi, two major graphic traditions emerged: Vaṭṭeḻuttu (\"rounded letters\") and the Pallava script. Vaṭṭeḻuttu, with its characteristically curved forms adapted to writing on palm leaves, was widely used in the Tamil region and parts of Kerala for several centuries. The Pallava script, another descendant of Brahmi, became influential across Southeast Asia and also contributed to the evolution of Grantha, a script used in Tamil Nadu to write Sanskrit. Over time, the interaction between Tamil, Vaṭṭeḻuttu, Grantha and related scripts produced a complex epigraphic landscape in which different scripts coexisted and were chosen according to language, genre and patronage. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_script) [[3]](https://dspace.cuni.cz/bitstream/handle/20.500.11956/188372/120468247.pdf?sequence=3)\n\nThe modern Tamil script, standardized by around the 5th century CE and further regularized in subsequent centuries, consists of independent vowel letters, consonant letters, and a special character called āytam (ஃ). Consonant signs inherently carry the vowel /a/, and other vowels are indicated by diacritics placed before, after, above or below the consonant. Unlike some other Brahmic scripts, Tamil does not employ extensive conjunct consonant clusters; instead, it typically represents consonant sequences with discrete signs, sometimes supplemented by the puḷḷi (virama-like dot) to suppress the inherent vowel. This relative simplicity makes Tamil orthography distinct within the broader Indic family, even while it shares a common historical origin in Brahmi. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_script) [[3]](https://dspace.cuni.cz/bitstream/handle/20.500.11956/188372/120468247.pdf?sequence=3)\n\nEpigraphic and palaeographic studies trace the evolution of Tamil letterforms through stone inscriptions, copper plates, palm-leaf manuscripts and, later, printed books. Scholars have documented gradual changes in stroke order, curvature, and ligaturing, as well as regional variants that reflect local scribal traditions. The transition from manuscript to print in the 19th and 20th centuries prompted script reforms, including efforts to simplify complex ligatures, standardize glyph shapes, and adapt Tamil to typewriter and digital encoding constraints. Modern Unicode standards now provide dedicated blocks for Tamil and Tamil Supplement, enabling the script to function across digital platforms while preserving its classical repertoire. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil_script) [[3]](https://dspace.cuni.cz/bitstream/handle/20.500.11956/188372/120468247.pdf?sequence=3) [[4]](https://www.academia.edu/169067266/The_Ceramic_Canvas_Tracing_the_Epigraphic_and_Sociolinguistic_Evolution_of_the_Tamil_Script)\n\nIn the context of Jain studies, Tamil script and its precursors are significant because many early Jain inscriptions in the Tamil country are written in Tamil-Brahmi, and later Jain literary works in Tamil employ the fully developed Tamil script. Rock-cut caves with stone beds at sites such as Arittapatti, Tirupparankunram, Kilavalavu and Sittannavasal bear Tamil-Brahmi inscriptions that mention Jain ascetics and donors, providing material evidence for the spread of Jainism in the region. These inscriptions, along with classical Tamil texts that show Jain influence, illustrate how the development of writing systems is intertwined with religious, philosophical and literary movements. [[2]](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-81-322-3756-3_2.pdf) [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tamil-Brahmi)\n\nToday, Tamil script continues to evolve in response to technological and sociolinguistic pressures. Digital typography, mobile keyboards and global communication have encouraged new conventions for transliteration, font design and orthographic normalization. At the same time, epigraphic research and manuscript preservation projects seek to document and conserve earlier stages of the script, including Tamil-Brahmi and Vaṭṭeḻuttu, as part of a broader effort to understand the historical depth of Tamil literacy and its connections to Jain, Buddhist and Hindu traditions in South India.",

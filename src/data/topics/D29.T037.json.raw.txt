  "overview": "Kundakunda (often styled Bhagavān Kundakundācārya in Digambara tradition) is one of the most influential Jain monks and philosophers of the Digambara sect. His name is attached to a corpus of Prakrit verse texts that became foundational for later Digambara self-understanding, especially in matters of the nature of the soul (jīva), karmic bondage, right faith (samyagdarśana), and the distinction between ultimate and conventional standpoints of truth.\n\nTraditional Digambara accounts place Kundakunda very early in the Common Era, often in the 2nd–3rd century CE, and connect him with southern India—regions such as the Tamil country, Ponnur Hills in present-day Tamil Nadu, and Kundadri in Karnataka. In these narratives he appears as a towering ascetic authority, a restorer of Mahāvīra’s teaching, and a spiritual guide whose name is recited immediately after Mahāvīra and Gautama in certain liturgical verses. [[0]](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Kundkund) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/bhagavana_kundakundacharya_010161_010161) Modern scholarship, however, is more cautious. Because his texts show doctrinal and linguistic features that fit a somewhat later milieu, and because explicit references to “Kundakunda” as an author appear only in later commentaries (notably Jayasena’s commentary on Pañcāstikāya), many researchers place the core layer of his works roughly between the 4th and 8th centuries CE, with later interpolations extending even further. [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kundakunda) [[3]](https://archania.org/p/individuals/philosophers/kundakunda)\n\nThe figure “Kundakunda” is therefore best approached as both a revered monastic authority in Digambara memory and a historically complex authorial construct. The texts attributed to him—such as Samayasāra, Pravacanasāra, Niyamasāra, Pañcāstikāyasāra, and Aṣṭapāhuda—were anonymous or fluidly transmitted for centuries before being firmly tied to his name. [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kundakunda) [[4]](https://www.britannica.com/biography/Kundakunda) Critical study of manuscripts suggests multiple layers of composition and redaction, with some sections likely authored by different hands within the broader Digambara scholastic world. Yet, taken together, these works articulate a distinctive vision of Jain doctrine centered on the “pure self” (śuddhātman) and the transformative insight that realizes it.\n\nPhilosophically, Kundakunda is renowned for his systematic use of two standpoints (naya): vyavahāra-naya, the conventional or mundane perspective, and niścaya-naya, the ultimate perspective. From the conventional standpoint, the soul is described in terms of its empirical states, karmic bondage, ethical discipline, and worldly relations. From the ultimate standpoint, the soul is pure, immutable, and intrinsically endowed with infinite knowledge, perception, bliss, and energy. [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kundakunda) [[0]](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Kundkund) Liberation (mokṣa) is understood as the realization of this ultimate standpoint, accompanied by the cessation of karmic influx and the destruction of existing karmic bonds. This two-perspective framework became central to later Digambara metaphysics and ethics, influencing authors such as Amṛtacandra and Akalaṅka.\n\nKundakunda’s works also engage, implicitly and explicitly, with broader Indian philosophical currents. Scholars have noted resonances with Sāṃkhya, certain strands of Mahāyāna Buddhism, and Advaita Vedānta, especially in the sharp distinction between the pure self and the changing world of matter and karma. [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kundakunda) At the same time, his texts remain firmly rooted in Jain categories: the seven or nine tattvas (fundamental realities), the vows (vrata) and restraints (saṃyama), the gradations of spiritual development (guṇasthāna), and the centrality of nonviolence (ahiṃsā). In Digambara practice, recitation and study of Kundakunda’s texts are regarded as powerful aids to right faith and inner purification.\n\nBecause of the combination of doctrinal depth, poetic compression, and complex transmission history, Kundakunda occupies a unique place in Jain intellectual history. For the Digambara community, he stands as a paradigmatic ācārya whose teachings illuminate the path of the soul from bondage to liberation. For historians and philologists, he represents a layered textual tradition that must be carefully reconstructed from manuscripts, commentaries, and sectarian memory. Any serious engagement with Digambara philosophy, especially on the nature of the self and the logic of multiple viewpoints, inevitably passes through the corpus attributed to Kundakunda.",

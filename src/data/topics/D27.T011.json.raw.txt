  "overview": "In Jain traditions, Sarasvati (also spelled Saraswati or Sarasvatī) is revered primarily as Shrutadevī or Shrutadevatā—the divine embodiment of sacred knowledge and scriptural learning. While her earliest attested form in South Asian religion is the Vedic river-goddess Sarasvatī, Jain communities gradually adopted and reinterpreted her as a goddess of speech, learning, and eloquence closely tied to the preservation and right understanding of Jain doctrine. In this role she is associated with the Jain canon (śruta), the limbs of scripture (aṅga), and the ancient, now-lost Purvas, and is invoked as a power that removes ignorance and karmic obstacles to samyagdarśana (right faith) and samyagjñāna (right knowledge). [[0]](https://devdutt.com/jain-saraswati-on-goose-or-peacock/) [[1]](https://www.anantaajournal.com/archives/2023/vol9issue5/PartA/9-5-5-336.pdf)\n\nWithin Jain cosmology, Sarasvati is counted among the higher, \"pure\" goddesses of the upper realms (ūrdhvaloka), alongside Lakṣmī. She is distinct from the more ambivalent yakṣīs and protective vidyādevīs of the middle and lower realms, and is characterized as vegetarian, auspicious, and oriented toward knowledge rather than worldly power. Medieval narrative and scholastic traditions describe Jain ācāryas and paṇḍits receiving her grace in the form of inspiration, clarity of speech, and success in debate. Stories about figures such as Bappabhatti Sūri and Hemacandra, who are said to have invoked or been blessed by Sarasvati, illustrate how intellectual achievement and scriptural mastery were imagined as requiring both rigorous study and divine support. [[0]](https://devdutt.com/jain-saraswati-on-goose-or-peacock/) [[2]](https://brill.com/previewpdf/view/journals/nu/34/2/article-p235_4.xml)\n\nIconographically, Jain Sarasvati shares features with Hindu and Buddhist forms but develops distinctive traits in Jain art. The earliest securely identified Jain image of Sarasvati comes from Kankali Tila at Mathura (Kushan period, ca. 2nd century CE), showing her seated with a manuscript and rosary—already marking her as a personification of learning. Later images from sites such as Deogarh, Khajuraho, Humcha, Halebid, Jinanathapura, and Western Indian temple complexes (Osian, Kumbhariya, Mount Abu, Taranga) depict her with four or more arms, holding combinations of manuscript, rosary (akṣamālā), lotus, water pot (kamaṇḍalu), and sometimes a vīṇā. She may be seated in padmāsana or lalitāsana, or standing in graceful tribhaṅga, often near or beside the Jina images, visually signaling her proximity to omniscient wisdom while remaining subordinate to the Jinas themselves. [[0]](https://devdutt.com/jain-saraswati-on-goose-or-peacock/) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jain_pratimao_me_sarasvati_chakreshwari_padmavati_aur_ambika_210818_210818) [[1]](https://www.anantaajournal.com/archives/2023/vol9issue5/PartA/9-5-5-336.pdf)\n\nJain iconographic manuals and later descriptive texts emphasize her fair complexion, white garments, and association with purity and clarity of mind. In some Śvetāmbara descriptions she rides a cow and holds a conch, rosary, bow, and arrow; in other regional traditions she is shown with a vīṇā and kamaṇḍalu, or as Śāradā, a multi-faced, multi-armed form presiding over the sixty-four arts. These variations reflect both intra-Jain sectarian diversity and the broader circulation of Sarasvati imagery across Hindu, Buddhist, and Jain communities. Yet in Jain usage, the central emphasis remains on her role as Shrutadevī, patron of scriptural study, grammar, poetics, and philosophical discourse. [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jain_pratimao_me_sarasvati_chakreshwari_padmavati_aur_ambika_210818_210818) [[1]](https://www.anantaajournal.com/archives/2023/vol9issue5/PartA/9-5-5-336.pdf) [[4]](https://books.google.com/books/about/Sarasvat%C4%AB_Riverine_Goddess_of_Knowledge.html?id=4lsYKIXBOK0C)\n\nRitually, Sarasvati worship among Jains becomes prominent in the medieval period, paralleling the rise of goddess cults and bhakti devotionalism in wider Indian religious history. Both lay and monastic communities participate in her veneration, especially in connection with learning, examinations, and the copying or recitation of texts. Digambara communities in particular observe Śruta Pañcamī, a festival in the hot season that commemorates the preservation of scriptural knowledge after the loss of the original canon; Sarasvati is honored as the guardian of this fragile transmission. In some temples, elaborate yantras, mantras, and tantric-style rituals are associated with her more powerful forms, indicating the integration of esoteric practices into Jain goddess traditions while still framing her as a beneficent, knowledge-oriented deity. [[0]](https://devdutt.com/jain-saraswati-on-goose-or-peacock/) [[2]](https://brill.com/previewpdf/view/journals/nu/34/2/article-p235_4.xml)\n\nFrom a comparative perspective, Sarasvati’s Jain forms cannot be fully understood without reference to her Vedic and Purāṇic background and her Buddhist transformations. Vedic hymns praise her as the best of rivers and goddesses, a source of inspired speech; later Hindu Purāṇas make her the consort or daughter of Brahmā and expand her domain to music and fine arts. Buddhist texts and images further develop Sarasvatī as a defender of the Dharma, sometimes weapon-bearing and multi-armed. Jain traditions selectively adopt and reinterpret these motifs, emphasizing manuscript, rosary, and vīṇā as symbols of scriptural learning and eloquence, while downplaying martial aspects. The result is a Jain Sarasvati who is recognizably part of the wider South Asian goddess landscape yet firmly integrated into Jain cosmology, ethics, and the ideal of right knowledge leading toward liberation.",

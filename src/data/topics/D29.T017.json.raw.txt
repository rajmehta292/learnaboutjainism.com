  "overview": "Trilokasara (त्रिलोकसारः, often spelled Triloksar or Triloksara) is a foundational Digambara Jain cosmological treatise composed by Acharya Nemichandra Siddhantachakravarti, generally dated to around the 9th–10th century CE. It belongs to the Karnānuyoga (the doctrinal-technical corpus) and presents a systematic exposition of the structure, dimensions, and ontology of the Jain universe (loka). The title literally means “Essence of the Three Worlds,” referring to the lower, middle, and upper realms that together constitute the Jain cosmos.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/triloksar_007269_007269) [[1]](https://insightjainism.vercel.app/docs/Scriptures/Triloksar/part1)\n\nThe work is originally written in Prakrit and consists of approximately 1018 gāthās (verses), traditionally divided into six major sections (adhikāras). These are: Loksāmānya-adhikāra (general description of the universe and hells), Bhavan-adhikāra (mansional heavens), Vyantar-loka-adhikāra (vyantara deities), Jyotiṣ-loka-adhikāra (luminary heavens), Vaimānika-adhikāra (higher celestial realms), and Naṛtiryag-loka-adhikāra (human and animal realms). Together, these chapters guide the reader from a bird’s-eye view of the entire universe down to detailed classifications of specific regions and beings.  [[1]](https://insightjainism.vercel.app/docs/Scriptures/Triloksar/part1)\n\nA central doctrinal emphasis of Trilokasara is that the universe is beginningless, uncreated, and self-existent. It rejects the notion of a creator deity and instead describes loka as an eternal domain containing sentient souls (jīva) and non-sentient substances (ajīva), structured according to precise spatial and quantitative measures. The text uses technical units such as rajju and elaborate numerical analogies (for example, counting mustard seeds) to convey the vastness of cosmic dimensions. These measurements are not merely speculative; they function as a framework for understanding karmic migration, the distribution of life forms, and the conditions under which liberation (mokṣa) is possible.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/triloksar_007269_007269) [[2]](https://manuscripts.vitragelibrary.org/collection-details/trilok-saar)\n\nIn its description of the three worlds, Trilokasara details the lower realms (adholoka), including various hells (naraka) with gradations of suffering; the middle world (madhyaloka), where humans, animals, plants, and many mobile beings reside; and the upper world (ūrdhvaloka), populated by multiple classes of celestial beings (devas) inhabiting distinct heavens. Each realm is characterized by specific karmic conditions, lifespans, bodies, and spiritual capacities. The text thereby provides a cosmographic map that supports ethical reflection: understanding where one’s actions may lead in future births is meant to encourage restraint, nonviolence, and pursuit of right faith, knowledge, and conduct.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/triloksar_007269_007269) [[1]](https://insightjainism.vercel.app/docs/Scriptures/Triloksar/part1)\n\nTrilokasara stands in dialogue with earlier cosmological works such as Tiloyapaṇṇatti, drawing on and systematizing their insights while presenting them in a concise verse form suitable for memorization and commentary. Over time, it has attracted important traditional exegesis, including Sanskrit commentary by Madhavachandra Traividyadeva and vernacular commentary by Pandit Todarmal in the Dhundhari dialect, as well as later Hindi expositions. These commentaries help unpack dense technical terminology, reconcile numerical schemes, and connect cosmology with broader Jain doctrine on karma, gunasthāna (spiritual stages), and liberation.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/triloksar_007269_007269) [[1]](https://insightjainism.vercel.app/docs/Scriptures/Triloksar/part1) [[3]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Triloksar/090512)\n\nWithin Digambara tradition, Trilokasara is often studied alongside Nemichandra’s other works (such as Gommatasāra) as part of an advanced curriculum in doctrinal and cosmological studies. Its influence extends into temple discourse, lay study circles, and modern educational initiatives that seek to present Jain cosmology in dialogue with contemporary science. At the same time, responsible teachers emphasize that the text’s authority lies in its role as a scriptural map rather than a literal astronomical manual: it articulates a moral and spiritual universe structured by karma and nonviolence, inviting practitioners to situate their lives within a vast, ethically charged cosmos.\n\nScholars and traditional teachers note that some details—such as exact numerical magnitudes or the physical interpretation of cosmic regions—require careful handling, especially when translated into modern languages. There are variations in manuscript traditions, occasional uncertainties in Prakrit readings, and differences among commentaries regarding specific measurements or classifications. For serious study, consultation of a reliable edition, traditional commentary, and guidance from a qualified Jain ācārya or scholar is recommended, particularly when using Trilokasara to inform practice or doctrinal positions.",

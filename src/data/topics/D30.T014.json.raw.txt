  "overview": "Haribhadra (often styled Haribhadrasūri or Haribhadra Yākinīputra) is one of the most influential Jain philosophers and doxographers of the classical period. He is generally associated with the Śvetāmbara tradition and is renowned for his extensive writings in Sanskrit and Prakrit on Jain doctrine, ethics, yoga, and comparative philosophy. Traditional accounts place him around 459–529 CE, but modern scholarship—based on his familiarity with Buddhist philosopher Dharmakīrti and references in later texts—more commonly situates him in the 7th–8th centuries CE.\n\nHagiographical narratives describe Haribhadra as a highly learned Brahmin, originally trained in Vedic literature and logic. In one widely transmitted story, he vows to become a disciple of anyone who can utter a sentence he cannot understand. A Jain nun, Yākinī Mahattarā, recites a verse that puzzles him, leading him to seek instruction from her teacher, Jinabhaṭasūri. After accepting initiation into Jainism, he receives the name Yākinīputra—“spiritual son of Yākinī”—and becomes a prominent monk of the Vidyādhara Kula, an ancient renunciant lineage now extinct.\n\nHistorically, Haribhadra is celebrated for his comparative and dialogical approach. In works such as the Ṣaḍdarśanasamuccaya and Yogadṛṣṭisamuccaya, he surveys major Indian philosophical systems—Jain, Buddhist, Nyāya-Vaiśeṣika, Sāṃkhya, Mīmāṃsā, Vedānta—and various yoga traditions. Rather than presenting them merely polemically, he analyzes their doctrines, methods, and soteriological aims, often emphasizing that different paths can share a common ultimate truth. This attitude is closely linked to the Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda (non-absolutism), which affirms the many-sidedness of reality and cautions against dogmatic one-sided views.\n\nHaribhadra’s Yogadṛṣṭisamuccaya is especially important for Jain yoga. It offers a systematic eightfold scheme of yoga—Mitrā, Tārā, Balā, Dīprā, Sthirā, Kāntā, Prabhā, and Parā—while engaging with Patañjali’s Yogasūtra and other Buddhist and Vedāntic yoga models. He retains a clear Jain identity, grounding yoga in non-violence, ethical discipline, and the goal of kevalajñāna (omniscience), yet he acknowledges that liberated beings across traditions teach a fundamentally similar truth, even if expressed in different languages and conceptual frameworks.\n\nIn the Ṣaḍdarśanasamuccaya, Haribhadra presents and evaluates six major philosophical systems, offering a concise doxography that became a key source for later Jain and non-Jain scholars. His writings also include works on logic, narrative literature, and devotional practice. Over time, the corpus attributed to “Haribhadra” expanded, and modern researchers distinguish at least two figures—Haribhadra Virahāṅka and Haribhadra Yākinīputra—based on differences in doctrinal emphasis and historical references. This has led to ongoing debates about the precise dating and authorship of individual texts.\n\nWithin Jain intellectual history, Haribhadra stands out as a bridge-builder: a monk who combined rigorous Jain commitment with openness to dialogue, careful study of rival systems, and a willingness to learn from them without surrendering his own tradition’s core principles. His works continue to be studied in Jain seminaries and universities, and they remain central for understanding how Jain thinkers engaged with the broader landscape of Indian philosophy, yoga, and interreligious conversation.",

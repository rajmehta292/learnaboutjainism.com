  "overview": "Interfaith application in the Jain tradition refers to the way Jain philosophical and ethical principles—especially ahiṃsā (non‑violence), anekāntavāda (doctrine of many‑sidedness), and syādvāda (conditional predication)—are brought into dialogue with other religious and secular worldviews in order to foster mutual understanding, peaceful coexistence, and shared ethical action. Historically, Jains have lived as a minority community within broader Hindu, Buddhist, Islamic, Christian, and Sikh environments, which has encouraged a dialogical posture rather than a triumphalist one. This posture is grounded not in relativism but in a disciplined recognition that truth is complex, perspectives are partial, and violence—physical, verbal, or intellectual—obstructs spiritual progress.\n\nFrom a doctrinal standpoint, interfaith application is most closely linked to anekāntavāda, the insight that reality possesses infinitely many aspects and that any single statement captures only a limited angle. When applied to religious diversity, this becomes a method for acknowledging that different traditions may grasp distinct dimensions of the same vast reality, or may at least articulate coherent paths for their adherents. Instead of erasing differences, Jain thinkers use anekāntavāda to analyze them carefully, showing how apparently conflicting claims can be understood as conditionally valid within their own frameworks. This has made Jainism an important resource in contemporary discussions of pluralism and interreligious dialogue, where it offers a non‑Western model of epistemic humility and dialogical identity.  [[0]](https://books.google.com/books/about/Jain_Approaches_to_Plurality.html?id=X1zvswEACAAJ)\n\nAhiṃsā provides the ethical backbone of Jain interfaith engagement. The commitment to non‑harm extends beyond the protection of living beings to include the avoidance of harsh speech, polemical aggression, and coercive proselytization. In interfaith settings, this means prioritizing listening over winning arguments, seeking reconciliation over victory, and refusing to justify violence in the name of religious truth. Modern scholarship has emphasized how Jain ethics—ahiṃsā, aparigraha (non‑possessiveness), and related vows—can inform global conversations on peacebuilding, environmental responsibility, and social harmony, making Jain perspectives relevant far beyond the community itself.  [[1]](https://rhimrj.co.in/index.php/rhimrj/article/view/796) [[2]](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s40961-025-00379-y.pdf)\n\nIn practical terms, Jain interfaith application appears in several domains. First, in academic and theological dialogue, Jain scholars and practitioners participate in conferences, councils, and bilateral exchanges where anekāntavāda is explicitly presented as a tool for understanding plurality. Second, in conflict resolution and mediation, Jain principles are integrated into models that combine religious resources from multiple traditions—for example, frameworks that bring together anekāntavāda, Islamic concepts of sulḥ (reconciliation), and Gandhian satyāgraha (truth‑force) to address communal tensions. These models argue that faith‑based approaches, when carefully articulated, can complement secular methods and speak more directly to communities whose identities are religiously shaped.  [[3]](https://irpj.euclid.int/articles/dispute-resolution-through-religious-lens-a-comparative-analysis-of-hinduism-islam-and-jainism-with-reference-to-a-common-word-and-anekantavad/) [[4]](https://irpj.euclid.int/articles/the-triple-e-mediation-model-beyond-monolithic-truths-integrating-anekantavada-sulh-and-satyagraha-in-interfaith-conflict-resolution-model/)\n\nThird, in grassroots interfaith initiatives, Jain organizations collaborate with other religious groups on projects such as vegetarianism and vegan advocacy, animal protection, environmental campaigns, and non‑violent education. Here, Jain teachings are not imposed but offered as one voice among many, with anekāntavāda guiding Jains to recognize the partial validity of others’ motivations—whether they arise from stewardship, compassion, or justice. This dialogical identity allows Jains to maintain their distinctive practices (such as strict dietary rules and careful attention to micro‑life) while still forming coalitions around shared concerns.\n\nModern scholarship on Jainism has highlighted that this interfaith application is not a recent invention but a development of long‑standing patterns. Classical Jain authors engaged in rigorous debates with Buddhist, Nyāya, Vedānta, and other schools, using anekāntavāda both to critique absolutist claims and to articulate Jain positions in relation to others. In colonial and post‑colonial periods, Jains adapted these tools to new contexts, including encounters with Christianity and modern secularism. Contemporary studies show that Jain dialogical identity can serve as a resource for global interreligious dialogue, offering a way to affirm one’s own path while remaining open to learning from others. Jainism does not collapse all differences into a single homogenized truth, nor does it endorse harmful practices simply because they belong to another tradition. Ahiṃsā remains a non‑negotiable criterion: any doctrine or ritual that involves cruelty, exploitation, or spiritual coercion is subject to ethical critique. Thus, Jain interfaith engagement is both hospitable and discerning—seeking dialogue, cooperation, and mutual enrichment, while retaining a firm commitment to non‑violence and spiritual discipline. For complex ethical or pastoral questions, especially those involving health, family obligations, or initiation into monastic life, Jain tradition emphasizes consultation with qualified human teachers and professionals, ensuring that interfaith openness never replaces careful, embodied guidance.\n\nIn summary, interfaith application in Jainism is the lived and conceptual practice of bringing Jain ethical and philosophical insights into constructive relationship with other traditions. It is grounded in scriptural teachings on non‑violence and many‑sidedness, elaborated by classical and modern thinkers, and expressed today in scholarship, mediation, activism, and everyday neighborly relations. Rather than a strategy of compromise, it is a disciplined spiritual stance: to see more, to harm less, and to speak with humility in a world of diverse convictions.",

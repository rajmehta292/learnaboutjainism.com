  "overview": "The historicity of Pārśvanātha (Pārśva), the 23rd Jina of the current avasarpinī, sits at the intersection of traditional Jain narrative, sectarian memory, and modern critical historiography. In Jain self-understanding, Pārśva is a fully historical tīrthaṅkara who lived in Vārāṇasī, founded an ascetic community, and whose fourfold discipline was later expanded by Mahāvīra. Śvetāmbara and Digambara traditions both treat him as a concrete person whose life, teachings, and miracles are embedded in a larger cosmic chronology of 24 Jinas. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81r%C5%9Bvan%C4%81tha)\n\nFrom the perspective of modern scholarship, Pārśva is often regarded as the earliest Jain teacher for whom a historical core can be plausibly reconstructed. Several historians place him roughly between the 9th and 8th centuries BCE, or more cautiously between the 8th and 6th centuries BCE, on the basis of internal textual chronology, comparison with Buddhist and Brahmanical sources, and the development of śramaṇa movements in North India. In this view, Pārśva stands as a proto-Jain ascetic leader whose community and discipline were later reformulated and universalized by Mahāvīra. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/P%C4%81r%C5%9Bvan%C4%81tha)\n\nRecent work has complicated this relatively confident picture. Bansidhar Bhatt’s \"Early Sources of the Jaina Tradition\" systematically re-examines early canonical and post-canonical materials and argues that the figure of Pārśva, as we now know him, is largely the product of later narrative construction and reform movements after Mahāvīra. According to this reassessment, the textual strata that seem to attest a distinct, earlier teacher named Pārśva may instead reflect retrospective systematization, with biographical and doctrinal details projected back to create a lineage of Jinas culminating in Mahāvīra. This does not necessarily deny that there were earlier ascetics or teachers, but it questions whether we can securely identify a single historical individual corresponding to the fully developed hagiographical Pārśvanātha. [[1]](https://api.pageplace.de/preview/DT0400.9781317683964_A49277669/preview-9781317683964_A49277669.pdf) [[2]](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/books/mono/10.4324/9781315774282/early-sources-jaina-tradition-bansidhar-bhatt)\n\nWithin the Jain tradition itself, the historicity of Pārśva is supported by multiple strands: (1) canonical references that presuppose an already established fourfold discipline (caturyama-dharma) associated with an earlier teacher; (2) narrative cycles locating his birth, renunciation, and liberation in specific places such as Vārāṇasī and Sammeda Śikhara; and (3) ritual and pilgrimage networks that treat sites linked to Pārśva as historically grounded. Studies of Jain sacred geography in Vārāṇasī, for example, show how biographies of Pārśva have been used from the early modern period onward to anchor Jain presence in a city otherwise perceived as predominantly Hindu, reinforcing the sense that his life unfolded in recognizable historical space. [[3]](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/11/3/117)\n\nA careful, source-grounded approach therefore distinguishes several layers: (a) early doctrinal references to a fourfold discipline and to previous teachers, which may indicate an older ascetic tradition; (b) canonical and commentarial passages that explicitly name Pārśva and place him in a lineage of Jinas; (c) fully developed hagiographies with miracles, serpent-protection motifs, and elaborate genealogies; and (d) modern sectarian and devotional elaborations. The more we move from (a) to (d), the richer the narrative but the weaker the direct historical evidentiary value. The historicity question is not simply whether Pārśva \"existed\" but how much of the later narrative can be traced back to early, independent testimony.\n\nFor Jain lay and monastic practice, the historicity of Pārśva is not primarily a critical problem but a lived assumption: he is venerated as a real tīrthaṅkara whose image, especially with the serpent hood, structures temple iconography and pilgrimage. The debate matters most for academic reconstruction of early Jain history, the evolution of vows (from four to five), and the relationship between Mahāvīra’s community and earlier ascetic groups. Given the limits of the textual record and the layered nature of the sources, responsible scholarship typically presents Pārśva as a figure with a probable historical nucleus, while openly acknowledging that many biographical details and chronological claims derive from later, sectarian narrative construction rather than independent, contemporaneous evidence.",

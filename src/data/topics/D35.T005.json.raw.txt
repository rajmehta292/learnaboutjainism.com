  "overview": "Svetambara Terapanth (Śvetāmbara Terāpanth) is a non-idolatrous sub-sect of the Svetambara branch of Jainism, founded in the 18th century in western India. It emerged as a reform movement within the broader Svetambara ascetic tradition, emphasizing rigorous discipline, centralized leadership, and a distinctive interpretation of ahiṃsā (non‑violence) and renunciation. The name “Terapanth” is commonly explained through narratives about thirteen (tera) lay followers or doctrinal points, though these etiological stories vary and are not uniformly attested in early sources.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Avet%C4%81mbara_Terapanth)\n\nHistorically, Terapanth traces its origin to Acharya Bhikshu (Bhikṣu), a monk originally associated with the Sthanakavasi (non‑idolatrous Svetambara) tradition. In Vikram Samvat 1817 (1760 CE), after sustained disagreement with his teacher Acharya Raghunathji over the conduct of ascetics and the interpretation of canonical discipline, Bhikshu separated with a small group of monks at Bagadi in Marwar (present‑day Rajasthan). This event is remembered as the formal beginning of the Svetambara Terapanth order. Bhikshu argued that contemporary ascetics had become lax in observing the canonical rules derived from the Jain Āgamas and sought a return to stricter, text‑based discipline.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Avet%C4%81mbara_Terapanth) [[1]](https://www.philtar.ac.uk/encyclopedia/jainism/shvter.html)\n\nOne of the defining features of Terapanth is its highly centralized organizational structure. The entire monastic community is placed under the authority of a single Acharya, regarded as the spiritual and administrative head of the order. This principle is codified in the maryādā—an internal code of conduct for ascetics—which regulates movement, preaching, relations with lay followers, and internal discipline. The Acharya’s role is not merely symbolic: decisions about monastic itineraries, disciplinary measures, and major initiatives are coordinated through this central leadership, giving Terapanth a reputation for strong internal cohesion and uniform practice. Over more than two centuries, the sect has had a relatively small number of Acharyas; contemporary accounts note eleven Acharyas to date, with Acharya Mahashraman recognized as the eleventh.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Avet%C4%81mbara_Terapanth) [[1]](https://www.philtar.ac.uk/encyclopedia/jainism/shvter.html)\n\nDoctrinally, Terapanth shares the core Svetambara Jain framework of the three jewels—right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct—along with belief in karma, rebirth, and liberation (mokṣa). Its distinctiveness lies in the way it interprets ahiṃsā and renunciation in the ascetic sphere. Acharya Bhikshu advanced a radical reading of non‑violence and non‑assistance: he argued that actively saving or assisting other living beings (for example, rescuing animals from slaughter) could entangle the rescuer in the future violence those beings might commit, thereby increasing karmic bondage. From this perspective, the highest lokottara (transcendent) religious duty of a monk is not to intervene in worldly affairs but to purify one’s own soul through restraint and inner discipline. Many Jains from other traditions have criticized this position as one‑sided (ekānta), arguing that it conflicts with the broader Jain doctrine of anekāntavāda (many‑sidedness) and the long‑standing emphasis on compassionate action.  [[1]](https://www.philtar.ac.uk/encyclopedia/jainism/shvter.html)\n\nTerapanth is firmly non‑idolatrous. Like Sthanakavasi Jains, Terapanthis do not worship images of Tirthankaras in temples and instead focus on meditation, scriptural study, and ethical discipline. Monks and nuns wear simple white robes in keeping with Svetambara norms and permanently use the muhapatti (mouth‑covering cloth), both as a symbol of restraint and as a practical measure to reduce harm to airborne organisms. The sect’s ascetics observe strict celibacy, non‑possession, truthfulness, and itinerant life, and they maintain a clear separation between monastic and lay roles. Lay Terapanthis participate through listening to discourses, observing vows, supporting ascetics, and engaging in ethical reform movements inspired by the Acharyas.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/%C5%9Avet%C4%81mbara_Terapanth) [[1]](https://www.philtar.ac.uk/encyclopedia/jainism/shvter.html)\n\nIn the 20th century, Terapanth became widely known beyond Jain circles through the Anuvrat (Anuvrata) movement, initiated by Acharya Tulsi. Building on the idea of “small vows” (aṇu‑vrata), Tulsi proposed a set of concise ethical commitments—such as refraining from violence, dishonesty, addiction, and social discrimination—addressed not only to Jains but to people of all religions and backgrounds. The movement sought moral rearmament and global peace, drawing analogies between the destructive potential of the atom (aṇu) and the constructive potential of atomic vows (anuvrata). This initiative, along with later developments like Preksha meditation, positioned Terapanth as a modernizing force within Jainism, engaging with contemporary issues such as nuclear threat, environmental concern, and interfaith ethics while still claiming continuity with canonical Jain principles.  [[1]](https://www.philtar.ac.uk/encyclopedia/jainism/shvter.html)\n\nGeographically, Terapanth has a strong presence in Rajasthan and neighboring regions, with communities also in Gujarat, Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, and diasporic centers such as the United Kingdom, North America, and Nepal. As a Svetambara sub‑sect, it shares scriptural reference points with other Svetambara groups, particularly the Āgamas, but it selectively emphasizes texts and interpretations that support its disciplined, non‑idolatrous, and centrally organized ethos. Modern scholarship notes both the internal coherence of Terapanth’s institutional life and ongoing debates about the balance between strict renunciation and compassionate social engagement. Some aspects of early Terapanth history—such as the precise reasons for Bhikshu’s break, the full content of his original writings, and the evolution of maryādā rules—remain dependent on sectarian chronicles and later reconstructions, and thus require careful comparison of manuscripts and oral traditions. For detailed doctrinal guidance or life‑changing decisions (such as initiation, prolonged fasting, or major vows), Terapanth followers are expected to consult living Acharyas or senior monks and nuns within the lineage rather than relying solely on written summaries or secondary accounts.\n",

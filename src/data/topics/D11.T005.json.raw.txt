  "overview": "Avirata-samyagdrishti (Sanskrit: अविरतसम्यग्दृष्टि, Prakrit: अविरतसमग्गदिट्ठि) is the technical Jain term for the fourth guṇasthāna (spiritual stage) on the path of liberation, usually translated as “vowless right faith” or “non-renouncer with right vision.” It designates a soul that has attained samyagdarśana—right faith or right worldview—but has not yet undertaken the formal vows of renunciation (virati) characteristic of higher stages. In the classical Jain schema of fourteen guṇasthānas, this stage marks the decisive transition from spiritual delusion (mithyātva) to authentic insight, while still remaining embedded in ordinary worldly conduct.\n\nThe term is a compound of “avirata” (non-abstaining, not yet observing vows) and “samyag-dṛṣṭi” (right vision/faith). Avirata here does not mean moral indifference; rather, it indicates that the soul has not yet entered the disciplined framework of partial or total renunciation (deśavirata and sarvavirata). The person at this stage continues to live as a householder or non-renouncing layperson, but their inner orientation has shifted from wrong belief to right faith in the fundamental Jain truths—such as the reality of the soul (jīva), the efficacy of karma, the existence of liberated beings (siddhas), and the authority of the Tīrthaṅkaras and their teachings.\n\nIn both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions, Avirata-samyagdrishti is described as the first stable stage of right faith after the abandonment of mithyātva (complete wrong belief) and mixed delusion (miśra-moha). The soul has overcome the most intense forms of deluding karma (anantanubandhi mohanīya), which previously bound it to entrenched false views. However, the conduct-deluding karmas that prevent full renunciation are still operative. As a result, the person’s behavior remains largely that of a lay follower, even though their inner conviction aligns with the Jain path.\n\nClassical handbooks of Jainology explain that right faith at this stage can arise in three principal modes: upaśama-samyaktva (right faith produced by temporary subsidence of wrong-belief karmas), kṣayopaśama-samyaktva (right faith produced by a combination of partial destruction and subsidence of those karmas), and kṣayika-samyaktva (right faith produced by their complete destruction. In all three cases, the defining feature of Avirata-samyagdrishti is that the soul has attained samyaktva but has not yet taken up the vows that characterize the fifth guṇasthāna (deśavirata) and beyond. The stage is therefore pivotal: it secures a correct orientation while leaving open the practical step of renunciation.\n\nFrom the perspective of lay practice, Avirata-samyagdrishti corresponds to a deeply committed lay follower who has genuine śraddhā (faith) in the Jain teachings, reverence for the Tīrthaṅkaras, and acceptance of the core metaphysical and ethical doctrines, yet has not formally entered the path of systematic vows such as the anuvratas (small vows) or the more advanced pratimās. Many texts emphasize that while the term “vowless” is used, the person is not devoid of ethical restraint; they may practice occasional austerities, charity, and nonviolence. What is absent is the structured, formally adopted renunciatory framework that transforms general ethical inclination into codified vows.\n\nDoctrinally, Avirata-samyagdrishti is crucial because right faith (samyagdarśana) is considered the gateway to all higher spiritual progress. Without it, scriptural study, austerities, and ritual observances remain spiritually ineffective or even counterproductive. With it, even modest practices begin to generate transformative merit and reduce karmic bondage. The fourth guṇasthāna thus marks the soul’s first firm foothold on the ladder of liberation, though it still remains far from the advanced stages of right conduct (samyak-cāritra) and the higher levels of meditation and omniscience.\n\nIn the broader karmic and guṇasthāna framework, Avirata-samyagdrishti is characterized by the subsidence or destruction of the wrong-belief (darśana-moha) karmas, while the conduct-deluding (cāritra-moha) karmas remain strong enough to prevent renunciation. The soul at this stage is protected from falling back into gross wrong belief, but it can still regress if right faith is not stabilized and supported by ethical discipline and spiritual practice. Hence traditional commentaries often urge that one should not remain content with Avirata-samyagdrishti but should move swiftly toward deśavirata (partial renunciation) and beyond.\n\nHistorically and in community education, this stage is sometimes used to explain the difference between mere cultural affiliation and genuine right faith. A person born into a Jain family may participate in rituals and festivals, yet still be at the level of mithyātva or miśra-moha if they lack clear conviction in the Jain path. Conversely, someone who has deeply internalized Jain teachings and reveres the Tīrthaṅkaras, even without formal vows, can be described—conceptually—as approximating Avirata-samyagdrishti. However, classical texts treat the guṇasthānas as precise states of the soul’s karmic configuration, not as loose sociological labels.\n\nBecause Avirata-samyagdrishti is a technical doctrinal category, its full understanding requires attention to the layered Jain ontology of jīva, karma, mohanīya (deluding) karmas, and the fourteen guṇasthānas. It also requires care in translation: “vowless right faith” can be misleading if taken to imply moral laxity. The stage is better understood as “right faith without formal renunciation,” a spiritually significant but incomplete condition that calls the soul onward to the disciplined path of vows and ultimately to liberation.",

  "overview": "Suparshvanatha (Sanskrit: सुपार्श्वनाथ, Supārśvanātha) is revered in Jain tradition as the seventh Tirthankara (ford-maker) of the current avasarpinī (descending) time-cycle. He belongs to the Ikṣvāku dynasty and is said to have been born in Vārāṇasī (Kāśī) to King Pratiṣṭha and Queen Pṛthvī. His emblem (lāñchana) is the svastika, and his body-color is described as golden, marking him iconographically distinct from other Tirthankaras. Traditional Jain cosmology attributes to him an extraordinarily long lifespan measured in pūrva units and a height of 200 dhanusha, situating him firmly within the mythic, supra-historical framework of Jain universal history rather than within conventional chronological dating.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suparshvanatha)\n\nIn narrative accounts preserved in both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions, Suparshvanatha passes through the standard sequence of Tirthankara life-stages: auspicious conception dreams of the mother, princely youth, righteous rule, renunciation, austerity, attainment of kevala-jñāna (omniscience), and finally mokṣa (liberation). Queen Pṛthvī is said to have experienced sixteen auspicious dreams prior to his birth, a motif shared with other Tirthankaras and used in Jain literature to signal the emergence of a world-teacher. After a prolonged period of just and compassionate kingship, Suparshvanatha is described as becoming deeply contemplative upon witnessing the impermanence of worldly phenomena—falling leaves, withering flowers, and the inevitable decay of all compounded things. This insight leads him to renounce his kingdom, entrust rule to his son, and adopt the life of a Jain ascetic.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suparshvanatha)\n\nFollowing his renunciation, Suparshvanatha is said to have practiced intense tapas (austerities) and meditation. After nine months of ascetic practice, he attained kevala-jñāna, becoming an omniscient Jina who establishes the dharma suitable for beings of his era. As with other Tirthankaras, his teaching is portrayed as a re-articulation of the eternal Jain path of non-violence (ahiṃsā), truthfulness (satya), non-stealing (asteya), chastity (brahmacarya), and non-possessiveness (aparigraha), though the detailed doctrinal formulations are systematized later in canonical and philosophical texts. Suparshvanatha’s samavasaraṇa (universal preaching assembly) becomes the setting in which gods, humans, and other beings receive instruction, reinforcing the Jain view that Tirthankaras are cosmically significant teachers whose influence transcends sectarian and regional boundaries.\n\nSuparshvanatha is traditionally said to have attained mokṣa at Śikharjī (Sammeda Śikhara) in present-day Jharkhand, a peak that later becomes one of the most important Jain pilgrimage sites. Multiple Tirthankaras, including Suparshvanatha, are believed to have achieved liberation there, and this layered sanctity shapes the ritual geography of Jain communities. In art and iconography, Suparshvanatha is depicted in standard Jina postures—either padmāsana (seated meditation) or kāyotsarga (standing meditation)—with a serene, inwardly focused expression. His svastika emblem is typically carved on the pedestal of the image, and his golden complexion is noted in textual descriptions, though in practice images may be rendered in stone or metal without strict adherence to color symbolism.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suparshvanatha)\n\nSectarian traditions—Digambara and Śvetāmbara—share the basic narrative structure of Suparshvanatha’s life but may differ in minor details of chronology, ritual emphasis, and associated yakṣa–yakṣī (attendant deities). In temple practice, Suparshvanatha is worshipped alongside the full set of twenty-four Tirthankaras, and his image often appears in larger Tirthankara arrays or on temple walls rather than as the sole central icon. Nonetheless, specific temples—such as the Suparshvanatha temple at Bhadaini in Vārāṇasī—commemorate his birth and life-story, embedding his memory in local sacred landscapes and reinforcing the link between universal Jain cosmology and particular historical cities.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Suparshvanatha)\n\nFrom the perspective of Jain philosophy, Suparshvanatha’s significance lies less in unique doctrinal innovations and more in his role as a perfected exemplar of the path. As a Tirthankara, he embodies the complete purification of karma, the traversal of the guṇasthānas (stages of spiritual development), and the realization of the soul’s innate qualities of infinite knowledge, perception, bliss, and energy. His biography functions as a narrative vehicle through which lay and monastic communities contemplate impermanence, ethical responsibility, and the possibility of liberation. In contemporary Jain discourse, Suparshvanatha is invoked in sermons, educational materials, and devotional recitations as one of the twenty-four luminous points in the cosmic cycle of Jain dharma, reminding practitioners that the path of non-violence and self-restraint has been repeatedly rediscovered and re-taught across vast stretches of time.\n\nModern scholarship, while unable to treat Suparshvanatha as a historically datable figure in the way that some later Tirthankaras (such as Pārśvanātha or Mahāvīra) are studied, recognizes his importance within the internal logic of Jain universal history and ritual life. Academic treatments of Jain cosmology and narrative literature analyze Suparshvanatha’s story as part of the broader pattern of Tirthankara biographies, noting how these accounts encode ethical ideals, social models of kingship and renunciation, and metaphysical claims about the structure of time and the soul’s journey. For students of Jainism, Suparshvanatha thus serves as a bridge between mythic cosmology and lived religious practice: a figure whose life-story is less about historical chronology and more about the enduring possibility of radical ethical transformation.",

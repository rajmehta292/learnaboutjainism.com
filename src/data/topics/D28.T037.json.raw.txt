  "overview": "Dashavaikalika (Daśavaikālika-sūtra, often also called Dasakaliyasuttam in Prakrit) is a key Jain canonical text that has played a formative role in shaping the discipline and ethos of ascetic life, especially within the Śvetāmbara traditions. It is widely regarded as a foundational manual for newly initiated monks and nuns, offering a concise yet rich synthesis of the basic rules of conduct (śramaṇa-ācāra), ethical restraints, and attitudes necessary for the path of renunciation. In many contemporary initiation practices, Dashavaikalika is memorized and recited soon after major rituals such as pratikramaṇa, and in some lineages it functionally replaces the Ācārāṅga-sūtra as the first practical handbook given to novices.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/dashvaikalika_sutra_229833_229833)\n\nThe text is traditionally attributed to Ācārya Śayyambhav Suri (also known in Prakrit forms such as Seyyambhava or Sijambhava), a learned Brahmin who, according to narrative accounts, turned from Vedic sacrificial religion to the path of the Jina after deep reflection on the nature of true knowledge and non-violence. He became a disciple of Ācārya Prabhavasvāmi and is remembered as the fourth teacher in that lineage. Dashavaikalika is said to have been composed especially for his young disciple Manak, whose short lifespan required a compact yet comprehensive guide to monastic life. This biographical framing reinforces the text’s character as a practical, urgent teaching aimed at beginners, rather than a purely theoretical treatise.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/dashvaikalika_sutra_229833_229833)\n\nFrom the standpoint of canonical classification, Dashavaikalika occupies an interesting position. In the Śvetāmbara tradition it is counted among the Mūlasūtras—fundamental texts used for the training of ascetics—and is often listed third among them. Earlier, in works such as the Nandi-sūtra, it was classified as a “kālika” text, meaning one to be read at specific times, but over centuries its pedagogical importance led to its elevation as a core manual. In Digambara discussions of the lost Angas and their supplements, Dashavaikalika (Dasakaliyasuttam) is grouped among the “Unanga Śruta,” texts that preserve the essence of teachings after the gradual loss of the original twelve Angas. Thus, both Śvetāmbara and Digambara scholarship recognize it as a bridge between early canonical material and later pedagogical literature.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/dashvaikalika_sutra_229833_229833) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_42_mool_03_dashvaikalik_sutra_001151_001151)\n\nStructurally, Dashavaikalika is divided into ten chapters (adhyayana) and supplemented by two chūlikās (appendices). Each chapter focuses on a distinct dimension of ascetic life: praise of dharma and the mendicant’s way of living on alms; the origins and inner disposition of the ascetic; stories illustrating minor and great ascetic conduct; detailed treatment of the six classes of living beings (ṣaḍ-jīvanikāya) and the implications of non-violence; rules for alms-seeking and food selection; purity and discipline of speech; mindfulness in conduct; humility and concentration; and the marks of the true monk. The chūlikās further refine themes such as pleasant, non-harmful speech and secluded, careful conduct. Later commentarial traditions—especially the niryukti (metrical commentary) of Bhadrabahu and the prose chūrṇi of Agastyasimha—expand these chapters with etymological analysis, doctrinal clarification, and practical examples, making the text a layered pedagogical corpus rather than a single, flat manual.  [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/dashvaikalaik_sutra_022576_022576) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_suttani_satikam_part_27_dasvaikaalika_003331_003331) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_42_mool_03_dashvaikalik_sutra_001151_001151)\n\nIn terms of content and ethical vision, Dashavaikalika emphasizes ahimsa (non-violence), saṁyama (self-restraint), and tapa (austerity) as the core of dharma, but always in close connection with concrete behaviors: how one walks, speaks, eats, accepts or refuses offerings, and interacts with rulers, householders, and other social groups. It warns against eighteen forms of corruption in monastic life, analyzes sinful and sinless speech, and insists on vigilance toward even the smallest forms of life. The text’s repeated use of similes—such as the mendicant compared to a bee taking nectar without harming flowers—illustrates its pedagogical strategy: to make subtle ethical ideals vivid and memorable for novices. In Sthānakavāsī and other non-image-worshipping Śvetāmbara subtraditions, illustrated and translated editions of Dashavaikalika continue to serve as basic compendia of ascetic conduct, showing its enduring relevance across sectarian lines.  [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_29_mool_02_dasvaikalik_sutra_sthanakvasi_007649_007649) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/dashvaikalaik_sutra_022576_022576)\n\nModern scholarship treats Dashavaikalika as an important window into the historical development of Jain monastic discipline and the transmission of canonical knowledge. Critical editions that present the mūla text alongside Bhadrabahu’s niryukti and Agastyasimha’s chūrṇi allow researchers to trace how key concepts—such as the classification of living beings, the gradations of vows, and the nuances of right speech—were interpreted and expanded over time. At the same time, traditional communities continue to approach the text devotionally and practically, reciting it in daily or periodic study sessions and using it as a mirror for self-examination in the monastic path. Because of this dual life—as a living manual and a historical document—Dashavaikalika occupies a central place in both religious practice and academic study of Jainism.

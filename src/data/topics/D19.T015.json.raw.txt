  "overview": "Vyutsarga (also spelled vyutsarga or vyutsargaḥ) is a key technical term in Jain philosophy and practice, most commonly translated as “renunciation” or “relinquishment.” In its strict doctrinal sense, it denotes the deliberate giving up of the attitudes of “I” and “mine” (ahaṃkāra and mamakāra) with respect to the body, possessions, and egoic identity. As an internal discipline, it is not merely an outward abandonment of things, but a transformation of the inner stance toward them—loosening the grip of possessiveness and self-centeredness so that the soul’s intrinsic qualities of knowledge, faith, and conduct can shine forth.  [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084911.html)\n\nIn the canonical classification of austerities (tapas), vyutsarga is listed among the six internal austerities (ābhyantara-tapa) in the Tattvārthasūtra 9.20: expiation (prāyaścitta), reverence (vinaya), service (vaiyāvṛttya), study (svādhyāya), renunciation (vyutsarga), and meditation (dhyāna). Internal austerities are called “internal” because they primarily restrain and refine the mind rather than focusing on gross bodily hardship. Vyutsarga, in this framework, is the conscious relinquishment of the sense of ownership and identity that binds the soul to karmic influx; by giving up the subtle insistence on “my body,” “my status,” “my feelings,” the practitioner weakens the passions (kaṣāya) that sustain bondage.  [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084911.html)\n\nTraditional commentaries elaborate that vyutsarga operates on two closely related levels. On the one hand, it is a mental renunciation—an inner letting go of attachment and ego. On the other hand, it is closely associated with the practice of kāyotsarga (“abandonment of the body”), a meditative posture in which the practitioner stands or sits motionless, withdrawing attention from bodily sensations and movements. Some sources describe kāyotsarga as the “full form” or complete embodiment of vyutsarga: when one successfully maintains bodily stillness while remaining inwardly detached, the renunciation of “I” and “mine” becomes experientially vivid. In this way, vyutsarga is both a doctrinal category of internal austerity and a lived contemplative discipline that integrates posture, attention, and insight.  [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/concept/vyutsarga) [[2]](https://www.jainqq.org/explore/001263/237)\n\nWithin broader Jain sādhanā (spiritual practice), vyutsarga is closely linked to the ideal of vītarāgatā—freedom from attachment and aversion. It supports the shedding of karmas (nirjarā) by undermining the very attitudes that attract and bind karmic particles to the soul. When a practitioner repeatedly cultivates vyutsarga, even in small daily acts of letting go—releasing resentment, softening pride, relinquishing the urge to control—these moments accumulate into a stable disposition of non-possessiveness (aparigraha). In both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions, the emphasis falls on the inner renunciation: external simplicity and bodily disciplines are valuable insofar as they foster the deeper relinquishment of egoic clinging.\n\nVyutsarga also has an important ethical dimension. By renouncing the attitude of “I” and “mine,” the practitioner becomes more sensitive to the reality of other living beings and more committed to ahiṃsā (non-harm). Many unintentional harms arise from restless movement, careless speech, and self-centered pursuit of comfort or status. Practices associated with vyutsarga—such as stillness, introspection, and relinquishment of possessiveness—help reduce such harms and align conduct with compassion. In this sense, vyutsarga is not an escape from the world but a disciplined way of being in the world with less ego and more responsibility.\n\nFinally, vyutsarga should be distinguished from formal monastic renunciation (dīkṣā). While dīkṣā involves institutional vows and a change of life-status, vyutsarga is a universal inner practice accessible to both laypeople and ascetics. Lay followers cultivate vyutsarga in everyday contexts—sharing resources, accepting loss without bitterness, practicing bodily stillness in samayika, and relinquishing the insistence on personal comfort. Ascetics deepen the same principle through more rigorous vows and extended meditative disciplines. In all cases, vyutsarga is a gradual, experiential process rather than a single dramatic act: it is the ongoing art of letting go, so that the soul can move toward its natural state of purity and liberation.",

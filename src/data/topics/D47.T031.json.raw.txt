  "overview": "In Jainism, animal welfare is rooted in the central ethical principle of ahimsa—non‑harm to all jivas (living beings). Animals are understood as embodied souls journeying through samsara, morally and spiritually significant in their own right, not merely as resources for human use. Jain ontology classifies living beings by the number of senses they possess, and animals are typically four‑ or five‑sensed beings, whose capacity for pain, fear, and attachment is taken very seriously in ethical deliberation. [[0]](https://ahimsa-living.org/index.php/jain-dharma/jain-ahimsa/)\n\nFrom early canonical texts onward, Jains are enjoined not to injure, subjugate, enslave, torture, or kill any living being, explicitly including animals, plants, and even micro‑organisms. The first of the great vows (mahavratas) for ascetics and its parallel minor vow (anuvrata) for laypeople prohibit killing and harming in thought, word, and deed, and also forbid ordering, approving, or facilitating harm done by others. This extends animal welfare beyond direct acts of cruelty to the entire chain of production and consumption—hunting, butchery, animal sacrifice, entertainment, and exploitative labor involving animals are all seen as grave forms of himsa. [[1]](https://rsc.byu.edu/deity-death/reverence-life-religion) [[2]](https://www.animalsandreligion.org/en/religions/animals-in-jainism)\n\nHistorically, this concern has expressed itself in strict vegetarianism, the establishment of animal shelters (panjrapoles), bird hospitals, and sanctuaries for cows, dogs, birds, and other animals near Jain temples and urban centers. Caring for injured or abandoned animals, feeding birds, and supporting sanctuaries are understood as religiously meritorious acts that cultivate compassion and reduce one’s participation in violent karmic networks. Modern Jain communities have extended this logic into contemporary debates on animal agriculture, laboratory testing, and wildlife conservation, often advocating for stronger legal protections and public policies that reduce animal suffering. [[0]](https://ahimsa-living.org/index.php/jain-dharma/jain-ahimsa/) [[3]](https://ojs.unito.it/index.php/kervan/article/view/12117) [[4]](https://scispace.com/pdf/karman-and-compassion-animals-in-the-jain-universal-history-27t2p99liq.pdf)\n\nDoctrinally, animal welfare is intertwined with other core Jain concepts. Aparigraha (non‑possessiveness) challenges the accumulation of goods and consumption patterns that depend on intensive animal exploitation, while anekantavada (many‑sidedness) invites Jains to recognize that animals have their own perspectives, experiences, and interests that must be taken seriously. Narratives from Jain universal history and biographies of Tirthankaras—such as stories of Prince Arishtanemi renouncing violence after witnessing animals destined for slaughter—illustrate how sensitivity to animal suffering can catalyze profound spiritual transformation. [[2]](https://www.animalsandreligion.org/en/religions/animals-in-jainism) [[4]](https://scispace.com/pdf/karman-and-compassion-animals-in-the-jain-universal-history-27t2p99liq.pdf)\n\nWithin and across Jain traditions (Digambara, Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka, Sthānakavāsī, Terāpanth), there is broad agreement that deliberate harm to animals is karmically heavy and spiritually regressive. At the same time, there are ongoing discussions about complex, modern questions: the ethics of dairy consumption in industrial contexts, the unintended harm caused by agriculture, and the limits of human capacity to avoid all forms of microscopic violence. Contemporary Jain scholars and activists often argue that foundational principles—ahimsa, aparigraha, and reverence for life—support moving toward plant‑based diets and minimizing all forms of animal use where reasonably possible, while acknowledging that detailed practical guidance should be sought from qualified teachers and, in matters touching health or law, from appropriate professionals.

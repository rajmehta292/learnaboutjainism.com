  "overview": "Paumacariya (also spelled Paumacariyam, Paumchariyam, or Padmacarita) is the earliest extant Jain retelling of the Ramayana, composed in Jaina Maharashtri Prakrit and traditionally attributed to Acharya Vimalasuri. It is a Purana‑kavya (epic‑purana) that re‑narrates the life of Rama—here called Padma—through explicitly Jain ethical, cosmological, and soteriological lenses. Most scholars date the work roughly to the 3rd–5th century CE, around 500–530 years after the nirvana of Mahavira, though the exact date and even Vimalasuri’s sectarian affiliation (Śvetāmbara or Digambara) remain debated in modern research.\n\nIn Paumacariya, Rama/Padma is presented not as an incarnation of Viṣṇu but as a human śalākāpuruṣa (great personage), specifically a Balabhadra, whose destiny culminates in renunciation and liberation. Violence is carefully displaced from Rama: Lakṣmaṇa, not Rama, kills Rāvaṇa, allowing Rama to embody ahiṃsā and royal restraint while still permitting the narrative’s martial climax. The poem integrates Jain doctrines of karma, cyclic time, and the taxonomy of 63 śalākāpuruṣas, situating Rama, Lakṣmaṇa, and Rāvaṇa within a larger universal history that also includes contemporaneous Jinas such as Munisuvrata. [[0]](https://mythicremembering.com/books/jain-ramayana/) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/vimalsuri_krut_paumchariya_me_pratima_vigyan_parak_samagree_211928_211928)\n\nThe narrative structure broadly follows pan‑Indian Ramayana episodes—Daśaratha’s court, the birth and education of the princes, Sītā’s svayaṃvara, exile, the abduction of Sītā, alliance with Sugriva, Hanumat’s mission to Laṅkā, and the final war—but each episode is ethically and rationally reinterpreted. Paumacariya often criticizes what it presents as “absurdities” or miraculous elements in non‑Jain Ramayana traditions, replacing them with explanations consistent with Jain rationalism and moral psychology. For example, Sītā is typically portrayed as Janaka’s natural daughter rather than a miraculous child from a furrow, and Rāma’s exile is framed more as a voluntary, politically and ethically motivated decision than as the result of divine fate or purely manipulative intrigue. [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/paumchariyam_part_1_001272_001272) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/critical_study_of_paumacariyam_022643_022643)\n\nRāvaṇa in Paumacariya is a complex figure: a powerful, learned ruler and, in many passages, a devout Jain lay follower whose downfall is tied to passion (kāma), pride (mada), and misuse of vidyās (magical knowledges). The text is rich in descriptions of cities, palaces, forests, and social life, offering valuable evidence for Gupta‑period culture and early Jain iconography. It contains numerous references to Jina images, temples, signet rings bearing Jina figures, and the worship of Tīrthaṅkaras such as Śāntinātha and Munisuvrata, thereby linking the Rama narrative to the evolving visual and ritual culture of Jainism. [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/vimalsuri_krut_paumchariya_me_pratima_vigyan_parak_samagree_211928_211928) [[4]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/essay/paumacariya-critical-study)\n\nPaumacariya also plays a foundational role in the development of Jain universal histories. It is credited in later scholarship with providing the first complete list of the 63 śalākāpuruṣas, systematizing figures like Rama (as Balabhadra), Lakṣmaṇa (as a Vasudeva or Baladeva depending on the tradition), and Rāvaṇa (as a Prativāsudeva). This framework is later elaborated in works such as Hemacandra’s Triṣaṣṭi‑śalākā‑puruṣa‑caritra. The epic’s influence extends to subsequent Jain Ramayana texts in Apabhraṃśa and Sanskrit, including Ravisena’s Padmapurāṇa and Svayambhudeva’s Paumacariu, which rework Vimalasuri’s materials while aligning them with their own doctrinal and literary agendas. [[0]](https://mythicremembering.com/books/jain-ramayana/) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/vimalsuri_krut_paumchariya_me_pratima_vigyan_parak_samagree_211928_211928)\n\nModern critical studies—such as K. R. Chandra’s \"A Critical Study of Paumacariyam\"—highlight how Paumacariya consistently humanizes its characters compared to the more overtly divine portrayals in Vālmīki’s Ramayana. Rama’s virtues are framed in terms of Jain lay ethics, kingship, and eventual renunciation rather than avatāric status; Sītā’s chastity and moral strength are emphasized without recourse to miraculous birth; and Lakṣmaṇa’s burden of violence is interpreted through Jain karmic theory. Comparative chapters in such studies show that Paumacariya reshapes key narrative nodes—Rama’s exile, Sītā’s abduction, the alliance with Sugriva, and the killing of Rāvaṇa—to foreground non‑violence, rational causality, and the inevitability of karmic consequences. [[4]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/essay/paumacariya-critical-study) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/critical_study_of_paumacariyam_022643_022643)\n\nBecause Paumacariya survives through multiple manuscripts and modern critical editions, there are textual uncertainties, variant readings, and occasional gaps. Scholars caution that any English summary or translation must distinguish between the Prakrit base text (tier A), traditional commentarial interpretations (tier B), and modern academic reconstructions (tier C). Where the poem’s doctrinal intent is clear—such as its insistence on ahiṃsā, its integration of Rama into the śalākāpuruṣa schema, and its critique of non‑Jain miracle stories—interpretation is relatively secure. Where manuscript evidence is thin or late, especially in minor episodes or descriptive passages, there remains room for disagreement and further philological work.\n",

  "overview": "Dhyanashataka (often written as Dhyānazataka, Dhyānashatak, or Dhyanadhyayan) is a classical Jain treatise on meditation that occupies a central place in the Svetambara tradition’s reflection on dhyāna. The work is transmitted together with a Sanskrit commentary by the eminent Acharya Haribhadrasuri and is preserved in later critical editions, notably the combined publication \"Dhyanhatak Tatha Dhyanstava\" edited by Balchandra Siddhantshastri. [[0]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Dhyan_Shatak_Romanized/022141) [[1]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Dhyanhatak_Tatha_Dhyanstava_Romanized/032155) It consists of approximately one hundred-plus verses or gāthās (commonly counted as 105), hence the title \"shataka\" (hundred), and systematically explores the nature, obstacles, and fruits of right meditation from a Jain doctrinal perspective.\n\nIn terms of genre, Dhyanashataka is both a hymn and a didactic manual. It weaves praise of the Tirthankaras and the vitarāga ideal together with practical guidance on how a practitioner should purify inner states, recognize unwholesome mental patterns, and cultivate auspicious dhyāna. The text is closely associated with other Jain works on meditation, such as Lalitavistara, Panchasutra, and Bhaskaranandi’s Dhyanastava, and later editors explicitly present these four as a cluster of foundational meditation scriptures. [[0]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Dhyan_Shatak_Romanized/022141) [[1]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Dhyanhatak_Tatha_Dhyanstava_Romanized/032155) Within this cluster, Dhyanashataka is distinctive for its relatively compact, verse-based structure and its emphasis on progressive inner transformation rather than ritual technique alone.\n\nHistorically, Dhyanashataka is situated in the broader Haribhadra lineage of philosophical and yogic writing. Haribhadrasuri is known for integrating Jain doctrine with wider Indian discussions of yoga and meditation, and his commentary on Dhyanashataka reflects this synthetic approach: he clarifies technical terms, aligns the verses with Jain categories of right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct (ratnatraya), and distinguishes Jain dhyāna from non-Jain contemplative practices while still acknowledging shared concerns such as mental steadiness and ethical purification. [[1]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Dhyanhatak_Tatha_Dhyanstava_Romanized/032155) The text thus serves as a bridge between early doctrinal treatments of meditation in canonical literature and later scholastic elaborations.\n\nFrom a doctrinal standpoint, Dhyanashataka underscores that true meditation is inseparable from ethical discipline and right worldview. It warns against ashubha-dhyāna (inauspicious meditation) characterized by attachment, aversion, and egoic fantasies, and instead advocates shubha-dhyāna oriented toward the pure self (ātma-svarūpa), the qualities of the Jinas, and the gradual loosening of karmic bondage. Later editors highlight three major strands in the work: (1) verses embedded in or related to the famous \"namutthunam\" and other devavandana hymns; (2) descriptions of inner practices required at different stages of spiritual maturation; and (3) analysis of how to avoid unwholesome meditation and cultivate wholesome forms. [[0]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Dhyan_Shatak_Romanized/022141) [[1]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Dhyanhatak_Tatha_Dhyanstava_Romanized/032155) In this way, Dhyanashataka functions as both a contemplative map and a diagnostic tool for the practitioner.\n\nIn contemporary Jain study, Dhyanashataka is used in advanced scriptural classes, meditation retreats, and scholarly research on Jain yoga. Critical editions with Hindi and Gujarati translations, appendices, and comparative notes make the text accessible to lay readers while preserving the technical precision of Haribhadra’s commentary. [[2]](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.346143) [[1]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Dhyanhatak_Tatha_Dhyanstava_Romanized/032155) Although it is primarily rooted in the Svetambara Murtipujaka milieu, its themes—such as the centrality of self-awareness, the critique of external dependence for happiness, and the insistence that liberation (siddhi) arises from pure meditation grounded in the three jewels—resonate across Jain traditions and invite comparative dialogue with other Indian contemplative systems.\n\nBecause Dhyanashataka is a scriptural and philosophical text rather than a modern meditation manual, its guidance is best approached under the supervision of qualified teachers within a living lineage. It offers conceptual clarity and devotional inspiration, but practical application—especially for intensive meditation or ascetic practices—requires attention to health, family responsibilities, and community norms, and should be coordinated with experienced human guides rather than treated as a stand-alone instruction book.",

  "overview": "Traditional taxonomy in Jain thought and modern biological taxonomy both attempt to make sense of the immense diversity of living beings, but they arise from very different aims, methods, and metaphysical commitments. In Jainism, classification is embedded within a soteriological and ethical framework: knowing what is living, how many senses it possesses, and what kind of body it has is essential for practicing ahimsa (non‑violence) and understanding the bondage of jiva (soul) by karma. Modern biology, by contrast, classifies organisms primarily to reflect evolutionary relationships, structural similarities, and genetic lineages, with an emphasis on empirical observation and testable hypotheses.\n\nClassical Jain texts such as the Tattvartha Sutra describe living beings through several intersecting schemes. One major scheme divides jivas into immobile (sthavara) and mobile (trasa) beings, and then further classifies them by the number of sense organs: one‑sensed, two‑sensed, three‑sensed, four‑sensed, and five‑sensed. This sensory taxonomy is closely tied to ethical gradation, because harming a being with more developed senses is considered more serious violence. Another scheme, found in later doctrinal elaborations, groups all living beings into six nikayas (earth‑bodied, water‑bodied, fire‑bodied, air‑bodied, plant‑bodied, and mobile beings). These categories extend life to entities that modern biology typically treats as inanimate, such as earth and water, because Jain ontology regards them as bodies inhabited by jivas with latent or minimal sensory capacity.\n\nModern biological taxonomy, especially since Linnaeus and later evolutionary theory, organizes life into hierarchical ranks such as domain, kingdom, phylum, class, order, family, genus, and species. The criteria are morphological traits, physiological processes, ecological roles, and—most decisively in contemporary practice—genetic relatedness. Binomial nomenclature gives each species a unique Latinized name, and phylogenetic trees attempt to reconstruct common ancestry. In this framework, life is generally restricted to organisms that meet criteria like metabolism, reproduction, and responsiveness, and non‑living matter (rocks, water, air) is excluded from the domain of living beings.\n\nWhen we compare Jain traditional taxonomy with modern biology, several convergences and divergences emerge. Scholarship that studies biology in the Tattvartha Sutra notes that Jain classification based on senses, mobility, and presence of mind can be mapped, in broad strokes, onto certain biological branches, even though the Jain system also includes volitional and spiritual dimensions that biology does not measure. Modern biology offers finer structural and genetic resolution and a globally standardized nomenclature, while Jain taxonomy offers a richer ethical and experiential description of beings, emphasizing their capacity for suffering and spiritual progress. Both systems recognize a broad distinction between plants and animals, but Jainism further insists on the living status of elemental bodies and micro‑organisms (nigoda), extending moral concern to realms that biology might treat as marginal or non‑living.\n\nFrom a Jain perspective, traditional taxonomy is not merely descriptive; it is prescriptive and contemplative. Knowing that a clod of earth or a drop of water is earth‑bodied or water‑bodied jiva shapes how a layperson or monk handles everyday activities, from walking and cooking to agriculture and industry. Modern biology, while increasingly engaged with environmental ethics, does not itself prescribe non‑violence; it provides data and models that can inform ethical reflection. Comparative studies and modern Jain thinkers often argue that bringing these two taxonomies into dialogue can deepen ecological awareness: biological science clarifies the mechanisms and interdependence of life, while Jain taxonomy reminds us that every form of life—however subtle—has intrinsic value and a claim to compassion.\n\nIt is important to note areas of disagreement and uncertainty. Jain claims about the exact number of species (often expressed as 8.4 million) and the living status of elemental bodies are rooted in scriptural authority and omniscience doctrines, not in empirical counting or laboratory verification. Modern biology, conversely, continues to revise its estimates of species diversity and its classification schemes as new data emerge. Translation of technical Jain terms like jiva, nikaya, and indriya into modern scientific language is approximate and interpretive, not exact. For detailed doctrinal guidance or for decisions that affect health, livelihood, or family responsibilities, a qualified human teacher or scholar within a living Jain lineage should be consulted, because neither traditional taxonomy nor biology alone can provide binding practical rulings.",

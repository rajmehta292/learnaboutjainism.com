  "overview": "In Jain literary and pedagogical history, \"thirty-two-text lists\" (often expressed in Sanskrit and Prakrit as dvātriṃśikā or battīs-grantha traditions) refer to structured collections of thirty-two scriptures or doctrinal topics that are grouped together for memorization, curriculum design, and sectarian self-definition. The number thirty-two functions as a mnemonic frame: it is large enough to encompass a broad canon or set of practices, yet finite and easily recitable in oral teaching.\n\nWithin Śvetāmbara Jainism—especially in Sthānakavāsī and Terāpanth lineages—the most prominent example is the list of \"32 Āgam\" (battīs āgam), a canonical set of scriptures accepted as authoritative. This list aggregates eleven Aṅga Āgams, twelve Upāṅga Āgams, four Cheda-sūtras, four Mūla-sūtras, and one Āvaśyaka-sūtra, yielding a total of thirty-two texts. The list is not itself a single scripture but a classificatory schema that shapes how monks, nuns, and laypeople understand the structure of revelation and commentary in their tradition. [[0]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/19436/what-are-the-names-of-the-32-aagam-wht-do-they-consist-of-)\n\nBeyond the 32 Āgam schema, Jain authors have also composed dvātriṃśikā-style works—texts that explicitly organize thirty-two virtues, contemplations, or narratives. An example from the Digambara scholastic milieu is Amitagati’s Bhāvanādvātriṃśatikā, which arranges thirty-two contemplations (bhāvanā) for lay practice and ethical reflection. Such dvātriṃśikā compositions parallel broader Indian literary habits, where thirty-two-tale or thirty-two-name collections (as seen in the non-Jain Siṃhāsana Dvātriṃśikā cycle) provide a didactic and aesthetic framework. [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Singhasan_Battisi) [[2]](https://encyclopediaofjainism.com/32-%E0%A4%B8%E0%A4%A8%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%A6%E0%A4%B0%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%AD_%E0%A4%97%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%B0%E0%A4%A8%E0%A5%8D%E0%A4%A5_%E0%A4%B8%E0%A5%82%E0%A4%9A%E0%A5%80/)\n\nIn Jain usage, thirty-two-text lists serve several functions. First, they stabilize sectarian canons: by fixing the count at thirty-two, communities can clearly state which scriptures they recognize, and how they differ from other Jain groups that count forty-five or more Āgams. Second, they support graded study: novices learn the broad categories (Aṅga, Upāṅga, Cheda, Mūla, Āvaśyaka), while advanced students delve into the content of each text. Third, they embody a pedagogical ideal in which doctrinal breadth is balanced with memorization and recitation, reflecting the oral-aural culture in which Jain scriptures were transmitted.\n\nComparatively, thirty-two-text lists also highlight intra-Jain diversity. Śvetāmbara Mūrti-pūjaka traditions often speak of forty-five Āgams, counting additional Cheda-sūtras and other texts, whereas Sthānakavāsī and Terāpanth communities emphasize the thirty-two-text list as their canonical boundary. Digambara traditions, which do not accept the Śvetāmbara Āgam corpus as preserved revelation, nonetheless employ dvātriṃśikā structures in ethical and contemplative literature. Thus, \"thirty-two-text lists\" are not a single pan-Jain canon but a family of enumerative strategies that different communities adapt to their doctrinal and historical circumstances.",

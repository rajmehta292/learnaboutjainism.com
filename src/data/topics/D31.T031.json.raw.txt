Jain traditions have long recognized that language change is inevitable, yet they also insist that the liberating knowledge encoded in scriptures must be preserved with great precision. Early canonical materials of the Śvetāmbara tradition were transmitted in Ardhamāgadhī Prakrit, while major scholastic syntheses such as the Tattvārtha Sūtra were composed in Sanskrit; later, these works were rendered into other Indic vernaculars (especially Gujarati, Hindi, Kannada) and, more recently, into English and other global languages. [[0]](https://archania.org/p/the-symbolic-world/symbolic-works/literature/religious-texts/jain-agamas) [[1]](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315443485-29/translating-sacred-scriptures-nalini-balbir)\n\nFrom a Jain perspective, translation is embedded in the exegetical tradition rather than treated as a purely modern, secular activity. Commentaries (ṭīkās, bhāṣyas, vṛttis) often function as de facto translations: they gloss difficult Prakrit forms, supply Sanskrit equivalents, and then paraphrase the sense in a more accessible idiom. Nalini Balbir’s study of the Śvetāmbara Jain tradition highlights how canonical passages were progressively rendered from Ardhamāgadhī into Sanskrit and then into vernaculars, using techniques such as word-by-word analysis, syntactic reordering, and explanatory paraphrase. This layered practice blurs the boundary between translation and interpretation, making it essential for modern readers to distinguish where a text ends and commentary begins. [[1]](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315443485-29/translating-sacred-scriptures-nalini-balbir)\n\nTechnical vocabulary poses a central challenge. Terms like jīva (living soul), ajīva (non-soul), pudgala (matter), āsrava (influx of karma), bandha (bondage), saṃvara (stoppage), nirjarā (shedding), mokṣa (liberation), and guṇasthāna (stages of spiritual development) carry precise meanings within Jain metaphysics and ethics that do not map neatly onto common English words. Translators must decide whether to retain the original terms, risking opacity but preserving doctrinal accuracy, or to use approximate equivalents that may subtly distort the system. The Tattvārtha Sūtra, for example, is extremely concise and technical; a strictly literal translation can appear cryptic, while a more expansive rendering risks sliding into commentary and sectarian interpretation. [[2]](https://noblechatter.com/faq/6709/tattvartha-sutra/what-challenges-arise-when-translating-the-tattvartha-sutra-into-modern-languages) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/tattvartha_sutra_jainagam_samanvay_002427_002427)\n\nSectarian diversity further complicates translation. Śvetāmbara communities treat the Prakrit Agamas as canonical scripture, whereas Digambara communities reject their full authenticity and instead privilege other early and scholastic works, including their own recensions of the Tattvārtha Sūtra and texts such as the Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama. A translation that follows a particular manuscript tradition or commentary may implicitly align with one sect’s doctrinal reading. Modern translators therefore often signal their sources, note variant readings, and indicate where Digambara and Śvetāmbara interpretations diverge, especially on issues such as the status of the Agamas, details of monastic discipline, and nuances of karma theory. [[0]](https://archania.org/p/the-symbolic-world/symbolic-works/literature/religious-texts/jain-agamas) [[4]](https://philosophy.institute/religions-of-the-world/exploring-sacred-texts-jainism-guide/)\n\nJain attitudes toward translation are generally positive when the goal is to make teachings accessible without compromising their liberative function. At the same time, certain formulaic passages—especially mantras like the Navakāra-mantra (Namaskāra-mantra)—are treated with special caution. Because these are believed to possess ritual and spiritual efficacy in their original phonetic form, many teachers prefer that they be recited in Prakrit or Sanskrit, with translations used for understanding rather than substitution. This distinction between “translation for comprehension” and “original for practice” is an important feature of Jain translation ethics. [[1]](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315443485-29/translating-sacred-scriptures-nalini-balbir) [[4]](https://philosophy.institute/religions-of-the-world/exploring-sacred-texts-jainism-guide/)\n\nIn contemporary scholarship and community education, translations are indispensable for global engagement with Jainism. Peer-reviewed editions and annotated translations of major texts help preserve philological rigor, while community-based translations in modern languages support lay study, youth education, and comparative philosophy. Yet responsible use of translations requires awareness of their limits: every rendering involves choices about terminology, doctrinal emphasis, and cultural framing. For serious religious decisions or nuanced doctrinal questions, Jains are encouraged to consult qualified teachers, lineage-based scholars, or monastic authorities who can mediate between the translated text and the living tradition. In this way, translations serve as bridges rather than replacements for the original scriptural and commentarial worlds.",

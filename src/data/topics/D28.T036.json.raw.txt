  "overview": "Uttaradhyayana (Uttarādhyayana Sūtra, Uttarajjhayaṇa) is one of the most important canonical texts of the Śvetāmbara Jain tradition and is widely respected, cited, and studied across other Jain lineages as well. Classified as the first of the four Mūlasūtras in the Śvetāmbara canon, it belongs to the later section of the Siddhānta and is traditionally regarded as containing the final sermons of Lord Mahāvīra, delivered in the closing phase of his life. [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/uttaradhyayan_sutra_mul_path_022569_022569) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_30_mool_03_uttaradhyayana_sutra_uttarajjhayanani_terapanth_003582_003582)\n\nThe work consists of 36 chapters (adhyayanas or “lectures”), each composed in Ardhamāgadhī Prakrit and structured in metrical gāthās. These chapters combine practical guidance for monastic discipline with narrative exempla, doctrinal summaries, and reflections on karma, rebirth, and liberation. The text’s overarching intention, as noted by Hermann Jacobi and later scholarship, is to instruct a young monk in his principal duties, commend the ascetic life through precepts and stories, warn against negligence and false asceticism, and provide basic theoretical information on living beings, non-living substances, and karmic processes. [[2]](https://theosophylib.com/hgj/uttaradhyayana-sutra/) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/uttaradhyayan_sutra_ek_parishilan_004252_004252)\n\nThematically, the Uttaradhyayana ranges from detailed prescriptions of vinaya (humility and discipline), endurance of hardships (pariṣaha), carefulness in movement and speech (samiti), and the cultivation of detachment (vairāgya), to narrative chapters about exemplary figures such as Kapila, King Nami, Harikeśa, Samudrapāla, and Kesi. These narratives illustrate the fruits of right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct, as well as the dangers of pride, carelessness, and sensual attachment. Later chapters treat more technical topics, including the nature of karman, the doctrine of leśyā (coloration of the soul by passions), and classifications of living beings and non-living things, thereby linking ethical practice with Jain metaphysics. [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/agam_30_mool_03_uttaradhyayana_sutra_uttarajjhayanani_terapanth_003582_003582) [[2]](https://theosophylib.com/hgj/uttaradhyayana-sutra/) [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/uttaradhyayanani_022588_022588)\n\nHistorically, the precise authorship and date of compilation remain debated. Traditional accounts attribute the discourses to Mahāvīra, preserved and systematized by his immediate disciples and later redactors, while modern scholars suggest that the individual lectures likely arose from earlier oral and written traditions and were subsequently collected into a single canonical unit. Stylistic variation between chapters and the mixture of narrative and doctrinal material support the view of composite authorship, even as the text functions as a coherent pedagogical manual for ascetics. [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/uttaradhyayan_sutra_ek_parishilan_004252_004252) [[2]](https://theosophylib.com/hgj/uttaradhyayana-sutra/)\n\nWithin Jain practice, the Uttaradhyayana is recited and studied by monks, nuns, and lay followers, especially during the rainy-season retreat (cāturmāsa) and on auspicious occasions such as Dīpāvalī. Its verses are frequently memorized, commented upon, and used as a basis for sermons emphasizing non-violence, self-restraint, impermanence, and the rarity of human birth. Numerous traditional commentaries and modern studies—across Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka, Sthānakavāsī, and Terāpanth contexts—have explored its language, structure, and doctrinal significance, making it a central bridge between early canonical formulations and later Jain ethical reflection.

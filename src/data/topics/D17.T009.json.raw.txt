  "overview": "Chaityavandana (often written Chaitya‑vandan or Chaitya‑vandana) is a central daily worship ritual in the Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka Jain tradition, performed in or facing a Jina temple (chaitya). The term itself combines “chaitya” (a sacred place or temple, especially one housing a Jina image) and “vandana” (reverential bowing or worship). In practice, Chaityavandana is a structured liturgy of praise, confession, recollection, and resolve, oriented toward the Pañca‑Parameṣṭhī—the five supreme beings: Arihants, Siddhas, Ācāryas, Upādhyāyas, and Sādhus—and, more specifically, toward the twenty‑four Tīrthaṅkaras whose images are enshrined in temples.  [[0]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/6187/chaitya-vandan-vidhi-and-sutras) [[1]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/8461/chaitvandan-process-explained-in-english)\n\nWithin the daily rhythm of Śvetāmbara lay practice, Chaityavandana is closely linked with other core observances such as samayika (meditative equanimity) and pratikramana (ritual repentance). Many manuals and commentaries present Chaityavandana as a way to integrate these elements: the practitioner purifies body and mind, recites foundational sutras, performs kayotsarga (standing in motionless posture), remembers the Tīrthaṅkaras, confesses faults, and renews commitment to self‑restraint (samyama). The ritual is not merely a sequence of verses; it is a disciplined, embodied practice meant to cultivate humility, non‑attachment, and awareness of the Jina’s path.  [[0]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/6187/chaitya-vandan-vidhi-and-sutras) [[1]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/8461/chaitvandan-process-explained-in-english)\n\nA typical Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka Chaityavandana begins with shuddhi (purification)—bathing, wearing clean simple clothes, and sitting or standing facing the temple or Jina image. The practitioner then recites the Navkār (Namaskāra) Mantra multiple times, bows to the Pañca‑Parameṣṭhī, and may perform kayotsarga while reciting texts such as the Logassa Sutra, which enumerates and venerates the twenty‑four Tīrthaṅkaras. Subsequent portions include verses like Jagachintāmaṇi Chaityavandana, Khamāsamana (asking forgiveness), Cattāri Maṅgalaṃ (four auspicious beings), and Tassa Uttari (further confession and resolve), among others. Each sutra has a traditional arth (interpretive meaning) that links the poetic Prakrit or Sanskrit wording to ethical and spiritual themes—renunciation, compassion, right faith, and the aspiration for liberation.  [[0]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/6187/chaitya-vandan-vidhi-and-sutras) [[2]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/7574/jain-shwetamber-chaitya-vandana-and-complete-meaning)\n\nHistorically, the ritual has been systematized and explained in manuals and commentaries such as “Chaityavandan Vidhi” and “Chaityavandanādi Bhāṣya Trayam,” which break down the liturgy into steps or “doors” (dvāras) and provide detailed guidance on posture, sequence, and meaning. These works reflect a broader Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka concern to make temple worship both doctrinally grounded and practically accessible: laypeople are taught not only how to recite the sutras but also how to understand their doctrinal content—karma theory, the nature of the soul, the qualities of Arihants and Siddhas, and the importance of right conduct. Sthānakavāsī and Terāpanthī Śvetāmbara communities, which emphasize non‑image worship and more internalized forms of devotion, may adapt or replace temple‑oriented liturgies with other forms of vandan and samayika. Digambara traditions have their own temple and image‑related rituals, but the term “Chaityavandana” in the sense of the standard Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka liturgy usually refers to the specific sequence of sutras and actions described above. In all cases, however, the underlying aims—reverence for the Jina, confession of faults, and strengthening of ethical restraint—remain shared Jain values.\n\nIn contemporary practice, Chaityavandana continues to function as a bridge between doctrinal study and lived devotion. For many youth and families, learning the Chaityavandana sutras, their pronunciation, and their meanings is a key part of religious education. Modern guides often provide transliteration and translation, helping those who do not know Prakrit or Sanskrit to connect with the ritual’s ethical and spiritual message. At the same time, traditional teachers emphasize that the ritual’s true fruit lies not in mere recitation but in the transformation of conduct—reducing violence, cultivating truthfulness, and moving gradually toward the ideals embodied by the Tīrthaṅkaras.\n",

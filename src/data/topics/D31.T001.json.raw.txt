  "overview": "Tattvārthasūtra (often called Tattvartha Sutra, Tattvarthādhigama-sūtra, or Mokṣa-śāstra) is one of the most authoritative and systematizing works of Jain philosophy, composed in Sanskrit between roughly the 2nd and 5th centuries CE and traditionally attributed to Ācārya Umāsvāti/Umāsvāmi. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tattvartha_Sutra) It is unique in being accepted as canonical and foundational by both major Jain traditions—Digambara and Śvetāmbara—as well as their sub‑traditions, and is often described as an epitome of Jainism because it gathers scattered doctrinal material into a concise, aphoristic framework.\n\nThe title itself signals its scope: “tattva” (true realities), “artha” (their meaning or nature), and “sūtra” (brief aphoristic statements). The work is therefore an organized presentation of the “true nature of realities” as understood in Jain thought. [[1]](https://jainworld.com/education/jain-education-material/senior-level/introduction-to-tattvartha-sutra/) In the broader landscape of Indian philosophy, its role is frequently compared to texts such as the Brahma Sūtras or Patañjali’s Yoga Sūtras, in that it offers a compact, systematic account of a whole darśana (philosophical system) in sutra form. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tattvartha_Sutra) The text is not part of the early Prakrit āgamas, yet over time it has come to be treated by many Jains as the most authoritative single manual of doctrine, recited in temples, studied in monastic and lay curricula, and commented upon in numerous languages.\n\nTattvārthasūtra is typically divided into ten chapters comprising around 344–357 sutras, depending on the recension (Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions differ slightly in sutra counts and arrangement). [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/tattvartha_sutra_aur_uski_tikaye_211095_211095) [[1]](https://jainworld.com/education/jain-education-material/senior-level/introduction-to-tattvartha-sutra/) The chapters move from epistemology and the path to liberation to cosmology, ontology, and ethics:\n1. Faith and Knowledge (samyag‑darśana and jñāna) and the definition of the path to liberation.\n2. The Category of the Living (jīva‑tattva), analyzing the soul, its attributes, and states.\n3. The Lower and Middle Worlds (adholoka and madhyaloka), describing hells and the human realm.\n4. The Celestial Beings (ūrdhvaloka), detailing heavenly abodes and deities.\n5. The Category of the Non‑Living (ajīva‑tattva), including matter, motion, rest, space, and time.\n6. Influx of Karma (āsrava), explaining how karmic particles enter and bind to the soul.\n7. Bondage of Karma (bandha), classifying karmas and their modes of bondage.\n8. The Five Vows and related conduct, outlining ethical disciplines.\n9. Stoppage and Shedding of Karma (saṃvara and nirjarā).\n10. Liberation (mokṣa), describing the fully purified, liberated soul. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tattvartha_Sutra) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/tattvartha_sutra_001632_001632)\n\nThe first sutra—“samyag‑darśana‑jñāna‑cāritrāṇi mokṣamārgaḥ”—encapsulates the Jain path to liberation as the union of Right Faith, Right Knowledge, and Right Conduct (the ratnatraya, or three jewels). [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tattvartha_Sutra) [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/mokshshastra_001795_001795) Subsequent sutras elaborate the nature and types of knowledge (including sensory, scriptural, clairvoyant, telepathic, and omniscient), the structure of the universe, the classification of beings, and the intricate doctrine of karma and its transformation. The famous sutra “parasparopagraho jīvānām” (“living beings are mutually supportive”) has become the official motto of Jainism, often cited to express the ethic of interdependence and non‑harm. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Tattvartha_Sutra)\n\nHistorically, Tattvārthasūtra marks a significant linguistic and intellectual transition. Earlier Jain scriptures were predominantly in Ardhamāgadhī Prakrit, the vernacular of the time, but as Sanskrit became the language of elite philosophical discourse, Jain scholars began composing in Sanskrit to participate in wider intellectual debates. Tattvārthasūtra is widely regarded as the first major Jain text in Sanskrit, and its sutra style parallels other classical Indian systems, helping to position Jain thought within the broader philosophical milieu. [[1]](https://jainworld.com/education/jain-education-material/senior-level/introduction-to-tattvartha-sutra/) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/tattvartha_sutra_aur_uski_tikaye_211095_211095) Over the centuries, it has attracted a rich tradition of commentaries—such as the Digambara Sarvārthasiddhi and Śvetāmbara Tattvārthādhigama‑bhāṣya—each elaborating, interpreting, and sometimes debating the precise meaning of its terse aphorisms.\n\nFrom a doctrinal standpoint, Tattvārthasūtra is central to technical discussions of tattvas (fundamental realities), karma theory, and the stages of spiritual development (guṇasthānas). It provides a framework for understanding how right cognition and ethical discipline gradually transform the soul’s karmic condition, culminating in kevala‑jñāna (omniscience) and final liberation. Because of its brevity and density, the text requires careful study with qualified teachers and commentarial guidance; its sutras often presuppose earlier scriptural material and traditional hermeneutics. Modern scholarship and community education continue to engage with Tattvārthasūtra, exploring its philosophical rigor, its ethical implications (especially the motto of mutual support among living beings), and its role in shaping both monastic and lay Jain practice.\n\nWhile the core doctrinal content is widely shared across Jain traditions, there remain differences in textual recension, interpretive emphasis, and commentarial lineage between Digambara and Śvetāmbara communities. Responsible study therefore pays attention to these variations, distinguishes between the primary sutras and later interpretations, and, for matters of practice or initiation, consults living authorities within the relevant lineage rather than treating the text—or any summary of it—as a standalone source of binding religious rulings.",

  "overview": "Reception history, in the context of Jain studies, refers to the ways in which Jain texts, doctrines, practices, and communities have been understood, appropriated, debated, and re‑presented over time by different audiences—Jain and non‑Jain, South Asian and global, pre‑modern and modern. It shifts attention from a static description of “what Jainism is” to a dynamic inquiry into how Jainism has been perceived, narrated, and reshaped across centuries. This includes the internal reception of teachings within Jain lineages and sects, as well as the external reception of Jainism in colonial scholarship, comparative religion, philosophy, and contemporary public discourse.\n\nWithin Jain traditions themselves, reception history can be traced in the ways canonical and post‑canonical texts are cited, commented upon, ritualized, and selectively emphasized. Digambara and Śvetāmbara communities, for example, have received and organized early teachings into distinct canons, with different attitudes toward textual preservation, oral transmission, and doctrinal authority. Over time, acharyas and commentators have re‑read foundational works such as the Tattvārthasūtra, Nāyādhammakahāo, and sectarian narrative collections, producing layered interpretive histories. These internal receptions are visible in commentarial chains, sectarian debates over issues like image‑worship, nudity, and scriptural authenticity, and the evolving pedagogical literature used in pathśālās and community education.\n\nExternally, reception history examines how Jainism has been portrayed by non‑Jain observers—Indologists, colonial administrators, missionaries, anthropologists, philosophers, and, more recently, global media and diaspora communities. Early European and colonial accounts often framed Jainism through the lens of “Indian sects,” emphasizing asceticism, non‑violence, and mercantile ethics, but frequently flattening historical complexity and sectarian diversity. Nineteenth‑ and early twentieth‑century textual scholarship focused heavily on ancient Prakrit canons and doctrinal summaries, presenting Jainism as a largely timeless system of ideas. Later anthropological and historical work, especially from the 1980s onward, began to highlight Jain communities as historically situated, socially embedded, and internally diverse, thereby transforming the reception of Jainism in academic discourse from a static doctrinal system to a living, changing tradition.  [[0]](https://jainastudies.soas.ac.uk/ijjs/ijjs-2202-2026.pdf)\n\nReception history also considers the colonial and post‑colonial period, during which Jain communities interacted with British rule, global trade, legal reforms, and missionary critique. Jains contributed to and contested emerging representations of their tradition, commissioning publications, sponsoring scholars, and engaging in public debates about non‑violence, animal protection, and religious identity. Recent scholarship has mined archival materials to show how Jains navigated colonial categories of “religion,” “community,” and “minority,” and how these encounters reshaped both internal self‑understanding and external portrayals.  [[1]](https://www.soas.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2022-06/The%20Jains%20in%20the%20Colonial%20World%20A%20Review%20IJJS%20Vol%2017%20No2%202021%201-13%20file156570.pdf)\n\nIn the contemporary era, reception history extends to modern Jain leaders, reform movements, and diaspora communities, whose teachings and institutional initiatives have reframed Jainism in relation to global ethics, environmentalism, interfaith dialogue, and modern education. Works that present “modern Jainism” as a historical phenomenon emphasize how ascetic leaders, lay organizations, and new media have reinterpreted classical doctrines for current concerns, thereby generating fresh receptions of concepts like ahiṃsā, anekāntavāda, and aparigraha.  [[2]](https://books.google.com/books/about/Modern_Jainism.html?id=e1LKEAAAQBAJ) [[3]](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/978-981-99-2485-1.pdf)\n\nMethodologically, reception history requires careful distinction between primary texts, traditional commentaries, and modern scholarship, as well as attention to translation choices, editorial decisions, and the social locations of interpreters. It highlights disagreements among sources, gaps in evidence, and the ways in which power, language, and historical context shape what is remembered or forgotten. For Jainism, this means recognizing that our picture of the tradition is always mediated—by canonical redactors, medieval commentators, colonial scholars, modern academics, and contemporary Jain voices—and that studying these mediations is itself a crucial part of understanding the tradition. Because reception history touches on identity, authority, and lived practice, detailed questions about specific lineages, vows, or life‑changing decisions should be discussed with qualified Jain teachers or scholars rooted in a living tradition, rather than treated as purely textual or historical matters.",

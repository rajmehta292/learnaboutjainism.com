  "overview": "In textual criticism, a \"stemma\" (more fully \"stemma codicum\") is a diagram that represents the hypothesized genealogical relationships among the surviving witnesses—usually manuscripts—of a given work.\n\nThe term derives from the Greek word \"στέμμα\" meaning \"wreath\" or \"garland,\" which in Latin antiquity was extended metaphorically to denote a genealogical tree. In modern scholarly usage, a stemma codicum is the visual expression of a critical editor’s reconstruction of how manuscripts are related through copying, loss, and contamination, ultimately tracing them back to a common ancestor or archetype. [[0]](https://wiki.helsinki.fi/xwiki/bin/view/stemmatology/Stemma/)\n\nFormally, a stemma is often described as an oriented, tree‑like graph, more precisely a directed acyclic graph (DAG). Each node represents either an extant manuscript or a hypothesized lost exemplar, while edges indicate lines of descent—copying events—from one witness to another. The orientation of the graph implies a root, called the archetype, from which all other witnesses descend. Intermediate nodes, sometimes called hyparchetypes, stand for lost manuscripts that must be posited to explain shared readings and errors among groups of surviving witnesses. Contamination—where a scribe uses more than one exemplar—appears in the diagram as additional, often dotted, edges that break the strict tree structure but still preserve acyclicity. [[0]](https://wiki.helsinki.fi/xwiki/bin/view/stemmatology/Stemma/) [[1]](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-03260086/document)\n\nHistorically, the idea of representing manuscript relationships as a genealogical tree emerged in the eighteenth century, when Johann Albrecht Bengel proposed a \"tabula quaedam quasi genealogica\" for New Testament witnesses. In the nineteenth century, scholars such as Carl Johan Schlyter and Carl Gottlob Zumpt began to publish explicit stemmata in their editions, and the method was systematized by Karl Lachmann, whose work on the New Testament and classical texts laid the foundations of modern stemmatics. Paul Maas’s \"Textkritik\" (1927) gave the classic concise formulation of the method, and the term \"stemma codicum\" became standard in textual criticism. [[0]](https://wiki.helsinki.fi/xwiki/bin/view/stemmatology/Stemma/) [[2]](https://universitas-scholarium.org/scholar/manuscript-tradition)\n\nMethodologically, constructing a stemma relies on the \"common errors\" principle: if two manuscripts share a distinctive, non‑trivial error, it is highly unlikely that they produced it independently; instead, they are presumed to descend from a common ancestor that already contained that error. By systematically identifying such shared errors and patterns of agreement and disagreement, the editor groups manuscripts into families and reconstructs the branching structure of the tradition. Once the stemma is established, it guides the reconstruction of the archetype: readings supported by independent branches of the tradition, especially where they avoid obvious secondary features, are preferred as closer to the original text. [[1]](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-03260086/document) [[3]](https://www.britannica.com/science/stemma-codicum)\n\nIn practice, stemmata serve several purposes. First, they provide a transparent, falsifiable model of the transmission history of a text, allowing other scholars to critique or refine the editor’s hypotheses. Second, they help identify redundant witnesses—manuscripts that merely repeat the text of others—and thus focus editorial effort on those witnesses that contribute independent information. Third, they illuminate the cultural and historical pathways through which a text traveled: clusters of manuscripts may correspond to particular regions, scriptoria, religious communities, or periods, and contamination can reveal moments of cross‑regional contact or editorial intervention. [[3]](https://www.britannica.com/science/stemma-codicum) [[2]](https://universitas-scholarium.org/scholar/manuscript-tradition)\n\nIn the digital age, stemmata have become objects of computational analysis and shared scholarly infrastructure. Projects such as \"Open Stemmata\" collect and publish stemmata for many traditions, treating them as data that can be queried, compared, and visualized across corpora. Computational phylogenetic methods, adapted from evolutionary biology, are increasingly used to infer stemmata from large sets of variant readings, especially when the number of witnesses is too great for purely manual analysis. These approaches do not replace traditional philological judgment but complement it, offering probabilistic models that can be checked against codicological, paleographical, and historical evidence. [[1]](https://shs.hal.science/halshs-03260086/document) [[2]](https://universitas-scholarium.org/scholar/manuscript-tradition)\n\nFor traditions such as Jain, Buddhist, or other pre‑modern religious literatures, stemmata are particularly important because many foundational texts survive only in manuscript form, often with complex regional and sectarian transmission histories. A carefully constructed stemma can help distinguish doctrinally motivated changes from ordinary scribal errors, trace how specific recensions became authoritative in different communities, and support critical editions that respect both the historical plurality of the tradition and the need for a usable base text. At the same time, stemmata remain hypotheses: they are revised as new manuscripts are discovered, as readings are reassessed, or as alternative models of textual transmission—such as those emphasizing fluidity and variance—are brought into dialogue with classical stemmatics.

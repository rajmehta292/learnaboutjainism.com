  "overview": "In Jain narrative tradition, Sagara is celebrated as one of the great Cakravartins (universal monarchs) of the current avasarpinī (descending time-cycle). He appears most prominently in Hemacandra’s \"Trishashti Shalaka Purusha Caritra\" as a paradigmatic world-conqueror whose political power is ultimately subordinated to the spiritual authority of a Tirthankara, specifically Ajitanatha, the second Jina of the present era.\n\nAs a Cakravartin, Sagara is depicted as possessing the cakra-ratna (discus jewel) and other royal insignia that mark his status as a universal ruler. Hemacandra’s account describes his coronation, the gathering of subordinate kings, and the elaborate rituals that affirm his sovereignty over Bharata-varṣa (the human world-region central to Jain cosmology). These conquests are narrated in detail—campaigns across various districts, the subjugation of rival rulers, and the orderly administration of his vast realm—yet the text consistently frames these achievements within the larger Jain vision of karmic bondage and impermanence. [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc212632.html) [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc212610.html)\n\nA crucial turning point in Sagara’s story occurs when he learns that Lord Ajitanatha is preaching in the samavasaraṇa, the specially constructed preaching hall of a Tirthankara. The narrative emphasizes that Sagara’s joy at hearing of the Jina’s presence surpasses even his delight in the appearance of his own cakra jewel, underscoring the hierarchy of spiritual over temporal power. He visits the samavasaraṇa with great reverence, offers immense gifts, and listens to the Jina’s discourse, which deepens his understanding of dharma and the path to liberation. [[2]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc212616.html)\n\nLater episodes portray Sagara’s continued campaigns—such as the conquest of the southern districts of the Sindhu and the passage through the formidable region of Tamisrā—but these are increasingly framed as duties carried out by a ruler who has recognized the superiority of spiritual insight. The text uses his life to illustrate how even the most powerful monarch remains subject to karma and must ultimately turn toward renunciation, right faith, and non-violence if he wishes to progress through the gunasthānas (stages of spiritual development). While different recensions and retellings vary in the exact details of his later spiritual status, the overarching theme is clear: Sagara’s worldly glory is meaningful only insofar as it becomes a vehicle for supporting Jain dharma and for his own inner transformation. [[3]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc212626.html) [[4]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc212633.html)\n\nIn comparative perspective, Sagara’s figure also illustrates how Jainism reinterprets the ideal of universal kingship. Unlike purely heroic or martial portrayals found in some other Indian traditions, the Jain Cakravartin is judged by his adherence to ahiṃsā (non-violence), his respect for ascetics, and his eventual recognition that spiritual liberation surpasses all worldly sovereignty. Sagara thus stands as both a political archetype and a moral exemplar: a ruler whose story warns against attachment to power while affirming the possibility of ethical kingship aligned with the teachings of the Jinas.",

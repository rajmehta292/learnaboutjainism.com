  "overview": "In Jain studies, \"authorship uncertainty\" refers to the complex and often contested questions about who composed, redacted, or transmitted a given text, and in which historical layers this happened. Because Jain literature developed over many centuries in multiple languages—primarily Ardhamāgadhī Prakrit, Jaina Śaurasenī, and Sanskrit—within both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions, the attribution of texts to named authors or acharyas is frequently a matter of later tradition rather than explicit, contemporaneous colophons. Scholarly work and internal commentarial traditions together reveal that many canonical and post‑canonical works are composite, layered, and sometimes anonymous, with authorial names attached only in later reception.\n\nAuthorship uncertainty arises from several overlapping factors. First, many early Jain scriptures, especially within the Śvetāmbara canon, lack explicit authorial statements; they present themselves as records of Mahāvīra’s teaching transmitted through gaṇadharas and later acharyas, but without clear individual authorship claims. Second, manuscript culture—copying, recasting, and combining texts into multiple‑text manuscripts—encouraged redaction and interpolation, making it difficult to distinguish an original core from later additions. Third, sectarian and lineage narratives sometimes retroactively assign revered names (such as Bhadrabāhu or Kundakunda) to texts that circulated anonymously for centuries, creating tension between traditional attributions and philological evidence.\n\nA prominent example is the corpus attributed to Kundakunda in the Digambara tradition. Modern scholarship notes that texts like Samayasāra, Pañcāstikāyasāra, and Pravacanasāra show signs of multiple authorship, interpolations, and stratified composition, with layers dating from roughly the 4th to as late as the 11th–12th centuries CE. The figure \"Kundakunda\" appears to have become a focal authorial persona only when later commentators, such as Jayasena on the Pañcāstikāya, explicitly named an author and constructed a biographical narrative. Before that, these works functioned as authoritative doctrinal compilations without a clearly identified composer. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Kundakunda)\n\nWithin the Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka tradition, the Kalpa Sūtra illustrates another dimension of authorship uncertainty. The text has been ritually central for over a millennium, especially during Paryuṣaṇa, yet its precise date of composition, redaction history, and authorial identity remain debated. Manuscript and ritual studies show that the Kalpa Sūtra was carefully shaped over time as a charter document for community identity, with different sections (Mahāvīra’s life, other Tīrthaṅkara biographies, monastic rules) possibly stemming from distinct textual strata. Scholarly reconstructions emphasize the roles of redactors, patrons, and ritual specialists rather than a single author, highlighting how Jain communities themselves negotiated textual authority through copying, illustrating, and performing the scripture. [[1]](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/b5e8df6c-a6f0-44ea-9073-44d910f6952e/full) [[2]](https://truth.parallax.kr/s/kalpa-sutra/academic)\n\nAuthorship uncertainty also appears in debates over the Chheda‑sūtras and their Niyuktis (early commentaries). Traditional accounts often attribute the Chheda‑sūtras to the fourteenth Purvadhara Bhadrabāhu, yet close reading of Niyukti and related commentarial material reveals anachronisms and references to later acharyas, suggesting that at least some Niyuktis were composed by a different Bhadrabāhu or by multiple later scholars. The fact that a Niyuktikāra offers salutations to Bhadrabāhu, rather than speaking as Bhadrabāhu, further complicates the identification. This case shows how internal evidence—names, historical references, and self‑presentation—can challenge long‑standing communal attributions while still respecting the devotional significance of those attributions. [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/brihatkalpa_sutra_prastavik_230177_230177)\n\nFrom a methodological perspective, Jain authorship uncertainty is addressed through a combination of philology, manuscript studies, and reception history. Scholars compare language layers, doctrinal vocabulary, and intertextual references to estimate relative chronology and possible authorial hands. Manuscript catalogues and studies of multiple‑text manuscripts reveal how texts were grouped, copied, and modified, indicating editorial decisions that shape what later appears as a unified work. At the same time, traditional commentaries and sectarian histories are treated as crucial witnesses to how communities themselves understood and constructed authorship, even when those constructions do not align perfectly with modern historical criticism. [[4]](https://www.academia.edu/79859960/Functions_of_Multiple_Text_Manuscripts_in_India_The_Jain_Case) [[1]](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/b5e8df6c-a6f0-44ea-9073-44d910f6952e/full)\n\nFor Jain practice and theology, authorship uncertainty does not necessarily undermine scriptural authority. Many texts are revered primarily because they faithfully transmit right doctrine (samyag‑darśana, samyag‑jñāna, samyag‑cāritra) and support the path to liberation, regardless of whether a single historical author can be identified. The tradition often emphasizes lineage, transmission, and correctness of teaching over individual authorship. Still, acknowledging uncertainty helps modern readers avoid oversimplified narratives, appreciate the collaborative and cumulative nature of Jain textual production, and remain honest about the limits of historical knowledge. It also encourages respectful dialogue between traditional attributions and academic reconstructions, recognizing that both operate within different but overlapping frameworks of authority and meaning.",

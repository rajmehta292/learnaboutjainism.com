  "overview": "Bioethics, in general terms, is the systematic study of moral questions arising from the biological sciences, medicine, and technologies that affect birth, life, and death. Within Jain thought, bioethics is not a separate, modern invention but an extension of a very old concern: how embodied beings should act in a universe saturated with living entities, each on its own karmic journey and worthy of moral regard. Jainism frames questions about medical treatment, reproductive technologies, organ donation, animal research, and end‑of‑life care through its core commitments to nonviolence (ahiṃsā), restraint, and carefulness (apramatta), rather than through rights language or utilitarian cost–benefit calculations.\n\nAt the heart of Jain bioethical reflection is the conviction that life (jīva) is pervasive and graded, from one‑sensed beings such as plants and microorganisms to five‑sensed beings such as humans and larger animals. Every living being possesses consciousness and an aversion to pain, and every act of mind, speech, and body has karmic consequences. This ontology makes even routine medical interventions morally serious: taking antibiotics, performing surgery, or using disinfectants all involve harm to some forms of life, even when they aim to preserve other lives. Jain bioethics therefore does not promise a harm‑free path; instead, it seeks to cultivate the least violent, most careful course of action in a given context, acknowledging both direct and indirect forms of harm.\n\nHistorically, early canonical texts show a cautious, sometimes skeptical attitude toward medicine, especially for mendicants whose ideal is radical non‑attachment and endurance of bodily hardship. Over time, however, post‑canonical literature and community practice reveal a growing acceptance of medicine for both laypeople and ascetics, provided it is approached with restraint and awareness of its karmic implications. This evolution is guided by the tools of carefulness: attention to pervasive life forms, examination of one’s desires and motivations, analysis of how harm is produced, and commitment to reducing avoidable harm. Contemporary Jain medical professionals often navigate these tools when considering issues such as abortion, in vitro fertilization, stem‑cell research, organ transplantation, and palliative sedation, balancing professional obligations, legal frameworks, and Jain ethical ideals.\n\nIn reproductive ethics, Jain perspectives tend to emphasize the moral status of the embryo or fetus as a living being whose destruction entails grave karmic consequences, while also recognizing complex circumstances such as maternal health, social pressures, and medical risk. Assisted reproductive technologies raise questions about the creation, selection, and disposal of embryos, prompting reflection on intention, attachment, and the multiplication of lives whose futures are uncertain. In animal research and biotechnology, the Jain commitment to ahiṃsā challenges practices that treat animals as mere instruments, urging alternatives that minimize suffering, reduce the number of beings harmed, or avoid such research when possible.\n\nEnd‑of‑life questions—such as life‑support withdrawal, pain management, and definitions of death—are filtered through Jain views of karma, consciousness, and the continuity of the soul. While the tradition values endurance and spiritual vigilance, it also recognizes the importance of reducing intense suffering and maintaining clarity of mind. Practices like terminal sedation, organ donation, or decisions about resuscitation are therefore evaluated in terms of intention, the degree and kind of harm involved, and their impact on the dying person’s capacity for spiritual awareness. The controversial ascetic practice of sallekhanā (gradual fasting at the end of life) is treated in classical sources as a highly regulated, rare vow undertaken with strict spiritual criteria, not as a general model for medical decision‑making.\n\nModern Jain bioethics, informed by classical texts and contemporary scholarship, does not offer a single, uniform answer to every medical dilemma. Instead, it provides a framework: recognize the ubiquity and diversity of life, cultivate nonviolent restraint, examine motivations, attend to both visible and invisible harms, and seek context‑sensitive ways to reduce avoidable violence. Within this framework, lay Jains are encouraged to consult qualified medical professionals, community leaders, and knowledgeable scholars when facing complex decisions, integrating Jain ethical insights with responsible participation in modern healthcare systems.",

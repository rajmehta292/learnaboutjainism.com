Jainism does not speak directly of robots, algorithms, or industrial machinery in its classical scriptures, yet its core doctrines—ahimsa (non‑violence), aparigraha (non‑possessiveness), anekantavada (many‑sidedness), and the detailed analysis of karma—offer a rigorous framework for evaluating how automated systems reshape work, ecology, and human character. The universe, according to Tattvartha Sutra, operates through intrinsic laws without a creator‑god, which means technological change is understood as part of the unfolding of natural and social causes rather than a divinely mandated destiny. For example, machines may spare humans from dangerous physical labor, but large‑scale industrial automation can intensify harm to lower life‑forms through pollution, resource extraction, and energy consumption. Contemporary Jain‑informed scholarship on technology and sustainability highlights how data centers, AI infrastructure, and automated logistics systems dramatically increase electricity demand and water usage, raising questions about collective responsibility for environmental violence against air‑bodied, water‑bodied, and earth‑bodied beings. [[2]](https://www.aijfr.com/papers/2026/4/6828.pdf) In Jain terms, the karmic burden of such systemic harm is not erased simply because the violence is indirect or mediated by machines; intention, complicity, and benefit from harmful systems all matter.\n\nAutomation also transforms labor and social relations. Jain lay ethics, especially as interpreted in modern community education, emphasize right livelihood (samyag ajiva) that avoids trades involving direct killing, deception, or exploitation. When factories, warehouses, and offices adopt automation, workers may face displacement, precarious employment, or intensified surveillance. These impacts are evaluated not only economically but morally: does automation promote the cultivation of equanimity, compassion, and self‑discipline, or does it encourage greed, competition, and disregard for vulnerable populations? Peer‑reviewed studies on Jain ethical thought in modern contexts note that rapid technological change often produces \"attitude‑behavior inconsistency,\" where professed values of non‑violence and restraint clash with participation in highly competitive, profit‑driven automated economies. [[3]](https://www.ijhssi.org/papers/vol15%282%29/1502168172.pdf)\n\nAnekantavada, the doctrine of many‑sidedness, cautions against simplistic judgments that automation is either wholly beneficial or wholly harmful. Instead, Jain analysis would distinguish multiple standpoints (nayas): from the standpoint of efficiency, automation can free time for study, meditation, and service; from the standpoint of attachment, it can deepen dependence on comfort, speed, and consumption; from the standpoint of ecological impact, it may either reduce waste through optimization or magnify it through scale. Systems‑theory readings of Jain metaphysics show how ahimsa can be extended to complex socio‑technical systems, treating automation as part of interdependent networks where entropy, exploitation, and compassion all co‑exist. [[4]](https://www.academia.edu/68621897/Ahimsa_Entropy_and_the_Problem_of_Evil_A_Systems_Theory_Analysis_of_Jaina_Metaphysics_in_the_Tattvarthasutra) This encourages practitioners to ask not only \"What does this machine do?\" but \"What patterns of harm and care does this automated system create across visible and invisible lives?\"\n\nIn contemporary Jain discourse, automation and artificial intelligence are increasingly discussed under the broader theme of sustainability and digital ethics. Scholars have begun dialogues that connect Jain vows—such as limiting possessions, mindful consumption, and truthfulness—to questions about data extraction, algorithmic bias, and the environmental footprint of automated infrastructures. [[2]](https://www.aijfr.com/papers/2026/4/6828.pdf) While no classical text can be cited as a direct rulebook for automation, the layered corpus (primary scriptures, commentaries, and modern scholarship) converges on a key insight: technological power must be governed by restraint and responsibility. Automation is acceptable, even welcome, when it reduces suffering, supports spiritual practice, and minimizes ecological harm; it becomes ethically problematic when it accelerates violence, inequality, and attachment. Because these judgments often involve complex trade‑offs and local conditions, serious decisions—such as large‑scale deployment of harmful technologies, or career changes that affect dependents—are best made in consultation with qualified human teachers, professionals, and community bodies rather than relying solely on abstract principles.",

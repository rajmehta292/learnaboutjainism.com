  "overview": "In Jain traditions, “manuscript records” refers not only to individual handwritten copies of scriptures and commentaries, but also to the systematic notes, catalogues, colophons, and archival practices through which communities have preserved their textual heritage over many centuries.\n\nFrom at least the early medieval period, Jain communities—both Digambara and Śvetāmbara—maintained bhaṇḍāras (scriptorium–libraries) where palm‑leaf and later paper manuscripts were copied, stored, and periodically inventoried. These bhaṇḍāras developed detailed cataloguing habits: scribes recorded titles, authors, sectarian affiliation, script, material (palm leaf or paper), and sometimes the donor or patron, creating a layered record that modern scholars now read as much for social history as for textual content. Descriptive catalogues of major bhaṇḍāras, such as those at Pattan and Cambay, continue this tradition in printed form, listing thousands of Jain works and documenting the physical state and provenance of the manuscripts.  [[0]](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.367307) [[1]](https://books.google.com/books/about/Catalogue_of_Palm_leaf_Manuscripts_in_th.html?id=M8Hn40WD38kC)\n\nPalm‑leaf manuscripts are especially significant in the history of Jain manuscript records. In western India, Śvetāmbara centers preserved large collections of palm‑leaf copies of canonical texts, narrative literature, and philosophical treatises, often richly illustrated and written in scripts such as Jaina Nāgarī. Catalogues of palm‑leaf manuscripts from the Śāntinātha Jain Bhaṇḍāra in Cambay, for example, show how titles, folio counts, and colophon data were systematically recorded, allowing later researchers to trace transmission lines and regional copying practices.  [[2]](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.495233) [[3]](https://archive.org/details/catalogueofpalmleafmanuscriptsintheshantinathjainbhandaracambaymunipunyavijayapart1gos135_478_m) In southern India, the Mudbidri bhaṇḍāra of the Digambara tradition preserves palm‑leaf manuscripts of foundational doctrinal works like the Śaṭkhaṇḍāgama and its related texts; these manuscripts include rare illustrations and detailed colophons that record copying dates, scribes, and ritual contexts, forming a crucial record of how Digambara communities safeguarded their scriptural corpus.  [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/mudbidrina_jain_bhandarna_prachin_tadpatriya_chitro_230206_230206)\n\nManuscript records also include the internal notes written by scribes and librarians. Colophons may mention the place of copying, the name of the copying monk or lay scribe, the donor who funded the work, and sometimes the circumstances of preservation (such as relocation after political upheaval). These notes are primary historical sources for reconstructing networks of Jain learning, trade routes that connected manuscript centers, and the evolution of sectarian identities. Modern printed catalogues, while secondary in form, often reproduce these colophons verbatim or in summary, thereby extending the manuscript record into contemporary scholarship.  [[0]](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.367307) [[1]](https://books.google.com/books/about/Catalogue_of_Palm_leaf_Manuscripts_in_th.html?id=M8Hn40WD38kC)\n\nToday, digitization projects and online repositories add another layer to Jain manuscript records. Scanned catalogues and manuscript images from institutions in India and abroad make it possible to cross‑reference older bhaṇḍāra lists with newly discovered items, track the movement of manuscripts into public and private collections, and identify gaps where texts are known only from references in catalogues but no longer physically survive. These digital records are valuable, but they must be used with care: OCR errors, incomplete metadata, and uncertain provenance mean that they should be treated as discovery tools rather than replacements for verified primary manuscripts and critical editions.

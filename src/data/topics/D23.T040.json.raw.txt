These sources are not merely historical records; they are didactic narratives designed to embody Jain doctrines of non‑violence, karma, renunciation, and liberation. As such, biography in the Jain tradition sits at the intersection of scripture, narrative epic, hagiography, and community memory.\n\nAt the most foundational level, early Śvetāmbara canonical texts in Ardhamāgadhī Prakrit—such as the Ācāranga‑sūtra, Kalpa‑sūtra, and Uttarādhyayana‑sūtra—preserve biographical material about Mahāvīra and other Tirthankaras alongside teachings on conduct and monastic discipline. The Kalpa‑sūtra, for example, contains the Jinacaritra section, which narrates the lives of Mahāvīra, Pārśva, Ariṣṭanemi, and Ṛṣabha, and offers shorter notices of the remaining Tirthankaras; it also includes the Sthavirāvalī, a lineage list of teachers, and the Sāmācārī, rules for the rainy‑season retreat. These epics integrate the lives of Tirthankaras and other illustrious beings into a vast cosmological and karmic framework, presenting biography as part of universal history. [[1]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/6068/books-on-jainism-from-start-history) They are not canonical in the same sense as the Śvetāmbara sūtras, but they function as authoritative narrative sources for Digambara communities and are widely studied across Jain traditions.\n\nA further stratum of biography sources consists of classical Sanskrit works like Hemacandra’s Triṣaṣṭi‑śalākā‑puruṣa‑caritra (“Lives of the Sixty‑Three Illustrious Persons”), which systematizes the careers of twenty‑four Tirthankaras along with Baladevas, Vāsudevas, Prativāsudevas, and Cakravartins. Hemacandra’s text draws on earlier Jain narrative materials and commentarial traditions, elaborating well‑known episodes and reincarnation cycles to create a comprehensive narrative theology of Jain exemplars. [[2]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc213206.html) Such works occupy a bridge position between scriptural biography and later hagiography, and they are crucial for understanding how Jain communities conceptualize ideal lives across cosmic time.\n\nBeyond these major texts, there is a wide range of regional and lineage‑specific hagiographies, charitras (biographical narratives), and kathā‑collections that recount the lives of particular Tirthankaras, ācāryas, monks, nuns, and lay devotees. Examples include Pārśva‑caritra narratives, Kālakācārya‑kathā, Śrīpāla‑kathā, and numerous vernacular works in Gujarati, Rajasthani, Hindi, and other languages. [[3]](https://jainpedia.org/manuscript/text-496275cf6f/) These sources often blend doctrinal teaching with local memory, temple histories, and miracle stories, and they play a significant role in shaping devotional practice and ethical imagination at the community level.\n\nModern scholarship and community education materials—such as historical syntheses by Natubhai Shah, social histories by Vilas Sangave, and studies of Jain art and iconography by John Cort and Pratapaditya Pal—provide another layer of biography sources. [[1]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/6068/books-on-jainism-from-start-history) While these are Tier C and D materials rather than primary scripture, they help situate traditional biographies within broader historical, social, and material contexts, and they often highlight how different Jain traditions (Śvetāmbara Mūrti‑pūjaka, Sthānakavāsī, Terāpanth, Digambara) remember and narrate the lives of the same figures with subtle variations.\n\nTaken together, Jain biography sources form a complex ecosystem: canonical sūtras, narrative purāṇas, epic caritras, regional hagiographies, and modern studies. For rigorous academic work, it is essential to distinguish between Tier A canonical texts, Tier B traditional commentaries, and later narrative or educational materials, and to note where traditions diverge or where historical evidence is thin. Because these biographies are primarily doctrinal and soteriological rather than critical historiography, they must be read with attention to genre, purpose, and sectarian context, and supplemented by qualified human scholarship when used for historical reconstruction or ethical guidance.",

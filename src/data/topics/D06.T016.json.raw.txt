  "overview": "In Jain philosophy, \"vedanīya\" (often rendered as Vedaniya karma) denotes the category of karmic matter responsible for producing the soul’s experiences of pleasure and pain. It is one of the four \"aghātiya\" (non-destructive) karmas, which shape the embodied and circumstantial conditions of existence without directly obscuring the soul’s intrinsic qualities of infinite knowledge, perception, energy, and bliss.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Types_of_Karma_%28Jainism%29) [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/essay/jain-science-and-spirituality/d/doc1144905.html) While the \"ghātiya\" karmas deform the very capacities of the jīva, vedanīya karma instead governs how those capacities are felt—whether the fruits of past actions are tasted as sukha (pleasant) or duḥkha (painful) experience.\n\nClassical Jain texts describe eight principal types of karma, grouped into four ghātiya and four aghātiya forms. The Tattvārthasūtra, a foundational, cross-sectarian manual of Jain doctrine, lists these eight in a compact sūtra: knowledge-obscuring, perception-obscuring, feeling-producing (vedanīya), deluding, lifespan-determining, body-determining, status-determining, and obstructive.  [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/essay/jain-science-and-spirituality/d/doc1144905.html) [[2]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Types_of_Karma_%28Jainism%29) Within this taxonomy, vedanīya karma is the specific bond that causes the soul to undergo alternating phases of joy, comfort, sorrow, and suffering, corresponding to the maturation of previously accumulated karmic particles. Because it is aghātiya, it does not damage the soul’s essential purity, but it does condition the experiential texture of each life.\n\nVedanīya karma is traditionally divided into two main subtypes: \"sātā vedanīya\" (pleasure-producing karma) and \"asātā vedanīya\" (pain-producing karma).  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Types_of_Karma_%28Jainism%29) [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/essay/jain-science-and-spirituality/d/doc1144905.html) Sātā vedanīya ripens as experiences such as physical ease, emotional happiness, favorable circumstances, and subtle inner contentment. Asātā vedanīya, by contrast, manifests as bodily illness, mental distress, social adversity, and other forms of duḥkha. Both are understood as precise, law-governed results of the soul’s own past activities—violent or compassionate, deceitful or truthful, greedy or restrained—rather than arbitrary rewards or punishments imposed by a deity. The same soul can carry both subtypes simultaneously, with their respective fruits emerging at different times according to their duration, intensity, and the conditions that trigger their maturation.\n\nFrom the standpoint of karmic process, vedanīya karma participates in the general cycle of influx (āsrava), bondage (bandha), stoppage (saṃvara), and shedding (nirjarā). When a living being acts under the influence of passions such as anger, pride, deceit, and greed, subtle karmic matter flows toward the soul and binds to it, taking on the specific form of pleasure- or pain-producing vedanīya karma depending on the quality of the act and its intention.  [[3]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Karma_in_Jainism) [[4]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Karma_in_Jainism) Through ethical discipline—especially ahiṃsā (non-violence), truthfulness, non-stealing, chastity, and non-possessiveness—the influx of new vedanīya karma can be reduced, and existing bonds can be weakened. Ascetic practices and patient endurance of present pleasure and pain are interpreted as opportunities for nirjarā, the gradual exhaustion of vedanīya and other karmas.\n\nJain thinkers emphasize that even pleasurable vedanīya karma is ultimately a form of bondage, because it keeps the soul invested in saṃsāra through attachment to agreeable experiences. Painful vedanīya karma, while difficult, can become a powerful occasion for detachment, introspection, and spiritual resolve. Thus, both sātā and asātā vedanīya are ambivalent: they are neither inherently good nor evil, but they reveal the moral architecture of the universe and the consequences of one’s own dispositions. Advanced spiritual practitioners aim not to maximize pleasure or minimize pain as such, but to cultivate equanimity (samatā) in the face of both, thereby loosening the hold of vedanīya karma and progressing toward the higher stages of spiritual development (guṇasthāna).\n\nIn the broader karmic system, vedanīya interacts with the other aghātiya karmas—āyu (lifespan), nāma (body-determining), and gotra (status)—to shape the concrete profile of a given birth.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Types_of_Karma_%28Jainism%29) [[2]](https://grokipedia.com/page/Types_of_Karma_%28Jainism%29) For example, a soul may be born with a particular body and social position (due to nāma and gotra karmas), live for a certain duration (due to āyu karma), and within that framework experience alternating phases of pleasure and pain (due to vedanīya karma). Yet, because vedanīya does not obscure the soul’s essential capacities, it is compatible with significant spiritual progress: even while undergoing intense suffering, a practitioner can cultivate right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct, using the experience of pain as a reminder of the urgency of liberation.\n\nAcross Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions, the basic definition of vedanīya as feeling-producing karma is shared, though technical enumerations of subtypes and detailed classifications may vary slightly. Scholarly and traditional commentaries highlight that the twofold division into pleasure and pain is sufficient for most doctrinal and practical purposes, while more granular subcategories serve to explain the diversity of lived experiences. In all cases, vedanīya karma illustrates a central Jain conviction: that the moral quality of one’s inner and outer actions is inseparable from the texture of one’s future feelings, and that liberation requires transforming not only what one does, but how one responds to the inevitable waves of sukha and duḥkha that arise from karmic maturation.",

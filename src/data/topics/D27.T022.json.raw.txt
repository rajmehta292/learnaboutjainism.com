  "overview": "In Jain traditions, \"yantra texts\" are ritual manuals and devotional compilations that describe, prescribe, and sometimes visually depict sacred diagrams (yantras) used in worship, protection, and meditation. A yantra in this context is typically a geometric or numerically structured diagram—often incorporating seed syllables (bīja-mantras), names of deities, and magic squares—intended to focus attention, invoke protective powers, and support dharma-dhyāna (religious contemplation). While yantras are widely associated with Hindu and Buddhist tantric practice, Jain communities have developed their own distinct corpus of yantra-related literature, shaped by Jain doctrines of ahiṃsā, karma, and liberation.\n\nThese texts usually belong to the broader genre of kalpa literature—ritual handbooks that systematize procedures for pūjā, homa, vrata, and protective rites. They may be composed in Sanskrit, Prakrit, or modern vernaculars, and are transmitted in both manuscript and printed form. Some works focus on a single deity, such as Ghantakarṇa Mahāvīra or Padmāvatī, and provide detailed instructions for drawing, consecrating, and using yantras alongside mantras and offerings. Others embed yantra diagrams within hymns or stotras, where the diagram is traced or visualized as part of recitation. Modern scholarship has highlighted that Jain yantras share structural features with Hindu and Buddhist mandalas—concentric lotus petals, intersecting triangles, protective squares (bhūpura)—yet their theological framing and soteriological aims remain distinctly Jain, emphasizing the destruction of karma and the cultivation of right faith (samyagdarśana).  [[0]](https://www.academia.edu/42205873/Jain_Ma%E1%B9%87%E1%B8%8Dalas_and_Yantras)\n\nHistorically, yantra texts appear in both Digambara and Śvetāmbara milieus, though their prominence and acceptance vary by lineage and local practice. Some communities treat yantra-mantra-tantra literature as a legitimate extension of the Jain scriptural tradition, arguing that such ritual technologies are \"śāstra-anukūla\" (in accordance with scripture) and can be harnessed for healing, protection from malevolent forces, and worldly welfare, provided they do not violate core ethical commitments. Other groups—especially certain Sthānakavāsī and reformist currents—are more skeptical, viewing elaborate yantra rituals as distractions from scriptural study and internal meditation. As a result, the reception of yantra texts is uneven, and their authority is often considered secondary to canonical āgamas and major commentaries.\n\nContemporary practice shows a wide spectrum: some temples and household shrines prominently display metal yantras of Padmāvatī, Ṛṣimaṇḍala, or Ghantakarṇa, accompanied by printed booklets explaining their use; other communities restrict yantra usage to specific festivals, protective rites, or private devotional routines. Scholars have documented the continued production and sale of Jain yantras in places like Palitana and Jaipur, as well as the adaptation of older textual prescriptions to modern materials (metal plates, laminated prints) and new ritual contexts. At the same time, many yantra texts remain understudied, with limited critical editions and few translations, making it difficult to fully map their historical development and doctrinal nuances. Where evidence is lacking or based on late, unverified editions, interpretations of yantra texts must be treated as provisional and checked against established Jain philosophical sources and living lineages.

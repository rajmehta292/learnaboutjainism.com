  "overview": "Restorative justice is a modern term for approaches to wrongdoing that focus on healing, accountability, and the repair of relationships rather than on punishment alone. In contemporary legal and criminological discourse, restorative justice refers to practices such as victim–offender mediation, community conferencing, and circles of dialogue, where those harmed, those responsible, and the wider community collaboratively address the consequences of an offense and seek constructive resolutions. Instead of asking only, “What law was broken and how should we punish?”, restorative frameworks ask, “Who was harmed, what are their needs, and what obligations arise for the wrongdoer and the community?”\n\nFrom a Jain perspective, the ethical foundations of restorative justice resonate strongly with ahiṃsā (non‑violence), kṣamā (forgiveness), maitri (friendliness), and dayā (compassion). Jain thought understands harm not merely as an external act but as an expression of inner passions (kaṣāya) such as anger, pride, deceit, and greed, which bind harmful karmas to the soul and prolong saṃsāra. Because violence ultimately injures the agent’s own spiritual progress, the response to wrongdoing must aim at transforming the inner dispositions that led to harm, not simply inflicting counter‑harm. This emphasis on inner moral transformation parallels restorative justice’s concern with genuine accountability, remorse, and change in the wrongdoer, rather than fear‑based compliance.  [[0]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/363505250_Ahimsa_and_Compassion_in_Jainism/fulltext/63e67513e2e1515b6b8750b7/Ahimsa-and-Compassion-in-Jainism.pdf)\n\nJain practice offers several ritual and ethical models that illuminate restorative justice. Daily pratikramaṇa (ritual of reflection and repentance) invites the practitioner to review the day’s actions, acknowledge harms done knowingly or unknowingly, confess them before the self and the community, and resolve to avoid them in the future. Annual festivals such as Paryuṣaṇa and Dasalakṣaṇa intensify this process, culminating in the public seeking and granting of forgiveness—“Micchāmi dukkaḍaṃ” or “Khamāmi savve jīve”—where Jains ask forgiveness from all beings for any harm caused. These practices embody a restorative logic: the wrongdoer does not disappear into a purely punitive system but remains embedded in a web of relationships that must be repaired through confession, remorse, forgiveness, and renewed commitment to non‑violence.  [[1]](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Viney-Jain/publication/260286730_Uttam_Kshama_Supreme_Forgiveness_A_Jain_Concept_and_Practice_for_Spiritual_Progress_Social_Harmony_and_Peace/links/00b7d53082e09d45af000000/Uttam-Kshama-Supreme-Forgiveness-A-Jain-Concept-and-Practice-for-Spiritual-Progress-Social-Harmony-and-Peace.pdf) [[0]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/363505250_Ahimsa_and_Compassion_in_Jainism/fulltext/63e67513e2e1515b6b8750b7/Ahimsa-and-Compassion-in-Jainism.pdf)\n\nRestorative justice also aligns with the Jain doctrine of parasparopagraho jīvānām—“living beings are mutually supportive”—which portrays all life as interdependent. Harm to one being reverberates through the network of relationships and karmic connections; conversely, acts of reconciliation and compassion contribute to social harmony and spiritual progress. In this view, justice cannot be reduced to state‑centered punishment or individual rights alone; it must consider the broader ecology of relationships, including non‑human life and the environment. Modern scholarship on Jain ethics has highlighted how ahiṃsā and compassion provide a moral foundation for peacebuilding, human rights, and ecological responsibility, suggesting that Jain principles can enrich contemporary restorative justice by expanding its concern beyond human parties to include all sentient beings and the natural world.  [[2]](https://www.echetana.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/01/1.-R-E-Bharat-1.pdf) [[3]](https://www.ijhssi.org/papers/vol15%282%29/1502168172.pdf) [[0]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/363505250_Ahimsa_and_Compassion_in_Jainism/fulltext/63e67513e2e1515b6b8750b7/Ahimsa-and-Compassion-in-Jainism.pdf)\n\nAt the same time, Jain thought introduces a distinctive metaphysical dimension to restorative justice through the doctrine of karma. Every intentional act of harm binds specific karmic particles to the soul, which ripen as future suffering; every act of restraint, forgiveness, and compassion helps shed karmic bondage. Restorative responses to wrongdoing—such as sincere confession, reparative action, and cultivation of non‑violent habits—are therefore not merely socially beneficial but spiritually necessary. They function as forms of self‑purification (śuddhi) that reduce the weight of karmic consequences. This perspective encourages both victims and offenders to see justice as a shared spiritual project: victims are invited to practice kṣamā, which lessens their own anger and karmic entanglement, while offenders are urged to accept responsibility and engage in reparative conduct as part of their path toward liberation.\n\nHowever, Jain sources also caution against sentimental or superficial forgiveness. True kṣamā requires a realistic appraisal of harm, protection of vulnerable beings, and disciplined effort to prevent future violence. Restorative justice, when informed by Jain ethics, must therefore balance compassion with clarity: it should not erase the seriousness of wrongdoing or neglect the safety of those harmed. Instead, it seeks a middle path where non‑violence, truthfulness (satya), non‑stealing (asteya), sexual restraint (brahmacarya), and non‑possessiveness (aparigraha) guide both the process and the outcomes of justice. In this way, Jain philosophy offers a deep ethical and spiritual framework for restorative justice—one that integrates personal transformation, communal reconciliation, and cosmic responsibility, while leaving specific legal and procedural questions to qualified human experts and living traditions.

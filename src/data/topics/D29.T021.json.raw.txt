  "overview": "Kshapanasara (often written as Kṣapaṇasāra) is a technical Digambara Jain treatise concerned with the detailed process of the destruction (kṣapaṇa) of karmic matter and the purification of the soul. In the received tradition it is closely associated with, and frequently transmitted together with, Labdhisara as an appendix to Acharya Nemichandra Siddhanta Chakravarti’s Gomatsara, the foundational Digambara compendium on the soul (jīva) and karma. While Gomatsara lays out the ontology of the soul and the full taxonomy of karmas, Kshapanasara focuses on the graded intensities of bondage, fruition, and transformation of karmas, explaining how they are progressively weakened and finally annihilated through right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct.  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/labdhisara_022409_022409) [[1]](https://jainqq.org/explore/010662/83)\n\nThe text is composed in a mixed Sanskrit–Prakrit gāthā style typical of Digambara doctrinal works, using highly compressed verses that presuppose familiarity with the broader karma doctrine. It elaborates on themes already present in the Karma-kāṇḍa of Gomatsara, such as the distinctions between bondage (bandha), fruition (udaya), and transformation or migration (saṃkramaṇa) of karmic particles, and the notion of infinite gradations (guṇaśreṇi) in their intensity. By analyzing how karmas increase or decrease in strength across time and across the soul’s provinces (pradeśa), Kshapanasara provides a fine-grained map of the soul’s progress from heavy delusion to the subtle purity required for higher guṇasthānas (spiritual stages).  [[1]](https://jainqq.org/explore/010662/83) [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/labdhisara_022409_022409)\n\nIn the Digambara curriculum, Kshapanasara is studied alongside Labdhisara and Gomatsara by advanced monks and scholars as part of the Karanānuyoga (the doctrinal-explanatory strand of scripture). Labdhisara emphasizes the various attainments (labdhi) that enable right belief and the initial purification of deluding karma, whereas Kshapanasara concentrates on the subsequent, more technical phases of karmic destruction and the mathematical-like progression of karmic intensities. Together, these texts guide the practitioner from the first emergence of right faith through unprecedented spiritual transformation (apūrvakaraṇa) and non-regressing progress (anivṛttikaraṇa), culminating in the complete eradication of ghātīya karmas and the attainment of kevalajñāna (omniscience).  [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/labdhisara_022409_022409) [[1]](https://jainqq.org/explore/010662/83)\n\nHistorically, Kshapanasara is transmitted within the Digambara manuscript tradition under titles such as “Labdhisara Kshapanasara,” indicating its close linkage with Labdhisara and its status as a specialized extension of Nemichandra’s karma doctrine. Modern printed editions and digital archives preserve both the original Devanagari or Grantha scripts and romanized versions, often accompanied by Hindi or Sanskrit commentaries that unpack the dense technical vocabulary. These commentaries explain, for example, how in each moment the intensity of karmic fruition can be infinitely greater than the immediately preceding bondage, and how the migration of karmic provinces into new configurations is again infinitely greater than their prior fruition—subtleties that are central to Kshapanasara’s contribution to Jain karmic theory.  [[1]](https://jainqq.org/explore/010662/83) [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/labdhisara_022409_022409)\n\nIn contemporary Jain studies, Kshapanasara is valued not only as a doctrinal manual but also as a window into the mathematical and quasi-scientific imagination of medieval Digambara thinkers. Its discussions of innumerable (asaṃkhyeya) and infinite (ananta) gradations of karmic intensity intersect with broader Jain reflections on number, time, and cosmology. For practitioners, however, the text remains primarily a guide to understanding how subtle changes in inner dispositions and external conduct affect the precise structure of karmic bondage, reinforcing the central Jain conviction that liberation is achieved through rigorous self-knowledge and disciplined ethical practice rather than through divine intervention or mere ritual observance.

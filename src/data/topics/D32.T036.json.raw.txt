  "overview": "In Jain studies, \"translation method\" refers to the layered, tradition-aware processes by which Jain scriptures and related literature move from their original languages—primarily Ardhamāgadhī Prakrit, other Prakrits, and classical Sanskrit—into later scholastic idioms and modern vernaculars such as Gujarati, Hindi, and English. Rather than a single fixed theory, Jain translation practice emerges from exegetical habits developed over centuries: word-by-word glossing, syntactic reordering, paraphrase, and doctrinal commentary. These methods are visible both in intra-Indian language transfer (Prakrit to Sanskrit or Gujarati) and in contemporary global translations. [[0]](https://library-search.open.ac.uk/discovery/fulldisplay?docid=cdi_informaworld_taylorfrancisbooks_10_4324_9781315443485_29_version2&context=PC&vid=44OPN_INST%3AVU1&lang=en&adaptor=Primo%20Central) [[1]](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315443485-29/translating-sacred-scriptures-nalini-balbir)\n\nA central feature of Jain translation method is the distinction between \"sūtra\" (concise aphoristic text) and \"artha\" (meaning, explanation, or interpretation). Canonical texts are often extremely compressed, requiring unpacking through commentary. Traditional translators and commentators treat the sūtra as a dense container of multiple potential meanings, while artha is the process and product of unfolding those meanings in a more expansive language. This unfolding may occur in the same language (Prakrit sūtra with Prakrit prose) or across languages (Prakrit sūtra with Sanskrit or vernacular explanation). The exegetical tradition therefore functions as an embedded translation apparatus, where interpretation and translation are intertwined. [[2]](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/43856371.pdf)\n\nHistorically, Śvetāmbara Jain communities developed systematic methods for transferring Ardhamāgadhī Prakrit scriptures into Sanskrit and later Gujarati. Scholars have noted that this process involves careful morphological analysis of Prakrit forms, reconstruction of underlying Sanskrit roots, and then re-expression in a target language that preserves doctrinal nuance while adapting to new audiences. Translators often proceed in stages: first segmenting the sūtra, then providing a literal gloss, followed by a more idiomatic rendering and finally a doctrinal explanation that situates the passage within broader Jain philosophy and practice. This multi-layered approach reflects the conviction that language transfer must safeguard both semantic content and soteriological intent. [[0]](https://library-search.open.ac.uk/discovery/fulldisplay?docid=cdi_informaworld_taylorfrancisbooks_10_4324_9781315443485_29_version2&context=PC&vid=44OPN_INST%3AVU1&lang=en&adaptor=Primo%20Central) [[1]](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315443485-29/translating-sacred-scriptures-nalini-balbir)\n\nModern academic translations of Jain texts build on these traditional methods but also introduce explicit theoretical frameworks from translation studies and philology. Editors compare multiple manuscripts, note variant readings, and distinguish between primary text (corpus tier A) and later commentaries (tiers B–C). They often provide parallel transliteration, literal translation, and interpretive translation, accompanied by footnotes that mark where doctrinal presuppositions, sectarian differences, or uncertain readings affect the choice of words. Particular care is taken with technical terms such as \"jīva\" (soul), \"ajīva\" (non-soul), \"karma\", \"guṇasthāna\" (stages of spiritual development), and \"ahiṃsā\" (non-violence), where overly broad or overly narrow equivalents in English can distort Jain metaphysics and ethics.\n\nJain translation method also grapples with the status of mantras and formulaic recitations, such as the Navakāra-mantra (Namaskāra-mantra). While Jain tradition generally exhibits a positive attitude toward translating scriptures, some formulaic utterances are treated as possessing special sonic or ritual power, making translators cautious about substituting them with purely semantic equivalents. In such cases, translators may retain the original Prakrit or Sanskrit form, offer transliteration, and then provide an explanatory translation that clarifies meaning without claiming to replicate ritual efficacy. This approach respects both the doctrinal emphasis on right knowledge and the lived devotional practices of communities. [[0]](https://library-search.open.ac.uk/discovery/fulldisplay?docid=cdi_informaworld_taylorfrancisbooks_10_4324_9781315443485_29_version2&context=PC&vid=44OPN_INST%3AVU1&lang=en&adaptor=Primo%20Central) [[1]](https://www.taylorfrancis.com/chapters/edit/10.4324/9781315443485-29/translating-sacred-scriptures-nalini-balbir)\n\nAcross sects—Śvetāmbara, Digambara, and various sub-traditions—translation method is shaped by differing canons and doctrinal emphases, yet there is a shared concern to preserve the path of liberation (mokṣa-mārga) through accurate transmission of teachings. Sectarian commentaries sometimes employ translation strategies to reconcile or highlight differences, using neutral terminology, layered formulations, or hierarchical distinctions between conventional and ultimate standpoints (vyavahāra and niścaya). Recent scholarship has shown how Prakrit and its translations can serve as tools for both doctrinal clarification and sectarian reconciliation, illustrating that translation in Jainism is not merely linguistic but also ethical and communal.

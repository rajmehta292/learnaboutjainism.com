  "overview": "In Jainism, fasting (upavāsa, anasana, tapas) is a respected spiritual discipline, but it is never meant to become a competition. The core aims of Jain fasting are inner purification, reduction of passions (kaṣāya), and gentle detachment from material and bodily attachment. When fasting is turned into a contest—who can fast longer, more frequently, or more dramatically—it shifts the focus from humility and self‑discipline to pride, display, and sometimes physical harm. This directly conflicts with the central Jain values of ahiṃsā (non‑violence), aparigraha (non‑possessiveness), and saṃyama (self‑restraint).\n\nTraditional Jain texts describe many forms of austerity—complete fasts (anasana), partial fasts (unodarī), limitation of food items (vṛtti‑saṃkṣepa), and renunciation of favorite tastes (rasa‑tyāga). These are framed as personal vows undertaken with right faith (samyag‑darśana), right knowledge (samyag‑jñāna), and right conduct (samyag‑cāritra). The emphasis is always on suitability to one’s health, stage of life, and spiritual capacity, guided by teachers and scriptures, not by peer pressure or public comparison. Austerities are praised when they are quiet, inward, and free from attachment to reputation.\n\nCompetitive fasting undermines this ethos in several ways. First, it can encourage subtle or overt violence toward one’s own body, especially when people push beyond safe limits to “win” or to impress others. Jain ethics treat the body as a vehicle for spiritual progress; harming it out of ego or rivalry is considered a form of hiṃsā. Second, competition inflames pride (māna), desire for praise (lābha‑pūjā), and comparison with others—precisely the passions that austerities are meant to reduce. Third, it can create coercive social environments, where children, youth, or vulnerable adults feel pressured to undertake fasts they do not understand or cannot safely sustain.\n\nModern Jain communities and educators increasingly articulate the principle of “no fasting competition” as a practical guideline: fasts should be voluntary, informed, medically safe, and spiritually motivated, never a public contest. Parents and teachers are urged to protect minors from excessive or waterless fasting, and to prioritize health, education, and balanced practice. For adults, responsible fasting includes consulting physicians when there are medical conditions (such as diabetes, pregnancy, or chronic illness) and seeking guidance from qualified religious teachers before undertaking extended austerities.\n\nFrom the perspective of karma theory and the fourteen guṇasthānas (stages of spiritual development), the value of a fast lies in the inner state—calmness, non‑attachment, and reduction of passions—not in the number of days or the extremity of the practice. A short, well‑understood fast performed with humility and compassion can be spiritually more beneficial than a long, competitive fast driven by ego. Texts that discuss terminal fasting (sallekhanā or samādhi‑maraṇa) insist on non‑coercion, mental stability, and gradual preparation; they explicitly distinguish it from impulsive self‑harm or socially pressured deprivation. By analogy, everyday fasting must also avoid any form of compulsion or rivalry.\n\nIn practical terms, a Jain ethic of “no fasting competition” encourages:\n\n1. **Intention over duration**: asking why one is fasting, not how long.\n2. **Health over display**: respecting medical limits and avoiding harm.\n3. **Guidance over imitation**: learning from scriptures and experienced teachers rather than copying extreme examples.\n4. **Privacy over publicity**: keeping austerities modest and non‑performative.\n\nBecause fasting touches both spiritual and bodily well‑being, responsible practice requires both scriptural understanding and medical prudence. When there is any risk of harm—especially for children, pregnant persons, the elderly, or those with illness—Jain principles of ahiṃsā and anukampā (compassion) support reducing, modifying, or even refraining from fasting. In such cases, alternative practices like sāmāyika (meditative equanimity), pratikramaṇa (ethical reflection and repentance), charity, and study of scriptures can be emphasized instead. Overall, the Jain vision is clear: austerity is a tool for inner freedom, not a stage for competition.",

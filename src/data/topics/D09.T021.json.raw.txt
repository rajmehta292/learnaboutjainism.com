  "overview": "Siddhasena Divākara (Jain Prakrit: \"Siddhasena Divākara\") is one of the foundational Jain philosophers of late antiquity, especially revered in the Śvetāmbara tradition as an ācārya whose works shaped Jain logic, epistemology, and the systematic articulation of anekāntavāda. Most traditional accounts place him in the 4th–5th century CE, flourishing under the Gupta Empire, and describe him as a Brahmin scholar who, after losing a debate to the Jain ācārya Vṛddhavadisūri, accepted initiation into the Jain saṅgha and eventually rose to the rank of ācārya. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siddhasenadiv%C4%81karasuri) [[1]](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Siddhasena_Divakara)\n\nHe is remembered by the honorific epithet \"Divākara\"—\"sun\" or \"illuminator\"—signifying his role as a light of the Jain order. Traditional narratives portray him as both a rigorous logician and a devotional poet, combining sharp analysis of philosophical doctrines with hymns of intense bhakti, especially toward Tīrthaṅkara Pārśvanātha. His life-story includes the famous episode in Ujjain, where his encounter with a Śiva liṅga and King Vikramāditya culminates in the revelation of the image of Avanti Pārśvanātha, now a major Śvetāmbara pilgrimage icon. [[1]](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Siddhasena_Divakara) [[2]](https://en.bharatpedia.org/wiki/Siddhasena)\n\nFrom the standpoint of Jain intellectual history, Siddhasena is most closely associated with the text *Sanmatitarka* (also known as *Sanmati-sūtra*), often described as the first major Jain work on logic composed in Sanskrit. In it, he offers a systematic treatment of Jain epistemology and the doctrine of anekāntavāda, organizing the classical seven *nayas* (viewpoints) into two broad families: those emphasizing the substantial, enduring aspect of reality (*dravyāstika-nayas*) and those emphasizing the changing, modal aspect (*paryāyāstika-nayas*). This classification helps explain how apparently conflicting descriptions of reality can be reconciled within a non-one-sided perspective. [[2]](https://en.bharatpedia.org/wiki/Siddhasena)\n\nAlongside *Sanmatitarka*, tradition attributes to Siddhasena other important works, including *Nyāyāvatāra* (a concise introduction to logical reasoning), *Kalyāṇamaṇḍira-stotra* (a Sanskrit hymn to Pārśvanātha that became one of the nine holiest recitations—*Nav-smaraṇa*—for Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka Jains), and *Vardhamāna-Śakra-stava*, a hymn praising Mahāvīra and the god Śakra. Many texts ascribed to him are lost or survive only in fragments or later quotations, and modern scholarship must carefully distinguish between securely attested works and later attributions. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Siddhasenadiv%C4%81karasuri) [[2]](https://en.bharatpedia.org/wiki/Siddhasena)\n\nIn doctrinal terms, Siddhasena stands at a crucial juncture between early canonical materials and later scholastic syntheses. He does not present himself as innovating new dogma; rather, he clarifies and defends existing Jain teachings—especially non-absolutism and the multi-perspectival nature of truth—using the emerging tools of Indian logic and debate theory. His work engages, implicitly and explicitly, with rival schools such as Buddhism, Nyāya, and Vedānta, though surviving passages often refer to them generically rather than by detailed names. This makes him a key figure for comparative philosophy: he shows how Jain thinkers adopted and adapted pan-Indian logical vocabulary while preserving a distinctively Jain metaphysical and ethical framework.\n\nDevotionally and ritually, Siddhasena’s *Kalyāṇamaṇḍira-stotra* has had enduring impact. It is recited in temples and homes, especially within the Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka community, as a hymn that combines praise of Pārśvanātha with reflections on the auspicious transformation of the devotee’s inner world. The Ujjain narrative—where his recitation is said to cause the liṅga to split and reveal the image of Avanti Pārśvanātha—illustrates how Jain tradition remembers him not only as a philosopher but also as a miracle-working saint whose devotion manifests tangible change in the sacred landscape. [[1]](https://www.wikiwand.com/en/articles/Siddhasena_Divakara) [[2]](https://en.bharatpedia.org/wiki/Siddhasena)\n\nModern scholarship, working with limited manuscript evidence and layered hagiographical traditions, generally treats Siddhasena as a historical Śvetāmbara monk and philosopher whose core contribution lies in the articulation of Jain logic and anekāntavāda. At the same time, scholars note that some later narratives—such as his supposed plan to translate all Jain scriptures from Prakrit into Sanskrit and the disciplinary pilgrimage imposed by his teacher—function more as didactic stories about humility, obedience, and the proper handling of scripture than as verifiable historical reports. As with many early Jain ācāryas, the boundary between history and legend remains porous, and careful study requires distinguishing primary textual evidence from later devotional elaboration.\n\nBecause Siddhasena’s works are used across sectarian lines and cited in both traditional commentaries and modern academic studies, he occupies a shared space in Jain intellectual memory. Digambara authors respect his logical formulations, even though his monastic affiliation is Śvetāmbara; Śvetāmbara authors highlight his role as an ācārya and hymn-composer. This cross-sect reception underscores the broader Jain conviction that rigorous reasoning and non-violent, many-sided truth are common treasures, not the property of a single lineage.",

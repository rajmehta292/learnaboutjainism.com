  "overview": "Southeast Asia is the southeastern subregion of Asia, stretching from the eastern fringes of the Indian subcontinent to the western Pacific and bounded by the Bay of Bengal, South China Sea, and Java Sea.\nIt is commonly divided into two major geographic zones: mainland Southeast Asia—Cambodia, Laos, Myanmar (Burma), Thailand, Vietnam, and the peninsular part of Malaysia plus Singapore—and maritime or insular Southeast Asia, comprising Indonesia, the Philippines, Brunei, Timor-Leste, and the island territories of Malaysia and Singapore.\nThis dual structure, part continental and part archipelagic, produces an exceptional range of landscapes, from the river valleys of the Mekong, Chao Phraya, and Irrawaddy to the volcanic chains of Indonesia and the Philippines, all largely within tropical and subtropical climate belts shaped by monsoon systems.  [[0]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Southeast-Asia) [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia)\n\nThe region covers roughly 4.5–5 million square kilometers of land and sea, with about 1.7 million square kilometers of land area, and has a population of around 675–680 million people, making it more populous than Europe or Latin America.\nIndonesia alone spans more than 3,000 miles west to east and includes over 17,000 islands, while even landlocked Laos is comparable in area to the United Kingdom, illustrating that many Southeast Asian states are territorially large despite perceptions of smallness.\nClimatically, most of the region experiences warm temperatures year-round, with distinct wet and dry seasons driven by monsoon winds; higher elevations and northern margins can be cooler, and the region sits astride the tectonically active Ring of Fire, giving rise to frequent earthquakes and volcanic activity.  [[0]](https://www.britannica.com/place/Southeast-Asia) [[2]](https://sounny.github.io/worldregionalgeography/chapters/10-southeast-asia/index.html)\n\nHistorically, Southeast Asia has been a crossroads of civilizations, trade routes, and religious traditions.\nEarly polities such as Funan, Champa, Srivijaya, Pagan, Angkor, Majapahit, and Ayutthaya emerged from indigenous societies interacting with traders and cultural influences from India, China, and the wider Indian Ocean world.\nIndian-derived scripts, concepts of kingship, and religions—Hinduism and Buddhism—spread widely, while Chinese commercial networks and migration further reshaped coastal societies.\nFrom the 16th century onward, European powers including Portugal, Spain, the Netherlands, Britain, and France established colonial regimes, carving the region into spheres such as French Indochina, the Dutch East Indies, British Malaya and Burma, and the Spanish (later American) Philippines.\nThe 20th century brought Japanese occupation during World War II, followed by decolonization, nationalist movements, and Cold War conflicts, most notably in Vietnam, Laos, and Cambodia, which left deep political and social legacies.  [[3]](https://en.alegsaonline.com/art/92265) [[4]](https://asiasociety.org/education/introduction-southeast-asia)\n\nCulturally and religiously, Southeast Asia is one of the most diverse regions on Earth.\nMajor religious communities include Muslim majorities in Indonesia, Malaysia, and Brunei; Theravada Buddhist majorities in Thailand, Myanmar, Cambodia, and Laos; a Catholic majority in the Philippines; and mixed or plural configurations in Singapore, Vietnam, and Timor-Leste, alongside Hindu enclaves such as Bali and enduring local and folk traditions.\nEthnolinguistic diversity is equally striking: Austronesian languages dominate much of maritime Southeast Asia, while Austroasiatic, Tai–Kadai, and Tibeto-Burman families are prominent on the mainland, with numerous minority and highland groups maintaining distinct identities.\nUrban centers such as Jakarta, Manila, Bangkok, Ho Chi Minh City, Kuala Lumpur, Singapore, and Yangon function as economic and cultural hubs, yet rural livelihoods—especially rice cultivation in fertile river plains and deltas—remain central to many societies.\nThis mosaic of religions, languages, and social structures challenges any notion of a homogeneous “Southeast Asian culture.”  [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Southeast_Asia) [[2]](https://sounny.github.io/worldregionalgeography/chapters/10-southeast-asia/index.html) [[4]](https://asiasociety.org/education/introduction-southeast-asia)\n\nEconomically, Southeast Asia has undergone rapid transformation in recent decades.\nThe Association of Southeast Asian Nations (ASEAN), founded in 1967 and now comprising ten member states (Brunei, Cambodia, Indonesia, Laos, Malaysia, Myanmar, the Philippines, Singapore, Thailand, and Vietnam), has promoted regional cooperation, trade liberalization, and political dialogue, contributing to the emergence of the region as a significant manufacturing, services, and logistics hub.\nStrategic maritime chokepoints such as the Strait of Malacca and the South China Sea place Southeast Asia at the heart of global shipping and energy routes, while export-oriented industries, tourism, and digital economies drive growth in many countries.\nAt the same time, the region faces serious challenges: environmental degradation from deforestation and palm oil expansion, loss of biodiversity, urban congestion, inequality, and vulnerability to climate change impacts such as sea-level rise and intensified storms.\nPolitical systems range from consolidated democracies to military or single-party regimes, and questions of human rights, minority protections, and sustainable development remain central to contemporary debates.  [[3]](https://en.alegsaonline.com/art/92265) [[2]](https://sounny.github.io/worldregionalgeography/chapters/10-southeast-asia/index.html)\n\nFrom a broader civilizational perspective, Southeast Asia continues to function as a bridge between South Asia, East Asia, and the Pacific.\nIts historical role as a meeting place for traders, pilgrims, and migrants has produced layered identities and syncretic cultural forms, visible in architecture, performing arts, cuisine, and religious practice.\nModern integration initiatives—whether through ASEAN, subregional economic corridors, or cross-border infrastructure—seek to harness this connectivity while managing geopolitical tensions involving major external powers.\nIn this sense, Southeast Asia is not only a geographic label but also a dynamic regional system whose significance in global economics, security, and culture is likely to grow in the coming decades.\n\nAlthough Jainism originated and developed primarily in the Indian subcontinent, Jain communities have migrated to and settled in parts of Southeast Asia in the modern period, especially in commercial centers such as Singapore, Malaysia, and, to a lesser extent, Thailand and Indonesia.\nThese diasporic communities establish temples and associations, maintain lay practices such as anuvratas (small vows), and participate in broader interreligious and multicultural urban life, illustrating how Southeast Asia’s plural environment accommodates diverse religious traditions, including those whose classical textual and historical centers lie outside the region.",

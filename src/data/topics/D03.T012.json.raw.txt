  "overview": "Kala (kāla) in Jain philosophy is the technical term for time, recognized as one of the six fundamental substances (dravya) that constitute reality, alongside soul (jīva), matter (pudgala), space (ākāśa), the medium of motion (dharma), and the medium of rest (adharma). As a dravya, kāla is not merely a way of counting moments but an ontological category that participates in the structure of existence itself. It is described as eternal, formless, subtle, and indispensable for the processes of change, motion, and continuity in all other substances.\n\nIn the canonical synthesis Tattvārtha Sūtra, accepted by both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions, time is explicitly classified as a substance. The sūtra \"kālaś ca\" (5.39) states that time also is a dravya, and the classical commentary Sarvārthasiddhi explains that kāla satisfies both major Jain definitions of substance: that which exists with origination (utpāda), destruction (vyaya), and permanence (dhrauvya), and that which possesses qualities (guṇa) and modes (paryāya). Time exhibits permanence through its own nature, while its modes arise and perish in relation to the changing states of other substances. In this way, kāla is both enduring and dynamically involved in the flux of the universe.\n\nJain thinkers distinguish between real (mukhya or niścaya) time and conventional (vyavahāra) time. Real time is the subtle, ontological dravya whose specific function is to assist substances in their continuity of being through gradual changes (vartanā), modifications (pariṇāma), movements (kriyā), endurance (paratva), and non-endurance (aparatva). Tattvārtha Sūtra 5.22 enumerates these functions, emphasizing that while substances have an inherent capacity to undergo new modes, an external instrumental cause is required for the incessant attainment of these modes, and this auxiliary cause is kāla. Conventional time, by contrast, is the empirically experienced and measured sequence of instants, hours, days, and ages, inferred from the motion of luminary deities (jyotiṣī deva) such as the sun, moon, and planets. The divisions of conventional time—like ghāḍī, muhūrta, day and night—are said to be caused by these celestial beings (Tattvārtha Sūtra 4.14).\n\nAt the finest level, Jain philosophy speaks of indivisible temporal units called samaya, which function as the atomic units of vyavahāra-kāla. Tattvārtha Sūtra 5.40 states that conventional time consists of infinite samaya, explaining that although the present is a single instant, the instants of past and future are infinite. These samaya aggregate into larger units such as āvalī and other measures, forming a detailed hierarchy of temporal quantification. This atomistic view of time underlies the broader Jain cosmological model of the kālacakra, the wheel of time, in which the universe passes through vast ascending (utsarpiṇī) and descending (avasarpiṇī) cycles. Each cycle comprises innumerable smaller periods, and the moral and spiritual conditions of beings vary across these phases.\n\nMetaphysically, kāla is acetanatva (non-conscious), amūrtatva (formless), sūkṣmatva (extremely subtle), and characterized by agurulaghutva, the capacity to maintain distinction without becoming heavier or lighter relative to other substances. Its particular quality is vartanā—the assistance it provides to other dravyas in their continuity through gradual change. Without time, the modes of substances could not arise in an ordered sequence, and karmic processes such as bondage, fruition, and dissociation would lack temporal structure. Thus, kāla is integral to Jain accounts of karma and liberation: while time itself does not cause bondage or release, it is the necessary condition for the unfolding of karmic states and for the progression of the soul through the fourteen stages of spiritual development (guṇasthāna).\n\nEthically and soteriologically, the Jain conception of time encourages both urgency and patience. On one hand, the vastness of cosmic cycles and the infinitude of samaya highlight the rarity of human birth and the preciousness of opportunities for right faith, knowledge, and conduct. On the other hand, the continuity and regularity of time’s assistance to change suggest that spiritual transformation is a gradual process, unfolding through countless instants of right effort. Modern scholarship has emphasized that Jain time theory combines a rigorous metaphysical atomism with a cyclical cosmology and a practical concern for how beings use their finite conventional time within an infinite temporal framework. In sum, kāla in Jainism is not merely a backdrop for events but a fundamental, subtle dravya that structures change, supports karmic processes, and frames the path to liberation.",

  "overview": "In Jainism, festival observance (parva–utsava) is woven into the rhythm of the religious year as a disciplined practice of self-purification rather than mere social celebration. Important days in the Jain calendar are called parvan, and they are typically marked by fasting, scriptural study, confession, and communal forgiveness, alongside temple worship and family gatherings. The major festivals cluster around the four‑month monsoon period (caturmāsa/chaumāsā), when monks and nuns traditionally remain in one place, allowing sustained contact between ascetics and laity. [[0]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Jainism/Festivals) [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_festivals)\n\nMany principal Jain festivals are linked to the five auspicious events (pañca‑kalyāṇaka) in the lives of the Tirthankaras—conception, birth, renunciation, attainment of omniscience, and final liberation (nirvāṇa). Mahāvīra’s birth (Mahāvīr janma‑kalyāṇaka), his nirvāṇa (commemorated at Diwali), and the annual season of repentance and forgiveness (Paryuṣaṇa/Daśalakṣaṇa) are central examples. On these occasions, lay Jains intensify observances such as fasting (upavāsa), temporary adoption of ascetic vows (pauṣadha), collective recitation of texts, and participation in processions and temple rituals, always framed by the core ethical principles of ahiṃsā (non‑violence), aparigraha (non‑possessiveness), and self‑restraint. [[0]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Jainism/Festivals) [[2]](https://jainworld.jainworld.com/jainbooks/guideline/46.htm)\n\nParyuṣaṇa Parva—eight days for Śvetāmbaras and ten days as Daśalakṣaṇa Parva for Digambaras—is widely regarded as the most important annual observance. It is explained as a time of “abiding” or “coming together,” when Jains turn inward through fasting, listening to scriptural discourses, and performing pratikramaṇa (ritual self‑examination and repentance). Śvetāmbara communities traditionally focus on public reading of the Kalpa Sūtra, while Digambara communities center Daśalakṣaṇa Parva on the Tattvārtha‑sūtra and the ten dharmas (qualities) such as kṣamā (forgiveness) and ārjava (straightforwardness). The final day, Samvatsarī, is marked by the practice of universal forgiveness—seeking and granting pardon for harms committed knowingly or unknowingly during the year. [[0]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Jainism/Festivals) [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_festivals) [[2]](https://jainworld.jainworld.com/jainbooks/guideline/46.htm)\n\nMahāvīr Janma Kalyāṇaka, celebrated in the month of Caitra, commemorates the birth of Mahāvīra with temple worship, ceremonial bathing (abhiṣeka) of the Jina image, processions, and discourses on his teachings. Diwali, falling on the new‑moon night of Kārtika, is observed by Jains as the nirvāṇa of Mahāvīra at Pāvāpuri and, in many traditions, as the occasion linked with Gautama Svāmī’s attainment of kevala‑jñāna (omniscience). Lamps symbolize the light of right knowledge rather than worldly prosperity, and the day often marks the beginning of a new religious year, followed by Jñāna Pañcamī, a festival dedicated to honoring scriptures and spiritual knowledge. [[0]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Jainism/Festivals) [[3]](https://bhaktilipi.in/jain-festivals-diwali-paryushan-mahavir-jayanti/) [[4]](https://jainsattva.com/jain-philosophy/jain-festival-list-celebration-traditions/)\n\nBeyond these widely known observances, Jain calendars include many other festivals such as Navapad Oli and Asṭāhnikā Parva, which emphasize austerity, worship of specific siddha‑cakras (sacred diagrams), and remembrance of scriptural narratives like the story of Śrīpāla and Māyānā. Regional and sectarian variations are significant: Śvetāmbara Mūrti‑pūjaka, Sthānakavāsī, and Terāpanth communities differ in their use of images and temple rituals, while Digambara communities may emphasize distinct textual recitations and pilgrimage practices. Yet across these differences, the inner orientation of Jain festival observance remains consistent—turning celebration into an opportunity for renunciation, ethical renewal, and deepened awareness of the path to liberation.

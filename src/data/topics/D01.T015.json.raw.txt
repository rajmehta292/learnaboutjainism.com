  "overview": "Jainism is often introduced through a few striking ideas—strict non‑violence, detailed rules about food, and the image of serene Jinas in meditation. Yet the tradition is far broader and more philosophically intricate than these surface impressions suggest. Classical Jain thinkers developed sophisticated accounts of reality (anekantavada, the doctrine of non‑one‑sidedness), logic, and ethics, all oriented toward the purification of the soul (jiva) and its liberation from karmic bondage.  [[0]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-philosophy/Jain-philosophy)\n\nMany misconceptions arise when key Jain concepts are detached from their scriptural and philosophical context. Ahimsa (non‑violence) is reduced to dietary rules, karma is treated as a vague moral metaphor, and the practice of sallekhana is misidentified with suicide. In reality, these ideas belong to a carefully structured path of right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct (triratna), articulated in canonical and post‑canonical texts such as the Agamas and the Tattvartha Sutra.  [[1]](https://philosophy.institute/indian-philosophy/jainism-path-liberation-ethics-morality/) [[2]](https://philosophy.institute/religions-of-the-world/exploring-sacred-texts-jainism-guide/)\n\nAnother common misunderstanding is that Jainism is merely a “negative” religion of renunciation and prohibition. The primary sources instead present a positive vision of the soul as inherently endowed with infinite knowledge, perception, energy, and bliss, temporarily obscured by karmic matter. Ethical discipline is not imposed for social control but is understood as a precise technology for stopping the influx of new karma (samvara) and shedding existing karma (nirjara).  [[1]](https://philosophy.institute/indian-philosophy/jainism-path-liberation-ethics-morality/)\n\nJainism is also frequently described as simply “atheistic,” as if the absence of a creator‑god implied a lack of devotion or spiritual depth. The tradition does reject a world‑creating deity, but it affirms a rich devotional culture centered on the Jinas—perfected beings who reveal the path to liberation—and on reverence for scriptures, teachers, and the community of ascetics.  [[0]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Indian-philosophy/Jain-philosophy) [[2]](https://philosophy.institute/religions-of-the-world/exploring-sacred-texts-jainism-guide/)\n\nFinally, misconceptions persist because of the diversity within Jain traditions themselves (Digambara, Svetambara Murtipujaka, Sthanakavasi, Terapanth, and others). Differences in scripture, ritual, and monastic discipline can be mistaken for contradictions in core doctrine. When read carefully, however, the major sects share foundational commitments to non‑violence, karmic causality, and the triratna, even while they debate details of practice and textual authority.

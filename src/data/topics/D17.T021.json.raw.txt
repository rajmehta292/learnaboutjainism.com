  "overview": "Svetambara liturgy refers to the structured body of prayers, recitations, ritual sequences, and festival observances used by the Svetambara Jain tradition in both monastic and lay contexts. It is not a single fixed \"service\" but a constellation of liturgical cycles—daily, fortnightly, four‑monthly, and annual—organized around the core aims of Jain practice: reverence for the Tirthankaras and the Panch Parameshti, purification of karma, cultivation of equanimity (samayik), and confession with resolve (pratikraman).\n\nAt the heart of Svetambara liturgy stand the Avashyakas, the \"essential\" six practices: samayik, chauvisantho (adoration of the 24 Tirthankaras), vandana (salutation to monks and nuns), alochana‑pratikraman (introspective confession), kayotsarga (meditation in bodily stillness), and pratyakhyan (formal resolve or renunciation). These Avashyakas form the backbone of pratikraman liturgies performed by monks, nuns, and laypeople, especially in the morning (raisi) and evening (devasi), as well as in the more elaborate pakshik (fortnightly), chaumasi (four‑monthly), and samvatsari (annual) pratikraman. [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratikramana) [[1]](https://jainworld.com/education/jain-education-material/senior-level/pratikraman/)\n\nSvetambara liturgy is deeply text‑based. The canonical Agamas—preserved primarily in Ardhamagadhi Prakrit—provide the doctrinal and ritual framework, while later liturgical compilations and commentaries systematize the order of recitation. The Avashyaka‑sutra, Kalpa‑sutra, Uttaradhyayana‑sutra, and other Svetambara Agamas are recited in specific seasons and contexts. For example, during Paryushana, the Kalpa‑sutra is publicly read, narrating the lives of the Tirthankaras, especially Mahavira, and prescribing monastic regulations; this reading is embedded in a larger liturgical cycle of fasting, pratikraman, and communal forgiveness (micchami dukkadam). [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Pratikramana)\n\nDaily liturgy for lay Svetambaras typically includes recitation of the Namokar Mantra, short samayik, and simplified pratikraman, often in the vernacular alongside Prakrit or Sanskrit verses. More extended forms, especially on holy days, incorporate hymns (stavan), praise of the Tirthankaras (chauvisantho/logassa), guru‑vandan, and scriptural readings. The liturgy is performed in temples with images (murtipujaka) or in non‑image halls (as in Sthanakavasi and Terapanth sub‑traditions), but the core Avashyaka structure remains recognizable across these branches, even where the specific sutras, melodies, or languages differ.\n\nSvetambara liturgy also includes puja sequences—ritual offerings to the Jina images—such as snatra‑puja, ashtaprakari‑puja (eightfold offerings), and various stotras like the Bhaktamara‑stotra and Uvasaggaharam‑stotra. These are not merely devotional songs but are embedded in carefully ordered acts of approaching the image, bowing, circumambulation, offering substances (water, rice, flowers, sandal paste), and reciting praise that emphasizes the Jina’s qualities of omniscience, non‑attachment, and liberation. While Digambara and Svetambara share many doctrinal elements, the Svetambara liturgical repertoire is distinctive in its particular Agamic recensions, its use of white‑clad monastic officiants, and its strong emphasis on communal pratikraman during Paryushana.\n\nModern Svetambara communities have adapted liturgy to contemporary contexts: shorter pratikraman booklets, bilingual editions (Prakrit with Gujarati, Hindi, or English), and audio‑visual guides for youth and diaspora communities. Yet the underlying structure—Avashyaka‑based introspection, confession, and resolve, framed by reverence to the Panch Parameshti—remains anchored in the canonical and early post‑canonical texts. Scholarly and traditional commentaries continue to debate details of sequence, pronunciation, and textual authenticity, but there is broad agreement that Svetambara liturgy is a disciplined, text‑centered path for karmic purification rather than a purely aesthetic or emotional ritual performance.",

  "overview": "In Jain studies, “name variants” refers to the different forms, spellings, and linguistic adaptations of personal and doctrinal names across Prakrit, Sanskrit, and modern Indian languages, as well as across regional and sectarian traditions. Because Jainism developed over many centuries in a multilingual environment, the same Tirthankara, acharya, text, or place can appear under several closely related names. These variants arise from phonetic shifts between Middle Indo‑Aryan Prakrits and Classical Sanskrit, from later vernaculars such as Hindi, Gujarati, Kannada, and Tamil, and from differing transliteration conventions into Roman script.\n\nAt the core of the tradition, many Tirthankara names are originally attested in Ardhamāgadhī or other Prakrit forms, later Sanskritized and then vernacularized. For example, the first Tirthankara appears in Prakrit as “Usabh” (or “Ṛsabha”) and in Sanskritized form as “Ṛṣabhanātha” or “Ādinātha”; the twenty‑fourth appears as “Vaddhamāṇa” in Prakrit and “Mahāvīra” in Sanskrit and modern usage. [[0]](https://jainmission.theywon.in/24-teerthankar-names/) The addition of the honorific “‑nātha” (“lord”) to many Sanskrit forms—Ajitanātha, Sumatinātha, Mallinātha—represents a later devotional and literary development rather than a change in the underlying identity of the Tirthankara.\n\nSectarian traditions (Digambara, Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka, Sthānakavāsī, Terāpanthī) generally agree on the identities of the twenty‑four Tirthankaras but may prefer slightly different spellings or emphases. Lists of Tirthankaras in iconographic catalogues, pilgrimage guides, and educational materials show minor orthographic differences—“Suvidhinātha” versus “Puṣpadanta,” “Śreyāṃsanātha” versus “Śreyansanātha”—while preserving the same sequence and attributes. [[1]](https://encyclopediaofjainism.com/the-jinas-their-names-and-symbols/) [[2]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/gallery/archaeological-museum-sarnath-gallery-4/12092) Regional languages further diversify these forms: Hindi and Gujarati often drop diacritics and simplify consonant clusters (e.g., “Parshvanath,” “Shantinath”), while South Indian languages adapt the names to local phonology.\n\nIn modern scholarship and digital resources, Roman transliteration introduces another layer of variation. Systems such as IAST, ISO, and simplified academic transliteration differ in how they represent retroflex consonants, long vowels, and nasalization, producing forms like “Rishabha,” “Ṛṣabha,” or “Ṛṣabha.” The same applies to doctrinal terms (e.g., “karmāśrava” vs. “karmasrava”) and place names (e.g., “Sammed Śikhar,” “Sammeta Śikhara,” “Sammed Shikharji”). These differences are usually technical rather than theological, but they can affect searchability, cataloguing, and cross‑tradition comparison.\n\nFrom a historical perspective, name variants preserve traces of Jainism’s movement through time and space. Prakrit forms reflect early canonical and narrative strata; Sanskrit forms align Jain literature with broader pan‑Indian scholastic culture; vernacular and Roman forms mark the tradition’s engagement with regional communities and global scholarship. Careful study of these variants—always anchored in primary textual witnesses—helps avoid confusion, supports accurate citation, and reveals how Jains themselves have continually re‑articulated their sacred persons and concepts in new linguistic environments.",

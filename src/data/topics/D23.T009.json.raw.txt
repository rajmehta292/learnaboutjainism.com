  "overview": "Sambhavanatha (often spelled Sambhavnath or Sambhavanath) is revered in Jain tradition as the third Tirthankara (ford-maker) of the current descending time-cycle (avasarpini) in Bharat Kshetra.\n\nIn both major Jain sects—Digambara and Śvetāmbara—Sambhavanatha is presented as a paradigmatic royal renouncer: a king of the Ikṣvāku lineage who turns from sovereignty to asceticism, attains kevala-jñāna (omniscience), and finally achieves mokṣa (liberation) as a Siddha. Standard narrative frameworks place his birth in the city of Śrāvastī to King Jitari (or Dṛḍharāja/Vimalvahan in some recensions) and Queen Susenā/Sena, emphasizing his golden bodily hue and extraordinary height, measured in traditional units such as 400 dhanusa. His emblem (lāñchana) in iconography is the horse, carved on the pedestal of his images and used as the primary visual identifier in temples and manuscripts.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sambhavanatha) [[1]](https://encyclopediaofjainism.com/lord-sambhavnath/)\n\nJain universal history situates Sambhavanatha within a long karmic trajectory. Earlier lives—such as King Vipulvahan/Vipulbal in Mahāvideha or Airāvata regions—are described as marked by intense compassion, generosity during famine, and deep detachment upon witnessing the impermanence of clouds and rain. These qualities are said to have led to the binding of Tirthankara-nāma-karma, the specific karmic configuration that results in Tirthankara birth. After rebirth in the Ikṣvāku dynasty, auspicious dreams and signs accompany his conception and birth, and the prosperity of the land is interpreted as a reflection of the purity of the soul in the queen’s womb—hence the name “Sambhavanatha,” linked to the idea that what seemed impossible (asambhava) became possible (sambhava).  [[1]](https://encyclopediaofjainism.com/lord-sambhavnath/) [[2]](https://jainmuseum.com/history-of-sambhavnath-bhagwan.htm)\n\nAccounts of his renunciation describe a long period of righteous rule in Śrāvastī, followed by a decisive moment of spiritual turning when he contemplates the transience of worldly phenomena. He then undertakes dīkṣā (formal initiation into ascetic life), often said to occur in a forest such as Sahetuk, under a silk-cotton or śālmali tree, accompanied by many other kings who renounce alongside him. After years of rigorous tapas (austerity) and silence, Sambhavanatha attains kevala-jñāna beneath a śāla or śālmali tree, where Indras (heavenly beings) construct a samavasaraṇa, the universal preaching hall. There he expounds the Jain path of right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct to vast assemblies of monks, nuns, laymen, and laywomen, with chief disciples (gaṇadharas) such as Charudatta/Charusen and notable female disciples like Śyāmā mentioned in some Śvetāmbara sources.  [[1]](https://encyclopediaofjainism.com/lord-sambhavnath/) [[3]](https://sambhavnath.com/index.html)\n\nHis nirvāṇa (final liberation) is consistently located at Sammed Śikharji (Mount Śikharji) in present-day Jharkhand, a major Jain pilgrimage site where many Tirthankaras are believed to have attained mokṣa. Traditional chronologies assign him an immensely long lifespan—millions of purvas—reflecting the cosmological scale of Jain time rather than historical dating. Sectarian narrative compendia differ on exact calendrical details (e.g., Margashirsha vs. Kārtika birth dates, specific tithis for kevala-jñāna and nirvāṇa), but they agree on his status as a Siddha who has completely destroyed all karmas.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sambhavanatha) [[1]](https://encyclopediaofjainism.com/lord-sambhavnath/) [[2]](https://jainmuseum.com/history-of-sambhavnath-bhagwan.htm)\n\nIn art and ritual, Sambhavanatha’s image is typically shown in meditation (padmāsana or kāyotsarga) with a serene golden complexion, the horse emblem, and attendant yakṣa-yakṣiṇī deities such as Trimukha (a three-faced yakṣa) and Pragya/Pragyapti. Temples and monuments dedicated to him appear across India: inscriptions and local traditions connect him to sites like Śobhnath at Śrāvastī, shrines in Ranthambore Fort (Rajasthan), and the Chaturmukha Basadi at Karkala (Karnataka), as well as large single-stone idols in Gujarat. These material traces show how the narrative of Sambhavanatha has been woven into regional devotional landscapes, manuscript traditions, and museum collections, while remaining anchored in the broader Jain theological framework of Tirthankaras as exemplars of non-violence, truthfulness, non-attachment, and spiritual awakening.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Sambhavanatha) [[3]](https://sambhavnath.com/index.html) [[2]](https://jainmuseum.com/history-of-sambhavnath-bhagwan.htm)\n\nFrom a textual-historical perspective, Sambhavanatha’s life-story is preserved not in a single canonical biography but across multiple layers of literature: sectarian Āgamas (especially in Śvetāmbara tradition), narrative compendia like Hemacandra’s Trīśaṣṭiśalākāpuruṣacaritra, Digambara purāṇas, and later encyclopedic and pedagogical works. These sources sometimes diverge on numerical details (e.g., counts of gaṇadharas, śrāvakas, śrāvikās) and specific calendrical data, and modern scholars and monastic authors explicitly acknowledge such variations. As a result, contemporary study of Sambhavanatha benefits from careful comparison of primary texts, commentaries, and regional traditions, with an awareness of where evidence is strong, where it is interpretive, and where transmission has introduced uncertainty or variant readings.

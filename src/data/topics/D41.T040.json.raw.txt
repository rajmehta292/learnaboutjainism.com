  "overview": "Varanasi—also known as Kāśī or Banaras—is one of the most important sacred cities in North India and holds a distinctive place in Jain tradition alongside its more widely known Hindu associations. Jain narratives, pilgrimage manuals, and temple histories present Varanasi not only as an ancient urban centre on the Ganga but as a landscape marked by the lives and kalyāṇakas (auspicious life-events) of several Tīrthaṅkaras.\n\nIn Jain sources, Varanasi is associated especially with four Tīrthaṅkaras: Suparśvanātha (7th), Śreyāṃsanātha (11th), Candraprabha (8th), and Pārśvanātha (23rd). Several texts and later pilgrimage traditions locate their births and other kalyāṇakas in the wider Kāśī region, making the city a multi-Tīrthaṅkara kṣetra rather than a single-figure shrine. [[0]](https://books.google.com/books/about/Jaina_Contribution_to_Varanasi.html?id=YoLXAAAAMAAJ) [[1]](https://www.jainheritagecentres.com/jainism-in-india/uttar-pradesh/varanasi-kashi/) The city is also linked to the activity of Bhagavān Mahāvīra, who is said to have visited and preached in Varanasi and nearby Sarnath, further strengthening its status as a Jain dharma-kṣetra. [[1]](https://www.jainheritagecentres.com/jainism-in-india/uttar-pradesh/varanasi-kashi/) [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parshvanath_Jain_temples%2C_Varanasi)\n\nFrom a historical and architectural perspective, Varanasi contains a network of Digambara and Śvetāmbara temples, upāśrayas (monastic lecture halls), and shrines that have grown over several centuries. Modern surveys count roughly two dozen Jain temples in and around the city, with especially prominent sites at Bhelupur (Bhelapur), Ram Ghāṭ, and other neighbourhoods near the riverfront. [[1]](https://www.jainheritagecentres.com/jainism-in-india/uttar-pradesh/varanasi-kashi/) [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parshvanath_Jain_temples%2C_Varanasi) The Parśvanātha Jain temples at Bhelupur form a major pilgrimage complex shared by both Digambara and Śvetāmbara communities, housing a black Digambara idol of Pārśvanātha dated to around the 9th–11th century and a white Śvetāmbara idol of similar size. [[1]](https://www.jainheritagecentres.com/jainism-in-india/uttar-pradesh/varanasi-kashi/) [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parshvanath_Jain_temples%2C_Varanasi) These temples are integrated into wider circuits of Jain pilgrimage that connect Varanasi with other North Indian centres such as Sarnath, Shikharji, and Ayodhya.\n\nEarly modern and contemporary Śvetāmbara monastic lineages have played a significant role in shaping the Jain presence in Varanasi. Studies of temple-building in the city highlight figures such as Kuśalacandrasūri of the Kharataragaccha, who was initiated in Varanasi in 1778 and spent his life as a yati (temple-dwelling monk) at the Cintāmaṇi Pārśvanātha temple near Ram Ghāṭ, and Ācārya Rājayaśasūri of the Tapāgaccha, active in the later 20th century. [[3]](https://www.mdpi.com/2077-1444/11/3/117) [[4]](https://www.academia.edu/42205855/Situating_P%C4%81r%C5%9Bvas_Biography_in_Varanasi) Drawing on merchant patronage and narrative traditions about Pārśvanātha—especially episodes involving the protection of snakes and the goddess Padmāvatī—these leaders sponsored substantial temple and shrine projects that inscribed Jain sacred geography into a city otherwise perceived primarily through Hindu lenses.\n\nJain literary and educational contributions also connect Varanasi to broader intellectual currents. Modern scholarship, such as Pranati Ghosal’s \"Jaina Contribution to Varanasi\", documents how Jain communities have participated in the city’s religious, philosophical, artistic, and educational life, including the production and preservation of manuscripts, images, and doctrinal works. [[0]](https://books.google.com/books/about/Jaina_Contribution_to_Varanasi.html?id=YoLXAAAAMAAJ) Within Jain ontology, Varanasi can be understood as a multi-layered sacred space: a site of Tīrthaṅkara kalyāṇakas, a centre of monastic and lay activity, and a node in networks of trade and patronage that enabled temple-building and textual transmission.\n\nAt the same time, Jain tradition does not treat Varanasi as exclusively Jain or exclusively Hindu. Instead, it is one example of a shared sacred city where multiple communities construct overlapping narratives and ritual geographies. Jain accounts emphasize ahiṃsā, spiritual discipline, and the exemplary lives of the Jinas, while coexisting with other religious imaginations of Kāśī. For contemporary Jain laypeople and renunciants, visiting Varanasi’s Jain temples—especially the Parśvanātha complex at Bhelupur and shrines associated with Padmāvatī—forms part of a broader practice of tīrtha-yātrā (pilgrimage), undertaken within the ethical and health guidelines of their respective traditions and, where necessary, in consultation with qualified teachers and medical professionals.",

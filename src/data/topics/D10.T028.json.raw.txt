  "overview": "The Jain–Mīmāṃsā debate refers to a sustained intellectual confrontation between Jain philosophers and the Pūrva-Mīmāṃsā school of Vedic exegesis, focused above all on the status of scriptural authority, the nature of omniscience (sarvajñatā), and the foundations of moral knowledge. Emerging prominently from about the 6th–8th centuries CE, this debate unfolded in Sanskrit scholastic literature, where Jains defended the possibility and necessity of a living omniscient teacher (tīrthaṅkara or kevalin), while Mīmāṃsakas upheld the Veda as the unique, authorless (apauruṣeya) and self-validating source of dharma. [[0]](https://www.oxfordreference.com/view/10.1093/oi/authority.20110803100159113) [[1]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362951218_The_Jain-Mimamsa_Debate_on_Omniscience/fulltext/6308cde2acd814437fdaca18/The-Jain-Mimamsa-Debate-on-Omniscience.pdf)\n\nFor Mīmāṃsā, the central concern is dharma known through Vedic injunctions (vidhi). Jaimini’s Mīmāṃsā Sūtra opens with “athāto dharma-jijñāsā”—“now, therefore, the inquiry into dharma”—and the tradition maintains that the Veda, being eternal and unauthored, is infallible and sufficient for knowing what ought to be done. The doctrine of apūrva, an unseen potency generated by correctly performed ritual action, guarantees the causal link between sacrifice and its promised results (such as heaven), without recourse to a personal deity. In this framework, revelation is impersonal, textual, and complete; there is no need for a living omniscient person whose testimony could override or supplement Vedic authority. [[2]](https://ir.vidyasagar.ac.in/bitstream/123456789/7947/1/10_Dr.%20Mukul%20Bala.pdf) [[0]](https://www.oxfordreference.com/view/10.1093/oi/authority.20110803100159113)\n\nJain thinkers, by contrast, affirm that human beings can attain kevala-jñāna, perfect and unobstructed knowledge, through the purification of the soul and the destruction of karmic bondage. The Jain path presupposes the historical reality of omniscient tīrthaṅkaras whose direct experience of reality grounds both scripture (āgama) and right conduct. In the Jain view, scriptural authority is ultimately derivative: texts are reliable because they preserve the teachings of omniscient beings, not because they are authorless. This leads Jains to challenge the Mīmāṃsā claim that an impersonal, unauthored Veda can be the sole and supreme source of dharma, and to argue instead that living omniscience provides a higher epistemic standpoint from which scriptural statements can be assessed, interpreted, or even corrected. [[1]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362951218_The_Jain-Mimamsa_Debate_on_Omniscience/fulltext/6308cde2acd814437fdaca18/The-Jain-Mimamsa-Debate-on-Omniscience.pdf)\n\nThe debate on omniscience thus becomes a debate on epistemology and hermeneutics. Mīmāṃsakas develop sophisticated theories of perception, inference, and linguistic meaning to defend the self-evidence and completeness of Vedic injunctions. They question whether claims of omniscience are logically coherent or empirically verifiable, and whether positing an omniscient person introduces unnecessary metaphysical assumptions beyond the already sufficient authority of the Veda. Jain philosophers respond by analyzing the conditions for valid testimony (śabda-pramāṇa), arguing that the testimony of an omniscient being is uniquely free from error and partiality, and by showing that ordinary scriptural texts, being mediated by fallible transmitters, require an ultimate, non-textual foundation in omniscient cognition. [[0]](https://www.oxfordreference.com/view/10.1093/oi/authority.20110803100159113) [[1]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362951218_The_Jain-Mimamsa_Debate_on_Omniscience/fulltext/6308cde2acd814437fdaca18/The-Jain-Mimamsa-Debate-on-Omniscience.pdf)\n\nHistorically, this controversy is part of a wider Indian discussion about reason and revelation, and about the sources of religious and moral knowledge. As modern scholarship has emphasized, the Jain–Mīmāṃsā debate parallels, in certain respects, Western disputes over the relation between natural reason and revealed scripture, yet it unfolds within a shared Sanskritic intellectual world where both sides accept karma, rebirth, and the pursuit of liberation or higher states. The disagreement is not over whether there is moral law, but over how that law is known: through an eternal, authorless text, or through the realized knowledge of living or historical omniscient beings. The debate therefore illuminates Jain self-understanding as a non-Vedic, yet textually sophisticated tradition, and clarifies Mīmāṃsā’s commitment to ritual, linguistic analysis, and the autonomy of scriptural dharma. [[1]](https://www.researchgate.net/publication/362951218_The_Jain-Mimamsa_Debate_on_Omniscience/fulltext/6308cde2acd814437fdaca18/The-Jain-Mimamsa-Debate-on-Omniscience.pdf)\n\nIn modern interpretation, the Jain–Mīmāṃsā debate also raises broader ethical and philosophical questions. If moral obligation is grounded in impersonal textual injunctions, as Mīmāṃsā suggests, then ethics becomes a matter of procedural correctness and rule-following. If, as Jains maintain, moral insight can be perfected in an omniscient person, then ethics can be oriented toward an ideal of complete understanding and compassionate guidance. Contemporary scholars use this classical debate to explore themes such as the nature of authority, the possibility of perfect knowledge, and the tension between scripturalism and experiential realization, showing how Jain and Mīmāṃsā positions continue to inform discussions of religious pluralism and the status of tradition in modern moral philosophy.

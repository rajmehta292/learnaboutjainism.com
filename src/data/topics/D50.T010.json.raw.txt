  "overview": "Art history is the systematic study of visual and material culture across time, place, and tradition. It examines how images, objects, buildings, and performances are produced, used, and interpreted, and how they embody social, religious, political, and economic meanings. In modern academia, art history combines close visual analysis with historical research, philology, anthropology, and philosophy to understand both the formal qualities of works of art and the worlds that created them.\n\nWithin the Jain tradition, art history focuses on temples, statuary, manuscript painting, ritual objects, and pilgrimage images that express core doctrines such as non‑violence (ahimsa), non‑attachment (aparigraha), and non‑absolutism (anekantavada). Jain communities have patronized major architectural complexes, including sites like Mount Shatrunjaya and Shikharji, as well as urban temples and monastic libraries (bhandars). These spaces are not merely decorative; they are structured environments for worship, scriptural recitation, and ethical reflection, and their visual programs often follow scriptural and shilpa‑śāstra (art‑technical) guidelines.\n\nA distinctive contribution of Jain art history is the study of illustrated manuscripts, especially from western India (Gujarat and Rajasthan) between the 11th and 18th centuries. Palm‑leaf and later paper manuscripts of texts such as the Kalpasutra were produced with painted folios and carved wooden covers (patli), often commissioned by merchant patrons and royal dynasties like the Chaulukyas (Solankis). These manuscripts were used in temple rituals such as jñāna‑pūjā (worship of knowledge) and remain central to festivals like Paryushana, where the Kalpasutra is recited and venerated.  [[0]](https://www.metmuseum.org/essays/jain-manuscript-painting) [[1]](https://artsandculture.google.com/story/nwUxc4-Wwf3BwA?hl=en)\n\nArt historians also study Jain iconography—the codified visual representation of Tirthankaras and other figures. Over centuries, distinct sectarian conventions emerged between Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions, and within Śvetāmbara sub‑sects such as Mūrtipūjaka, Sthānakavāsī, and Terāpanth. Recent scholarship has mapped how attributes like posture, clothing or nudity, attendant deities, and emblem (lāñchana) vary across regions and periods, drawing on canonical texts, shilpa‑śāstra literature, inscriptions, and field observation.  [[2]](https://english.dainikjagranmpcg.com/new-scholarly-work-maps-the-evolution-of-jain-tirthankara-iconography/article-18814) [[3]](https://jainastudies.soas.ac.uk/ijjs/ijjs-2204-2026.pdf)\n\nAnother important field is the study of tirtha‑paṭa—large, often cartographic paintings of sacred sites used especially in Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka practice. These works depict mountains, temple clusters, narrative scenes, and ritual activities associated with pilgrimage places, and are treated as visual embodiments (avatāra) of the tirtha itself. Displayed in temples and homes, they facilitate bhāva‑yātrā, an internal or emotional pilgrimage understood to carry spiritual merit comparable to physical travel. Contemporary research uses object‑based analysis, interviews with artists, and observation of rituals to reconstruct the history, functions, and meanings of these images.  [[4]](https://openresearch-repository.anu.edu.au/items/09f47065-2f32-417e-8b29-17b58a462655/full)\n\nBeyond specifically Jain materials, art history as a discipline engages with broader questions: how patronage shapes artistic production; how trade routes and migrations transmit styles; how technologies (from stone carving to digital imaging) transform visual culture; and how modern museums, archives, and digital platforms preserve, interpret, or sometimes distort religious art. For Jain communities, these questions intersect with concerns about accurate representation of doctrine, ethical treatment of sacred images, and the preservation of manuscripts and sculptures in both temple bhandars and public institutions.\n\nMethodologically, Jain art history requires careful distinction between primary sources (canonical texts, inscriptions, original artworks), traditional commentaries, and modern scholarship. It also demands sensitivity to living practice: images are not only historical artifacts but ongoing objects of devotion. Responsible study therefore combines textual citation, visual analysis, and consultation with qualified teachers and practitioners, while acknowledging areas of uncertainty, contested interpretation, and gaps in the surviving material record.",

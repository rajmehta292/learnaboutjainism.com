  "overview": "In Jainism, care for mendicants (sādhus and sādhvīs) is a central dimension of lay religious life and an institutional pillar of the fourfold community (caturvidha saṅgha: monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen). The relationship is structured not as patronage in a worldly sense, but as a carefully regulated form of service (sevā) and sharing (saṁvibhāga) grounded in ahiṁsā, humility, and spiritual reciprocity. Mendicants renounce possessions, earning, and direct participation in economic life; lay followers (śrāvakas and śrāvikās) therefore assume responsibility for their material support—food, shelter, clothing, medicines—under strict ethical and ritual constraints.\n\nClassical Jain texts describe this support primarily through the categories of dāna (giving) and ātithisaṁvibhāga (sharing with worthy guests), where mendicants are paradigmatic recipients. In Śrāvakaprajñapti (Savay Paṇṇatti) and related śrāvaka-ācāra works, the training vow of ātithisaṁvibhāga is explicitly framed as a discipline of offering to monks and nuns, alongside other deserving beings. Care is not merely material: it includes listening to sermons, requesting instruction, protecting mendicants from harm, and maintaining conditions conducive to their vows—such as arranging non-violent food, safe lodgings, and transport that minimizes injury to living beings. [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/savay_pannatti_022026_022026)\n\nThe mendicant–lay relationship is governed by mutual obligations. Mendicants must remain detached, avoid demanding or choosing luxurious offerings, and accept only what is given within the rules of their lineage and sect. Lay donors must give without expectation of worldly reward, with right faith (samyagdarśana), and with attention to the purity of means: wealth obtained through violence, deceit, or exploitation is considered spiritually problematic as a basis for dāna. Texts also emphasize the importance of intention—gentleness, reverence, and non-coercive giving—as well as the avoidance of harm in the act of care itself (for example, preparing food in ways that minimize injury to micro-organisms).\n\nAcross Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions, the concrete forms of care differ—especially regarding clothing, food rules, and modes of travel—but the underlying principles remain consistent: support should enable mendicants to pursue the path of liberation (mokṣa) with minimal worldly entanglement. Historical and epigraphic evidence shows that Jain communities developed complex systems of endowments, communal kitchens, and temple-based institutions to sustain mendicant orders, while also debating the limits of luxury and institutional wealth. Modern Jain communities continue to negotiate these questions, especially in urban contexts, balancing traditional ideals of simplicity with contemporary needs such as healthcare, education, and safe accommodation for mendicants.\n\nImportantly, care for mendicants is framed as spiritually beneficial for laypeople, but not as a transactional guarantee of merit. Rather, it is one expression of the broader Jain ethic of non-violence and interdependence: by sustaining those who embody renunciation and discipline, lay followers participate—indirectly—in the ascetic ideal, while remaining within the responsibilities of household life. Many texts therefore present attentive, non-harming, and modest care for mendicants as both a duty and an opportunity for deepening right faith, humility, and awareness of karma.",

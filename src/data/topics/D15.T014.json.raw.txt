  "overview": "Digambara nudity (digambaratva) refers to the distinctive practice of complete nakedness among male ascetics of the Digambara (“sky‑clad”) tradition of Jainism. The term itself comes from Sanskrit: “diś” (direction, quarters) and “ambara” (garment), meaning “clothed in the quarters of the sky.” In Digambara understanding, nudity is not a mere external custom but a radical expression of aparigraha (non‑possession) and complete renunciation of all material property, including clothing. It is seen as the visible sign that the monk has crossed the boundary of social convention and entered a life oriented solely toward liberation (mokṣa).  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digambara) [[1]](https://www.philtar.ac.uk/encyclopedia/jainism/digam.html)\n\nHistorically, the Digambara–Śvetāmbara difference over nudity crystallized around the question of whether a monk who owns anything—especially garments—can attain kevala‑jñāna (omniscience) and final liberation. Digambaras insist that true nirgrantha (bondless) ascetics must be without possessions and therefore naked; even a loincloth is considered a compromise. Śvetāmbaras, by contrast, allow white robes and argue that clothing, when held without attachment, does not obstruct liberation. Intermediate positions, such as the historical Yapaniya tradition, regarded nudity as the ideal path (utsarga‑mārga) but allowed clothing as an exception (apavāda‑mārga) in particular circumstances.  [[1]](https://www.philtar.ac.uk/encyclopedia/jainism/digam.html) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jain_achar_me_achelakatva_aur_sachalekatva_ka_prashna_210586_210586)\n\nFor Digambaras, nudity is closely tied to the exemplary conduct of the Jinas (Tīrthaṅkaras). They maintain that Mahāvīra and the other Tīrthaṅkaras practiced complete nudity after initiation, and that later monks must follow this model as far as possible. Nudity symbolizes the stripping away of all social identity, pride, and shame, and it is interpreted as the external counterpart of inner detachment from rāga and dveṣa (attachment and aversion). The naked body is meant to be “transparent” to the world—nothing can be hidden, so hypocrisy and secret indulgence are theoretically minimized. Traditional authors also argue that dirty clothing attracts living organisms and may lead to their injury, whereas a naked monk, practicing vigilance, can better avoid harm to tiny creatures.  [[1]](https://www.philtar.ac.uk/encyclopedia/jainism/digam.html) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/digambaratva_aur_digambar_muni_090155_090155)\n\nIn practice, Digambara nudity is restricted to fully initiated male munis (monks). They carry almost no personal objects, typically only a community‑owned picchī (a broom of fallen peacock feathers) and sometimes a water gourd, both justified as instruments of ahiṃsā (non‑violence) rather than possessions. They beg for food, eat once a day, and avoid bathing to reduce harm to water‑dwelling organisms. Female ascetics in the Digambara tradition (āryikās) are clothed; the tradition holds that social conditions and modesty norms make female nudity impracticable, and doctrinally it teaches that women must be reborn as men to attain liberation. These gendered rules around nudity are a major point of contrast with Śvetāmbara communities, which recognize fully liberated women and clothe both monks and nuns in white.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Digambara) [[1]](https://www.philtar.ac.uk/encyclopedia/jainism/digam.html) [[4]](https://universalium.en-academic.com/103876/Digambara)\n\nFrom a historical perspective, the emergence of Digambara nudity as a defining sectarian marker is linked to debates over famine migrations, monastic discipline, and the loss or preservation of early scriptures. Later Digambara narratives describe a southward migration under Bhadrabāhu, whose group maintained nudity, while northern monks under Sthūlabhadra adopted white garments; modern scholarship treats these accounts cautiously, noting their late composition and sectarian bias. By the first centuries CE, inscriptions and sculpture from sites such as Mathurā show both clothed and unclothed Jain ascetics, suggesting that practice was diverse before the sectarian lines hardened. The Council of Valabhī (5th century CE), which codified Śvetāmbara scriptures without Digambara participation, marks a further stage in the differentiation of “sky‑clad” and “white‑clad” identities.  [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jain_achar_me_achelakatva_aur_sachalekatva_ka_prashna_210586_210586) [[4]](https://universalium.en-academic.com/103876/Digambara)\n\nModern discussions of Digambara nudity engage ethical, social, and legal questions. Within the tradition, authors emphasize that genuine digambaratva is not only physical nakedness but also inner purity—a “naked mind” free from passions and deceit. They defend the practice as a disciplined, voluntary renunciation undertaken by a very small number of highly trained ascetics, not as exhibitionism or protest. At the same time, contemporary Digambara communities must navigate state regulations, public sensibilities, and changing urban environments, which can limit where and how nude ascetics move and reside. Scholarly and inter‑sectarian dialogues continue to explore how ancient ideals of non‑possession and radical renunciation can be interpreted in ways that respect both religious freedom and the dignity and safety of all involved.

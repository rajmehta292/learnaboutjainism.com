  "overview": "Śīlāṅka (often transliterated as Shilanka or Silanka) is a prominent Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka Jain monk and Sanskrit commentator of the 9th century CE, best known for his extensive exegesis on canonical āgamas such as the Ācārāṅga-sūtra and Sūtrakṛtāṅga-sūtra. [[0]](https://journals.uni-lj.si/as/article/download/249/308/0) [[1]](https://www.jaina-prosopography.org/person/772) He belongs to the Nirvṛtikula lineage and is remembered in prosopographical records with honorifics such as tattvāditya, indicating his reputation as a ‘sun of doctrine’ within the tradition. [[1]](https://www.jaina-prosopography.org/person/772)\n\nHistorically, Śīlāṅka stands at a crucial moment in the development of Jain scholasticism, when Prakrit canonical texts were increasingly being interpreted through Sanskrit commentaries. His works participate in the broader movement, noted by modern scholars, in which Jain exegetical literature from roughly the 8th to 13th centuries sought to clarify, systematize, and defend the meaning of the āgamas in dialogue with contemporary philosophical currents. [[0]](https://journals.uni-lj.si/as/article/download/249/308/0) [[2]](https://jainqq.org/explore/001578/90) Śīlāṅka’s commentaries are thus both philological—explaining difficult words, grammar, and syntax—and doctrinal, elaborating on ethics, metaphysics, and soteriology.\n\nProsopographical data suggest that Śīlāṅka was active around Śaka 798 (approximately 876 CE), with Gambhūtā/Gāmbhu in Gujarat mentioned as a key location associated with his authorship of the Ācārāṅgavṛtti and other works. [[1]](https://www.jaina-prosopography.org/person/772) He is described as a temple-dwelling (caityavāsin) sūri and ācārya, indicating both his monastic seniority and his role as a teacher and commentator. Traditional records also connect him as a disciple within the line stemming from Jinabhadra, a major earlier commentator, which situates Śīlāṅka inside a living chain of exegetical transmission rather than as an isolated intellectual figure. [[1]](https://www.jaina-prosopography.org/person/772)\n\nŚīlāṅka’s commentary on the Ācārāṅga-sūtra is especially significant because the Ācārāṅga is regarded as one of the oldest and most authoritative Jain scriptures, focusing on right conduct (ācāra), non-violence (ahiṃsā), and the nature of the soul (jīva). Later studies of this commentary highlight how Śīlāṅka not only explains the literal meaning of the sutras but also integrates broader philosophical reflections, cultural references, and linguistic analysis, making his work a key resource for understanding early Jain ethics and ascetic ideals. [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/acharang_shilank_vrutti_ek_adhyayan_004238_004238) [[4]](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Johannes-Bronkhorst/publication/263470153_On_the_chronology_of_the_Tattvartha_Sutra_and_some_early_commentaries/links/0f31753a29169d94ad000000/On-the-chronology-of-the-Tattvartha-Sutra-and-some-early-commentaries.pdf) His style shows awareness of other Jain and non-Jain philosophical positions, and he occasionally engages in doctrinal clarification or defense, contributing to the consolidation of Śvetāmbara doctrinal identity.\n\nIn addition to the Ācārāṅgavṛtti, Śīlāṅka is credited with commentaries on the Sūtrakṛtāṅga and with participation in the tradition of commentary on the Viśeṣāvaśyakabhāṣya, further demonstrating his breadth as an exegete of canonical and ritual texts. [[1]](https://www.jaina-prosopography.org/person/772) [[2]](https://jainqq.org/explore/001578/90) Modern scholarship on Jain commentaries often cites Śīlāṅka when tracing the reception of key doctrinal notions such as tattva (fundamental principles), karma theory, and the classification of souls, showing that his works became reference points for later authors. At the same time, the surviving manuscripts and editions of his commentaries are still being critically studied, and some aspects of his biography and corpus remain debated or only partially documented, reminding us that our picture of Śīlāṅka is based on a combination of traditional lists, colophons, and modern textual research rather than a single definitive source.\n\nFrom the perspective of Jain religious life, Śīlāṅka’s importance lies less in personal authority and more in his role as a transmitter and interpreter of the canonical word. His commentaries help bridge the gap between terse Prakrit sutras and the lived practice of monks and laypeople, clarifying how foundational teachings on non-violence, restraint, and right knowledge were understood in the 9th century and beyond. For contemporary students of Jainism, Śīlāṅka offers a window into the intellectual world of medieval Śvetāmbara monastic communities, where scriptural study, philosophical debate, and careful linguistic analysis were integral to preserving and deepening the tradition.",

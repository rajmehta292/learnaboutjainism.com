  "overview": "The Kesi–Gautama dialogue is a pivotal narrative preserved in the twenty‑third lecture of the Uttarādhyayana Sūtra, a Śvetāmbara canonical text traditionally attributed to Gaṇadhara Sudharma Svāmī. It stages a meeting between two senior mendicants from different Jain lineages: Kesi (Kēśi), the chief disciple of the earlier Jina Pārśva, and Gautama (Gautama Svāmī), the foremost disciple of Jina Vardhamāna Mahāvīra. Set in Śrāvastī, in the parks of Tinduka and Koṣṭhaka, the story dramatizes questions that had arisen among their respective pupils about differences in doctrine and practice between the Pārśva‑tradition and the Mahāvīra‑tradition.\n\nThe narrative opens by praising Pārśva as an omniscient Arhat and \"Light of the World\" and introducing Kesi as a learned śramaṇa endowed with śruta‑jñāna and avadhi‑jñāna, wandering from village to village until he arrives at Śrāvastī. In parallel, it introduces Mahāvīra as the current Jina and Gautama as his eminent disciple, master of the twelve Aṅgas and surrounded by many pupils. Both mendicants take up residence in nearby parks, living with careful self‑protection (guptis) and discipline. Their pupils, observing the coexistence of two venerable traditions, begin to wonder whether their own Law (dhamma) or the other is truly correct, and why there are differences if both paths aim at the same spiritual goal. [[0]](https://sacred-texts.com/jai/sbe45/sbe4525.htm) [[1]](https://www.herenow4u.net/index.php?id=108644)\n\nTwo specific doctrinal issues frame the dialogue. First, Kesi asks why Pārśva’s Law recognizes only four vows (cāujjāma), whereas Mahāvīra’s Law enjoins five vows. Second, he asks why Mahāvīra’s Law forbids clothing (a‑celaga), while Pārśva’s allows an upper and lower garment (sa‑antara‑uttara). Both systems, he notes, pursue the same end of liberation, so he questions the cause of this difference and whether Gautama has any misgivings about a \"two‑fold Law.\" Gautama responds by emphasizing the role of the Tīrthaṅkaras’ superior knowledge in adapting discipline to changing times and circumstances. He explains that outward marks such as clothing are secondary; the true causes of liberation are right knowledge, right faith, and right conduct, not external signs. In this way, he defends the five‑vow system and stricter nudity discipline as appropriate for the current age, while affirming continuity of the essential spiritual aims. [[0]](https://sacred-texts.com/jai/sbe45/sbe4525.htm) [[2]](https://www.jstor.org/stable/23497346)\n\nThe dialogue culminates in Kesi’s doubts being dispelled. The text reports that his \"misgivings were cut off\" and that he abandons the former four‑vow Law to adopt the five‑vow discipline taught by Mahāvīra, effectively being re‑initiated as a mendicant disciple of Gautama. This narrative has been read within the tradition as a reconciliation and unification of earlier and later Jain lineages, presenting Mahāvīra’s teaching as a legitimate development rather than a rupture. In modern scholarship, the Kesi–Gautama dialogue has become a key source for understanding early sectarian debates, especially regarding the number of vows and the question of clothing, which later crystallized into the distinction between Śvetāmbara (white‑clad) and Digambara (sky‑clad) mendicants. Padmanabh S. Jaini, for example, uses this passage as a starting point for tracing the history of Śvetāmbara–Digambara controversies over mendicant nudity and canonical authority. [[2]](https://www.jstor.org/stable/23497346) [[3]](https://link.springer.com/content/pdf/10.1007/s10781-007-9020-x.pdf)\n\nFrom a doctrinal perspective, the dialogue illustrates several important Jain themes. It highlights the centrality of the five great vows (mahāvratas) in Mahāvīra’s system—non‑violence, truthfulness, non‑stealing, celibacy, and non‑attachment—while acknowledging an earlier four‑vow formulation associated with Pārśva. It underscores the Jain emphasis on internal transformation over external symbols, insisting that liberation depends on the triad of right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct rather than on clothing or other visible markers. It also models respectful debate and resolution between mendicants, with Kesi approaching Gautama courteously, offering him a proper seat, and engaging in dialogue before accepting a revised discipline. As such, the Kesi–Gautama dialogue functions both as a narrative of lineage integration and as a didactic text on how to navigate apparent differences in practice while preserving the continuity of the Jain path.\n\nHistorically, the dialogue is preserved only in the Śvetāmbara Uttarādhyayana Sūtra and is not recognized as canonical by Digambara traditions, which affects how different communities treat its authority. Within Śvetāmbara circles, however, it has been influential in shaping understandings of the relationship between Pārśva and Mahāvīra, the evolution of monastic rules, and the legitimacy of clothed mendicancy. Modern translations—such as Hermann Jacobi’s English rendering in the Sacred Books of the East and contemporary online editions—have made the text widely accessible, though they involve interpretive choices and occasional uncertainties in Prakrit reconstruction. Careful study therefore benefits from consulting multiple editions, traditional commentaries, and qualified teachers to appreciate both the narrative and its doctrinal implications.",

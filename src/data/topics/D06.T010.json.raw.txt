  "overview": "In Jain philosophy, \"ghātiyā karmas\" (destructive or harming karmas) are the four categories of karmic matter that directly impair the intrinsic qualities of the soul—its knowledge, perception, right faith, and energy. They are contrasted with \"aghātiyā karmas\" (non‑destructive karmas), which shape the external conditions of embodiment but do not alter the essential nature of the soul. The doctrine of ghātiyā karmas is central to classical karma theory and to the technical description of the path to liberation (mokṣa), especially in the Tattvārthasūtra, a text accepted by both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions.  [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084869.html) [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Types_of_Karma_%28Jainism%29)\n\nThe four ghātiyā karmas are: (1) jñānāvaraṇīya karma—knowledge‑obscuring karma; (2) darśanāvaraṇīya karma—perception‑obscuring karma; (3) mohanīya karma—deluding karma; and (4) antarāya karma—obstructive or power‑obstructing karma. These are classified as \"harming\" because they directly veil or distort the soul’s innate capacities. Jñānāvaraṇīya karma covers the various forms of knowledge (mati, śruta, avadhi, manaḥparyaya, and kevala‑jñāna), preventing full cognition of reality. Darśanāvaraṇīya karma obscures the modes of perception (such as ocular, non‑ocular, and omniscient perception), limiting the soul’s ability to \"see\" truth. Mohanīya karma is called the \"king\" of karmas in many expositions; it produces wrong belief (mithyātva) and passions (kaṣāyas), thereby generating attachment and aversion. Antarāya karma obstructs the manifestation of the soul’s energy, creating hindrances in giving, receiving, enjoying, and exerting spiritual effort.  [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Types_of_Karma_%28Jainism%29) [[2]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084871.html) [[3]](https://jainsutra.org/fundamentals/ghati-aghati-v4)\n\nFrom the standpoint of bondage (bandha), ghātiyā karmas are part of the eightfold prakṛti‑bandha enumerated in Tattvārthasūtra 8.4: jñānāvaraṇa, darśanāvaraṇa, vedanīya, mohanīya, āyuḥ, nāma, gotra, and antarāya. Among these, the first, second, fourth, and eighth are classified as ghātiyā because they directly affect the soul’s qualities, while the remaining four are aghātiyā, determining lifespan, body, status, and feelings. The commentarial tradition explains that karmic matter, once attracted and bound due to activities (yoga) and passions (kaṣāya), differentiates into these eight types according to the soul’s modifications. Ghātiyā karmas thus represent the most profound level of spiritual obstruction, and their destruction is a decisive milestone on the path to omniscience.  [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084869.html) [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Types_of_Karma_%28Jainism%29)\n\nIn the technical description of the stages of spiritual development (guṇasthāna), ghātiyā karmas play a crucial role. Tattvārthasūtra 10.1 states that omniscience (kevala‑jñāna) arises when deluding karma (moha) is destroyed and when knowledge‑obscuring, perception‑obscuring, and obstructive karmas are simultaneously annihilated. This means that the complete destruction of all four ghātiyā karmas marks the transition from a mundane soul (saṃsārī jīva) to an omniscient being (kevalin or arihant). Although the aghātiyā karmas may still remain until the end of the final embodied life, the soul’s essential qualities are fully manifest once ghātiyā karmas are eliminated. Liberation (mokṣa) is attained when, after omniscience, the remaining aghātiyā karmas are shed at the moment of final bodily death.  [[4]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084941.html) [[3]](https://jainsutra.org/fundamentals/ghati-aghati-v4)\n\nEach ghātiyā karma has numerous subtypes, reflecting the fine‑grained ways in which karmic matter can obstruct the soul. Tattvārthasūtra’s traditional enumeration speaks of 148 subtypes of karma in total, including five of jñānāvaraṇīya, nine of darśanāvaraṇīya, twenty‑eight of mohanīya, and five of antarāya. For example, the five knowledge‑obscuring karmas correspond to the five kinds of knowledge: sensory (mati), scriptural (śruta), clairvoyant (avadhi), telepathic (manaḥparyaya), and omniscient (kevala). The nine perception‑obscuring karmas relate to different modes and ranges of perception. Mohanīya karma is subdivided into faith‑deluding (darśana‑mohanīya) and conduct‑deluding (cāritra‑mohanīya), each with further sub‑categories linked to specific forms of wrong belief and passions. Antarāya karma includes obstructions to giving (dāna), receiving (lābha), enjoyment (bhoga and upabhoga), and exertion (vīrya).  [[1]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Types_of_Karma_%28Jainism%29) [[2]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084871.html)\n\nFrom a practical, lay‑practice perspective, ghātiyā karmas are not merely abstract categories but are tied to ethical conduct and inner transformation. Non‑violence (ahiṃsā), truthfulness (satya), non‑stealing (asteya), chastity (brahmacarya), and non‑possessiveness (aparigraha) are understood as disciplines that prevent new ghātiyā karmas from being bound and that help in the gradual shedding of existing ones. Meditative practices, scriptural study, and right faith (samyag‑darśana) are described as means to weaken mohanīya karma in particular, since delusion is seen as the root from which other harmful karmas gain strength. While technical texts provide detailed taxonomies, traditional teachers emphasize that the experiential reduction of anger, pride, deceit, and greed is the lived sign that ghātiyā karmas are being attenuated. \n\nModern expositions often use metaphors to explain ghātiyā karmas: jñānāvaraṇīya and darśanāvaraṇīya are likened to thick clouds covering the sun of knowledge and perception; mohanīya is compared to a distorting lens that makes one misjudge reality; and antarāya is described as a barrier that blocks the soul’s capacity to act on its wholesome intentions. These metaphors aim to preserve the classical structure while making the doctrine accessible to contemporary readers. However, the core idea remains consistent across sects and periods: ghātiyā karmas are the deepest obscurations of the soul, and the Jain path is fundamentally a process of recognizing, weakening, and ultimately destroying them through disciplined ethical and spiritual practice.",

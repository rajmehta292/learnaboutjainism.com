  "overview": "Yogabindu (Sanskrit: योगबिन्दु, “Drop of Yoga” or “Constituent Element of Yoga”) is a seminal Jain treatise on yoga traditionally attributed to the Śvetāmbara ācārya Haribhadra, often designated in modern scholarship as Haribhadra Virahāṅka. Composed in Sanskrit, probably between the 6th and 8th centuries CE, it stands alongside Haribhadra’s other yoga works—such as the Yogadṛṣṭisamuccaya and Yogaviṁśikā—as a foundational text for understanding how Jain thinkers engaged with, reinterpreted, and systematized yoga within a distinctly Jain soteriological framework. [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/yogabindu_011123_011123) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/prachin_stavanavli_23_parshwanath_032246_032246)\n\nThe central concern of Yogabindu is to define yoga as the path leading to mokṣa (liberation) and to articulate its structure in a way that both honors Jain doctrinal commitments and acknowledges the broader Indian yogic milieu. Haribhadra explicitly identifies yoga with the threefold Jain path of right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct (samyagdarśana, samyagjñāna, samyakcāritra), insisting that genuine yoga is whatever effectively connects the soul (jīva) to its liberated state. In contrast to purely psychological or posture-centered definitions, he emphasizes yoga as a comprehensive discipline of ethical conduct, meditative practice, and philosophical insight that purifies the soul of karmic bondage. [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/yogabindu_011123_011123) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/prachin_stavanavli_23_parshwanath_032246_032246)\n\nA distinctive feature of Yogabindu is its non-sectarian and comparative orientation. Haribhadra surveys definitions of yoga from other traditions—most notably Patañjali’s Yogasūtra, which defines yoga as the cessation of mental modifications (yogaḥ citta-vṛtti-nirodhaḥ)—and then reframes yoga as “that which joins the soul to mokṣa” (mokṣeṇa yojanād yogaḥ). This allows him to affirm the value of diverse yogic paths while maintaining a Jain doctrinal core. He argues that differences in metaphysical views, ritual practices, or social customs among Buddhists, Hindus, and Jains should not obscure the underlying unity of authentic yoga, which is always oriented toward liberation and grounded in moral discipline and meditative insight. [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/yogabindu_011123_011123) [[2]](https://www.arihantainstitute.org/blog/64-the-yoga-of-haribhadra-viraha-n-ka-s-yogabindu)\n\nStructurally, Yogabindu is organized into multiple sections that discuss the nature of the soul, bondage and liberation, the criteria for true yoga, and the graded path of spiritual practice. One influential doctrinal contribution is Haribhadra’s presentation of a five-limbed Jain yoga scheme—centered on adhyātma (contemplation of the true nature of the soul), bhāvanā (cultivation of contemplations such as impermanence and non-attachment), dhyāna (meditation), and related disciplines—rather than simply adopting Patañjali’s eightfold aṣṭāṅga-yoga. In doing so, he integrates Jain categories like guṇasthāna (stages of spiritual development) and kevala-jñāna (omniscience) with broader yogic terminology, even equating the Jain attainment of kevala with Patañjali’s asamprajñāta-samādhi in one notable verse. [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/prachin_stavanavli_23_parshwanath_032246_032246) [[2]](https://www.arihantainstitute.org/blog/64-the-yoga-of-haribhadra-viraha-n-ka-s-yogabindu)\n\nPhilosophically, Yogabindu defends a pluralistic yet realist Jain ontology. Haribhadra critiques monistic views—such as certain Advaita Vedānta positions that posit a single undifferentiated self—on the grounds that they render the distinction between bondage and liberation unintelligible. He insists on the multiplicity of souls, each with its own inherent capacity (yogyatā) for karmic connection and disconnection, and underscores that all phenomena, including divine favor, operate according to causal laws rather than arbitrary grace. Yoga, in this framework, is praised as a powerful purifier of the mind and a means to overcome suffering, desire, and vice, likened to a wish-fulfilling tree or jewel and to fire that refines gold. [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/yogabindu_011123_011123) [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/prachin_stavanavli_23_parshwanath_032246_032246)\n\nHistorically and doctrinally, Yogabindu occupies an important place in Jain intellectual history. It exemplifies anekāntavāda—the doctrine of manifold aspects—by harmonizing multiple philosophical perspectives while retaining a clear Jain identity. It also serves as a bridge text for comparative studies of yoga, showing how Jain thinkers appropriated and transformed concepts from Patañjali and other traditions into a Jain path of liberation. Modern editions and translations, such as K. K. Dixit’s 1968 English edition from the L. D. Institute of Indology and later Hindi translations with commentary, have made the text accessible to scholars and practitioners, enabling detailed study of Jain yoga as a sophisticated, dialogical engagement with the wider Indian philosophical world.

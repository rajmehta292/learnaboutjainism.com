  "overview": "Prashamarati (often found as Prashamrati, Prasamrati, or Prashamarati‑prakaran) is a Jain doctrinal treatise whose central concern is the cultivation of inner peace (prashama) and the joy that arises from the pacification of passions and karmic bondage. In the classical tradition it is associated with Ācārya Umāsvāti (also known as Umasvāti or Umasvāmī), the author of the Tattvārthādhigama‑sūtra, and is presented as a systematic exposition of the path that leads from worldly entanglement to the tranquil state of the liberated soul. [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/prasamrati_prakarana_022070_022070)\n\nThe text belongs to the genre of prakaraṇa‑grantha—topical manuals that gather and organize key doctrinal themes for study and contemplation. Prashamarati lays out, in metrical Sanskrit, the duties of householders and monks, the contemplations (bhāvanā), the fourteen stages of spiritual development (guṇasthāna), the six substances (dravya), the nine fundamental categories (nava‑tattva), and the logic of conditional predication (saptabhaṅgī). Its verses are meant not only to be understood intellectually but also to be recited and internalized as a form of svādhyāya (repeated scriptural study), gradually transforming the practitioner’s dispositions and weakening the hold of destructive karmas. [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/prasamrati_prakarana_022070_022070)\n\nAcross Śvetāmbara circles, Prashamarati is often studied alongside Tattvārthasūtra, functioning as a more devotional and practice‑oriented companion to that highly technical work. Later Jain authors and editors—such as Vijay Śrī Rajsekhar, Tapasvī Muni Akalankvijay, and Acharya Vijay Bhadraguptasuri—have produced Sanskrit and Gujarati editions, commentaries, and pedagogical adaptations of Prashamarati, sometimes expanding the material or re‑framing it for lay audiences and youth. These modern presentations emphasize that the “joys of peace of the soul” are accessible to anyone who earnestly engages with the text’s guidance, regardless of sectarian affiliation, and they frequently highlight its relevance for character‑building, ethical decision‑making, and meditative practice in contemporary life. [[1]](https://archive.org/details/in.ernet.dli.2015.314417) [[2]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Prashamrati_Prakaran_Romanized/022150) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/prashamrati_008922_008922)\n\nFrom a doctrinal standpoint, Prashamarati is important because it weaves together ontology (the nature of substances and tattvas), soteriology (the path to mokṣa), and ethics (vows, restraints, and contemplations) into a single narrative of pacification. The “peace” it describes is not mere emotional calm but the progressive silencing of rāga (attachment) and dveṣa (aversion), culminating in the complete destruction of ghātiyā‑karmas and the attainment of kevalajñāna (omniscience). The text insists that this process begins with right faith (samyag‑darśana), is supported by right knowledge (samyag‑jñāna), and is fulfilled through right conduct (samyag‑cāritra), thereby reinforcing the canonical triad of the Jain path while giving it a distinctly experiential and contemplative flavor. [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/prasamrati_prakarana_022070_022070)\n\nModern Jain educational initiatives and reprints of Prashamarati present it as a bridge between rigorous philosophical study and accessible spiritual practice. Editors and translators stress that even partial, distracted recitation of its verses can serve as a gradual cleansing of karmic “dust,” provided it is accompanied by sincere effort to understand and live according to their meaning. At the same time, responsible teachers caution that Prashamarati does not replace the need for guidance from living ācāryas and sādhu‑s, especially when its teachings intersect with demanding ascetic practices, complex karmic doctrines, or life‑altering decisions. In this way, Prashamarati remains a respected, widely studied text whose authority is grounded in the classical Jain scriptural tradition but whose application is mediated through contemporary communities and qualified human interpreters.

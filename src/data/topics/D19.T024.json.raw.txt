  "overview": "In Jain contexts, the word \"Tela\" (often related to Sanskrit \"Taila\" or \"Taila/Tela\" in modern Indo-Aryan languages) generally refers to oil—especially edible or medicinal oils used in cooking, anointing, and ritual care. The term itself is not uniquely Jain; it is part of the broader Sanskrit and vernacular vocabulary where \"taila\" denotes oil, and Jain authors and communities adopt this common usage in their own dietary and ritual frameworks. [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/tailapatra)\n\nWithin Jain lay practice, Tela most often appears in discussions of food discipline (āhāra-niyama) and external austerities (bāhya-tapa). In observances such as Ayambil Oli (Navapad Oli), many sanghs explicitly prescribe that the single daily meal be prepared without taste-enhancing items, including milk products, sugar/jaggery, and oil or ghee. Here \"tela\" is not a sacred substance in itself but a category of rich, taste-intensifying ingredients that are voluntarily renounced for the sake of simplicity, restraint of passions (kaṣāya), and tapas. [[1]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/4790/what-is-ayambil-oli) These rules are part of community maryādā (disciplinary codes) rather than a single universally binding canonical passage, and details can vary between Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions.\n\nBeyond fasting rules, Tela also appears in everyday Jain life as cooking oil, lamp oil, and occasionally as a base for medicinal preparations. Classical Jain texts on medicine, conduct, and monastic discipline mention oils (taila) in the same broad sense as other Indian traditions, but surviving references are usually brief and functional—for example, oil used for massage, treatment of ailments, or lighting. In temple contexts, especially among mūrtipūjaka Śvetāmbaras, oil can be used for lamps (dīpa) and for maintaining cleanliness or polish of metal objects, though the central focus of worship remains on darśana, pūjā, and internal virtues rather than on the oil itself.\n\nIt is important to distinguish Jain uses of Tela from non-Jain ritual oils such as the Phuluri Tela of the Jagannath tradition in Puri, where a special herbal oil is applied to deities during the Anasara period as part of a healing ritual. Its moral valence depends on intention, method of production (especially non-violence toward seeds, plants, and living beings), and the context of use. When Tela is avoided in fasts such as Ayambil, the emphasis is on reducing attachment to taste and bodily comfort, not on declaring oil inherently impure. When Tela is used—for example, in moderate cooking or medicine—the guiding principles remain ahiṃsā, aparigraha, and thoughtful restraint.

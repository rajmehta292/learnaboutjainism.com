  "overview": "Rajaprashniya (Prākrit: Rāyapasenaijjaṃ; Sanskrit: Rājapraśnīya) is the second Upāṅga-sūtra of the Śvetāmbara Jain canon, traditionally classified among the twelve Upāṅgas that elaborate and support the main Aṅga texts. It is preserved in Ardhamāgadhī Prākrit, with later Sanskrit recensions and extensive commentarial traditions, especially in the Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka and Sthānakavāsī lineages.\n\nThe title “Rajaprashniya” literally means “(the text) concerning the king’s questions.” At its core, the work presents a sustained dialogue between King Pradeśī (Pradeshi) and the Jain monk Keśi Kumāra. The king, initially aligned with non-Jain kriyāvāda (ritualist) and akriyāvāda (non-action) doctrines, poses searching questions about the nature of the soul, karma, bondage and liberation, the efficacy of ritual, and the meaning of right conduct. Keśi’s replies systematically refute various heretical positions and establish the Jain path of samyagdarśana (right faith), samyagjñāna (right knowledge), and samyakcāritra (right conduct). In this way, the text functions both as a narrative conversion story and as a doctrinal treatise on core Jain metaphysics and ethics.  [[0]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Rajprashniya_Sutra/035223)\n\nA distinctive feature of Rajaprashniya is its integration of cosmological and karmic narrative. The king’s transformation culminates in his death in a state of spiritual maturity, leading to rebirth as a celestial being in the Saudharma heaven. The text then describes his divine status, assembly, and worship, and later his encounter with Lord Mahāvīra, including the famous episode of the king’s elaborate thirty-two-fold dance and musical offering before the Tīrthaṅkara. These episodes illustrate the Jain understanding of karmic fruition: meritorious conduct and right faith yield auspicious rebirths, yet even heavenly existence is ultimately transient compared to the goal of mokṣa.\n\nFrom a canonical perspective, Rajaprashniya is closely linked to the Sūtrakṛtāṅga, one of the principal Aṅgas. Traditional exegetes explain that the Upāṅga elaborates themes already present in Sūtrakṛtāṅga, especially the critique of false teachers and the classification of heretical doctrines. The text explicitly references the large number of kriyāvādins, akriyāvādins, agnostics, and other pāṣaṇḍikas (heretics) whose views are rejected so that the Jain samaya (doctrine) can be firmly established.  [[0]](https://jainqq.org/booktext/Rajprashniya_Sutra/035223)\n\nHistorically, Rajaprashniya has been transmitted with influential Sanskrit commentaries, notably that of Ācārya Malayagiri, and has been edited and translated in modern times by scholars such as N. V. Vaidya and various Jain monks and pandits. These editions provide critical introductions, philological notes, and vernacular translations (Hindi, Gujarati, etc.), making the text accessible to both traditional students and academic readers. The work is studied in sectarian curricula (especially Śvetāmbara) as a key source for understanding Jain views on karma, spiritual hierarchy, and the proper relationship between political power and renunciant authority.\n\nIn contemporary Jain education, Rajaprashniya is often used to illustrate how genuine philosophical inquiry—embodied in the king’s questions—can lead from skepticism and attachment to ritual or power toward a disciplined, non-violent, and introspective life. The narrative warns against blind adherence to external rites without inner transformation, while affirming that rulers and laypeople can, through right guidance, align their lives with ahiṃsā, aparigraha, and the broader Jain ethical vision. Because the text is embedded in a larger canonical framework, traditional teachers emphasize reading it alongside its commentaries and related Aṅga passages, and they recommend consulting qualified ācāryas or scholars for interpretive and practical questions rather than treating the text as a standalone manual for life decisions.",

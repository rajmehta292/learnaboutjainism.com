  "overview": "Karmagranthas (literally “karma‑treatises”) are a distinctive body of Jain doctrinal literature devoted to the detailed analysis of karman—its types, modes of bondage, maturation, and destruction—as the central explanatory principle of saṃsāra and liberation. Both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions regard karma doctrine as one of the most technically developed areas of Jain philosophy, and their respective karmagranthas preserve, systematize, and expand material ultimately traced to the lost eighth Purva, the Karma‑pravāda‑purva. [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jain_karm_sahitya_ka_sankshipta_vivran_229878_229878)\n\nIn Jain thought, karma is conceived not merely as moral retribution but as a subtle material (dravya‑karma) that binds to the soul (jīva) due to passions (kaṣāya), wrong belief (mithyātva), and lack of self‑restraint (asaṃyama). Classical karmagranthas analyze this bondage through four parameters—prakṛti (nature), sthiti (duration), rasa or anubhāga (intensity), and pradeśa (quantity)—and classify eight primary karmas: jñānāvaraṇīya, darśanāvaraṇīya, vedanīya, mohanīya, āyuṣya, nāma, gotra, and antarāya. [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/karmgranth_01_02_03_032107_032107) These texts then subdivide each primary karma into numerous prakṛtis, sometimes running into hundreds of categories, to explain the diversity of embodied existence, from lifespan and social status to bodily form, sensory capacities, and psychological tendencies. [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/karmagrantha_karmaprakruti_panchasangraha_002536_002536)\n\nIn the Digambara tradition, early and foundational treatments of karma doctrine are found in works such as the Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama, Kaṣāya‑prābhṛta, and Mahābandha, which are not always labeled “karmagrantha” in title but function as core karma texts. [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jain_karm_sahitya_ka_sankshipta_vivran_229878_229878) Later Digambara scholastic literature continues this line with extensive discussions of bondage, fruition (vipāka), and the progression of the soul through guṇasthānas. In the Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka tradition, the term “karmagrantha” is more explicit: texts such as Bandha‑śataka, Karma‑prakṛti (often classified as a saṃgrahaṇī), Saptatikā, and various Panchasaṃgraha compilations are recognized as key karma treatises, frequently transmitted with bhāṣya, cūrṇi, vṛtti, ṭīkā, and avacūrī commentaries. [[0]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/jain_karm_sahitya_ka_sankshipta_vivran_229878_229878) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/karmagrantha_karmaprakruti_panchasangraha_002536_002536)\n\nA notable Śvetāmbara compilation is “Karmagrantha Karmaprakṛti Pañcasaṃgraha,” produced in the Hemacandracārya Granthamālā series in Ahmedabad. It gathers five major karma treatises, including Devendrasūri’s “Karma‑vipāka‑nāma,” which systematically expounds the eight primary karmas and their sub‑types, especially jñānāvaraṇīya, darśanāvaraṇīya, vedanīya, and mohanīya. [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/karmagrantha_karmaprakruti_panchasangraha_002536_002536) Devendrasūri’s work, together with later Karmagranth 1–3 collections, illustrates how medieval authors present karma doctrine in metrical Prakrit or Apabhraṃśa gāthās accompanied by vernacular prose explanations, making highly technical material accessible to lay and monastic audiences engaged in svādhyāya (scriptural study). [[1]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/karmgranth_01_02_03_032107_032107)\n\nModern bibliographic surveys, such as Hiralal Rasikdas Kapadia’s “Karm Siddhānta Sambandhī Sāhitya,” map the vast corpus of karma literature across both sects, distinguishing canonical sources, commentaries, and later scholastic syntheses. [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/karm_siddhant_sambandhi_sahitya_022682_022682) These studies emphasize that Jain karma literature is unusually extensive compared to other Indian traditions, and that karmagranthas form a technical backbone for advanced doctrinal study, especially in relation to guṇasthānas, lesyās, and the path of purification (saṃvara and nirjarā). At the same time, they note textual losses, variant recensions, and differing sectarian classifications, reminding readers that reconstructions of the original Karma‑pravāda‑purva and its exact relationship to surviving karmagranthas involve scholarly inference rather than direct manuscript evidence.\n\nFrom a practice perspective, karmagranthas are primarily study texts rather than ritual manuals. They are used in svādhyāya, doctrinal classes, and advanced curriculum for monks, nuns, and serious lay students to cultivate right view (samyag‑darśana) by understanding karma as an adversary rather than a friend. [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/karm_siddhant_sambandhi_sahitya_022682_022682) Interpretive traditions differ on how far the minute classifications should be taken as literal cosmological description versus pedagogical schemata, and contemporary teachers often highlight the ethical and introspective applications—recognizing passions, observing mental states, and aligning conduct with non‑violence and restraint—over purely taxonomic detail. Where questions of medical safety, extreme austerities, or life‑decisions arise, responsible Jain guidance insists that karmagrantha study be complemented by consultation with qualified ācāryas and appropriate professional experts, rather than treated as a stand‑alone manual for health or legal choices.",

  "overview": "Shreyansanatha (Śreyāṃsanātha, Śreyāṃśanātha) is revered in Jain tradition as the eleventh Tirthankara of the current descending time-cycle (avasarpinī) in Bharata-kṣetra. He is counted among the twenty-four Jinas who re-establish the path of liberation (mokṣa-mārga) in each cosmic era, and his life narrative is preserved in both Śvetāmbara and Digambara textual lineages. According to Jain universal history, he was born in the Ikṣvāku dynasty, a royal lineage frequently associated with early Tirthankaras, and his spiritual career exemplifies the transition from royal authority to complete renunciation and omniscience (kevala-jñāna).  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shreyansanatha) [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/shreyamsanatha)\n\nMost Śvetāmbara sources describe his birthplace as Siṃhapurī (often identified with the region near present-day Sarnath in Uttar Pradesh) and name his parents King Viṣṇu and Queen Viṣṇu or Viṣṇudrī, while Digambara traditions generally agree on the father’s name but give the mother as Veṇudevī. His emblem (lāñchana) is the rhinoceros, and his complexion is described as golden (kāñcana-varṇa). Iconographic manuals such as the Aparājitapṛcchā and later works on Jaina iconography specify his height as 80 dhanuṣa (bows)—a conventional cosmological measure corresponding to an immense, symbolic stature rather than a historical physical height.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shreyansanatha) [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/shreyamsanatha)\n\nNarrative texts portray Shreyansanatha first as a powerful ruler who governs his kingdom with justice and prosperity. Eventually, confronted with the impermanence of worldly power and the subtle bondage of karma, he renounces his throne, adopts the life of an ascetic, and undertakes rigorous spiritual discipline. Through right faith (samyag-darśana), right knowledge (samyag-jñāna), and right conduct (samyag-cāritra), he attains kevala-jñāna and becomes a Tirthankara—one who establishes a ford (tīrtha) across saṃsāra for other beings. After a prolonged period of teaching, he finally attains liberation (mokṣa) at the sacred mountain later known as Śikharjī (Parasnātha Hills in Jharkhand), a major pilgrimage site for Jains of multiple sects.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Shreyansanatha) [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/shreyamsanatha)\n\nWithin the doctrinal framework, Shreyansanatha’s life is used to illustrate key Jain themes: the vast temporal scales of cosmic history, the exemplary role of Tirthankaras in organizing the fourfold community (monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen), and the possibility of complete karmic eradication through disciplined practice. His narrative is embedded in larger Purāṇic-style compilations such as Hemacandra’s \"Triṣaṣṭi-śalākā-puruṣa-caritra\" and is cross-referenced in canonical texts that classify Tirthankaras by attributes such as height, color, emblem, and parentage. These sources sometimes differ in numerical details (lifespan, intervals between Tirthankaras, counts of disciples), and traditional scholars explicitly acknowledge such variations as \"pāṭhāntara\" (textual divergences), emphasizing that the spiritual message—nonviolence, detachment, and the path to liberation—remains central even when historical or cosmological specifics vary.  [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/shreyamsanatha) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/tirthankar_11_shreyanshnath_bhagwan_parichay_035111_035111)\n\nIn contemporary Jain practice, Shreyansanatha is venerated in temples across India and the diaspora, with images typically showing the rhinoceros emblem on the pedestal or near the feet of the Jina. Devotional literature, sermons, and modern study guides draw on both canonical references and later narrative expansions to present his life as a model of courageous renunciation and steadfast pursuit of spiritual freedom. While the historical dating of his life in conventional chronological terms is not attempted—Jain cosmology places him in a mythic time-scale of millions of years—his ethical and spiritual significance is treated as timeless, inviting lay and monastic practitioners alike to reflect on the limits of worldly success and the possibility of radical inner transformation.\n",

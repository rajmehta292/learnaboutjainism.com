  "overview": "In Jain philosophy, “soul comparisons” primarily concern how different jīvas (souls) are classified, related, and ultimately understood to be equal in essence yet diverse in states and capacities. The central claim is that reality is divided into two fundamental categories: jīva (soul, living substance) and ajīva (non-soul, non-living substance). All jīvas share an intrinsic mark—conscious experience (upayoga)—but they differ in the kinds of bodies they inhabit, the number of sense-organs they possess, and the degree of karmic bondage that obscures their innate qualities.  [[0]](https://unfinishablemap.org/topics/jain-philosophy-of-mind/) [[1]](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Outlines_of_Jainism/Part_2/Chapter_2)\n\nFrom the standpoint of basic classification, Jain texts compare souls along several axes. One major comparison is between saṃsārī jīvas (transmigrating souls) and mukta jīvas (liberated souls). Saṃsārī souls wander through the cycle of birth and death, bound by karmic matter that limits their knowledge, perception, bliss, and energy. Mukta souls, by contrast, have completely shed karmic bondage and exist as siddhas at the apex of the universe, endowed with infinite knowledge (ananta-jñāna), infinite perception (ananta-darśana), infinite bliss (ananta-sukha), and infinite energy (ananta-vīrya). Yet even here, the comparison is not between two different kinds of substance: both are jīvas; the difference lies only in the presence or absence of karma.  [[2]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084627.html) [[3]](https://philosophy.institute/religions-of-the-world/soul-concept-jainism-analysis/)\n\nAnother important set of comparisons concerns the kinds of embodied souls. Jain metaphysics distinguishes sthāvara (stationary) souls—such as earth, water, fire, air, and vegetable souls—from trasa (mobile) souls, which possess two or more sense-organs. Stationary souls are one-sensed; mobile souls range from two-sensed beings up to five-sensed beings with mind, including humans, many animals, and celestial beings. These comparisons show a graded map of sentience: all souls are conscious, but their embodied capacities for perception, cognition, and agency vary according to the number of senses and the development of mind. The hierarchy is ethical as well as ontological, since harming even the lowest one-sensed beings still injures a conscious jīva.  [[1]](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Outlines_of_Jainism/Part_2/Chapter_2) [[3]](https://philosophy.institute/religions-of-the-world/soul-concept-jainism-analysis/)\n\nJain thinkers also compare souls from two complementary standpoints: the conventional (vyavahāra-naya) and the ultimate (niścaya-naya). From the conventional standpoint, souls are described in relation to bodies, senses, karmic states, and worldly activities—they are doers of actions, enjoyers of their fruits, and occupants of particular realms. From the ultimate standpoint, however, the soul is pure consciousness, free from all adventitious attributes of matter. Texts like Kundakunda’s Samayasāra emphasize that, in truth, the self is only the knowing and seeing principle; distinctions based on body, caste, species, or social identity are merely conventional. Soul comparisons therefore serve to remind practitioners that while empirical differences are real at the worldly level, they must not obscure the deeper equality of all jīvas as potential siddhas.  [[1]](https://en.wikisource.org/wiki/Outlines_of_Jainism/Part_2/Chapter_2) [[4]](https://ontologicalatlas.com/schools/jainism-anekantavada/)\n\nComparative discussions within Jainism also extend to how its concept of soul differs from other Indian traditions. Unlike many Vedānta schools, Jainism rejects the idea of a single universal Self and instead affirms an infinite plurality of numerically distinct souls. Unlike Buddhism, it does not accept anattā (no-self); the existence of enduring, conscious substances is a foundational axiom. At the same time, Jain epistemology insists on anekāntavāda—the doctrine of many-sidedness—which cautions that any statement about the soul captures only one aspect of a complex reality. Thus, when Jains compare souls—liberated and bound, stationary and mobile, one-sensed and five-sensed—they do so within a framework that combines ontological precision with epistemic humility. The ultimate ethical implication is that every soul, however obscured or limited, deserves non-violence (ahiṃsā) and has the capacity, through right faith, knowledge, and conduct, to realize its inherent perfection.

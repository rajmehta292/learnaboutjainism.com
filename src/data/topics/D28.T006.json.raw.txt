  "overview": "The Cheda-sutras (Sanskrit: Chedasūtra; Prakrit: Chedasutta) are a distinct group of canonical disciplinary texts within the Śvetāmbara Jain tradition, classified among the Āgamas as specialized regulations for monastic conduct and procedures of ecclesiastical discipline. They focus on the \"cheda\"—literally cutting, reduction, or modification—of a monk’s spiritual status and ascetic privileges in response to transgressions, lapses in vows, or complex situations in communal life. In this sense, they function as juridical and procedural manuals for the saṅgha, complementing the more doctrinal and narrative scriptures by detailing how rules are applied, how faults are confessed, and how penances are assigned.  [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/essay/jainism-and-patanjali-yoga/d/doc1208504.html) [[1]](https://www.hinduwebsite.com/jainism/jaincanon.asp)\n\nIn the standard Śvetāmbara classification, six texts are usually counted as Cheda-sutras: Vyavahāra-sūtra, Kalpa-sūtra, Niyama-sāra (often referred to simply as Niyama-sūtra in the canonical grouping), Mahāniśītha-sūtra, Jitkalpa-sūtra, and Daśāśrutaskandha-sūtra. These works collectively regulate matters such as admission to monastic life, gradations of fault, procedures for confession (ālochanā) and expiation (prāyaścitta), rules for wandering and residence during the rainy season, and the handling of serious breaches that may lead to partial or full loss of status. They also codify the authority of senior monks in adjudicating disputes and maintaining communal harmony, thereby serving as a practical framework for implementing the ethical ideals found in other parts of the canon.  [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/hinduism/essay/jainism-and-patanjali-yoga/d/doc1208504.html) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/trini_chedsutrani_003463_003463)\n\nHistorically, the Cheda-sutras emerged as part of the broader organization of Jain canonical literature after Mahāvīra, when councils and scholastic efforts sought to preserve and systematize teachings in the form of sūtras and commentaries. Their language is primarily Prakrit (especially Ardhamāgadhī and related dialects), with later scholastic layers in Sanskrit and vernacular commentaries. While Digambara tradition holds that the original canonical Āgamas—including the Cheda-sutras—were ultimately lost, Śvetāmbara communities continued to transmit and study these texts, producing extensive commentarial traditions that interpret their disciplinary rules in changing historical contexts. Modern scholarship treats the Cheda-sutras as crucial sources for reconstructing the social history of Jain monasticism, internal legal culture, and the evolution of ascetic norms.  [[3]](https://iaspoint.com/jain-literature-as-a-source/) [[1]](https://www.hinduwebsite.com/jainism/jaincanon.asp)\n\nIn contemporary practice, the Cheda-sutras remain important for monastic training and for understanding how ideals like ahiṃsā (non-violence), satya (truthfulness), and aparigraha (non-possession) are concretely enforced within the saṅgha. They are often studied alongside their traditional commentaries, which clarify technical terminology, classify types of faults, and explain the rationale behind specific disciplinary measures. For lay Jains, the Cheda-sutras are less frequently read directly, but their influence is felt indirectly through the disciplined example of monks and nuns whose lives are shaped by these regulations. Because they deal with complex issues of authority, confession, and penance, responsible interpretation today typically involves consultation with qualified ācāryas or scholars, especially when questions touch on living disciplinary practice or require decisions within a specific lineage.\n\nIt is important to distinguish between the canonical Cheda-sutras and later works that bear similar names or focus on discipline. Some post-canonical texts and modern manuals draw on Cheda-sutra material but adapt it to new circumstances; these should not be conflated with the original Āgama texts. Furthermore, translation and edition history is uneven: while some Cheda-sutras have been critically edited and translated with scholarly apparatus, others are available mainly through traditional editions or community-based publications. This means that precise wording, verse numbering, and even the inclusion of certain passages can vary between editions, and careful study requires attention to the specific textual lineage and editorial choices involved.

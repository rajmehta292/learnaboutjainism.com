  "overview": "Pramatta-samyata (Sanskrit: \"pramatta-samyata\"; Prakrit: \"pamatta-samiyatta\") is a technical term in Jain doctrine describing one of the fourteen guṇasthānas, or stages of spiritual development of the soul. Etymologically, \"pramatta\" means negligent, careless, or inattentive, while \"samyata\" means restrained, disciplined, or one who observes self-control. Together, the compound denotes a state in which full ethical restraint has been adopted, but the practitioner’s conduct is still occasionally affected by lapses of attentiveness and subtle negligence. It is therefore a high, but not yet perfected, stage on the path toward liberation.\n\nWithin the standard Jain enumeration of the guṇasthānas, pramatta-samyata is the stage that follows deśa-virata (partial self-restraint) and precedes apramatta-samyata (non-negligent self-restraint). In this seventh stage, the soul has embraced complete renunciation of harmful activities and observance of vows, yet the influence of passions (kaṣāyas) and the residual force of karmic matter still allow for minor carelessness in thought, speech, or bodily action. The defining feature is not moral collapse, but the presence of spiritual negligence (pramāda) in spite of an overall commitment to right conduct (samyak-cāritra). This makes pramatta-samyata a crucial transitional phase: the aspirant has crossed the threshold into full restraint but must still refine awareness and vigilance to progress further.\n\nClassical Jain authors analyze pramatta-samyata in close connection with the concept of pramāda, which includes states such as intoxication, excessive sleep, frivolous talk, and inattentive behavior that weaken mindfulness and open the door to karmic influx (āsrava). At the pramatta-samyata stage, such pramāda is significantly reduced compared to lower stages, but not entirely eliminated. The aspirant may occasionally act with a trace of carelessness, even while formally keeping the vows. This explains why pramatta-samyata is considered spiritually superior to deśa-virata, where restraint itself is only partial, yet still inferior to apramatta-samyata, where negligence is fully overcome and deep meditation (dhyāna) becomes more stable.\n\nFrom the perspective of karma theory, pramatta-samyata marks an advanced phase of the soul’s purification. The gross forms of wrong belief (mithyātva) have already been overcome, and right faith (samyag-darśana) and right conduct (samyak-cāritra) are present. However, the subtle operation of mohanīya (deluding) karmas continues to manifest as pramāda, preventing the complete inhibition (saṃvara) and shedding (nirjarā) of karmic matter. Traditional commentaries emphasize that progress beyond pramatta-samyata requires sustained vigilance, introspection, and the cultivation of non-negligent awareness in every activity, so that even minor lapses are gradually removed.\n\nBoth Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions accept pramatta-samyata as a standard guṇasthāna, though they may differ in some details of classification and practical examples. In monastic contexts, pramatta-samyata is often illustrated by a monk who has taken all major vows and follows the discipline, yet occasionally becomes inattentive—perhaps in handling possessions, in speech, or in the precision of observances—without falling into deliberate transgression. In lay contexts, the term is more often used conceptually, to highlight that even those who have adopted extensive vows can still be hindered by carelessness. Thus, pramatta-samyata serves as a reminder that ethical restraint must be joined with continuous awareness to lead toward higher stages.\n\nIn spiritual practice, pramatta-samyata underscores the Jain emphasis on inner quality of conduct, not merely external observance. The aspirant at this stage is encouraged to refine mindfulness, reduce distractions, and cultivate a steady, alert presence in all actions. As negligence diminishes, the soul becomes capable of entering apramatta-samyata, where restraint is accompanied by unwavering attentiveness, and the path opens toward the more rarefied stages characterized by novel spiritual experiences, the weakening and eventual destruction of passions, and finally omniscience (kevala-jñāna) and liberation (mokṣa).",

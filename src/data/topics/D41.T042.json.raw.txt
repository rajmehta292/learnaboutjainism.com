  "overview": "Diaspora temples are Jain places of worship, community, and education established outside the Indian subcontinent, primarily by lay Jains who migrated for trade, study, and professional work. From the early twentieth century onward, Jains formed communities in East Africa, the United Kingdom, continental Europe, North America, and later in regions such as the Persian Gulf and Australasia. These temples—often called derāsar, mandir, or Jain centres—anchor religious identity in new environments, provide spaces for pūjā, svādhyāya (scriptural study), and saṅgh activities, and serve as visible markers of Jain presence in multicultural societies. [[0]](https://www.arihantainstitute.org/blog/63-the-jain-diaspora)\n\nHistorically, one of the earliest known Jain temples outside India was built in Zanzibar around 1905, reflecting the movement of Gujarati merchant communities into East Africa under British colonial rule. Subsequent temples arose in Nairobi and Mombasa, frequently adopting the Māru-Gurjara architectural style familiar from western India, with carved pillars, śikhara towers, and marble ornamentation. In many cases, building elements and mūrtis were carved in India and shipped abroad for assembly and consecration, symbolically extending the sacred geography of Jain tirthas into the diaspora while maintaining ritual continuity with homeland traditions. [[0]](https://www.arihantainstitute.org/blog/63-the-jain-diaspora) [[1]](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/a/ars/13441566.0045.005/1%3A8/--international-jaina-style-maru-gurjara-temples-under?rgn=div1%3Bview=fulltext)\n\nIn the United Kingdom, a significant portion of the Jain population arrived as “twice migrants” from East Africa in the 1960s and 1970s. The Jain Centre in Leicester—housed in a former Anglican church—became one of the first major Jain temples in Britain, incorporating a derāsar, upāśraya, library, auditorium, and dining hall. Its façade and interior were transformed with Māru-Gurjara ornamentation, while the community adopted a multi-sect model: Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka, Digambara, and non-image-worshipping Jains all use the same complex, with separate shrines and shared halls. This pattern of adaptive reuse of existing buildings, combined with imported temple architecture, is characteristic of many diaspora temples in Europe and North America. [[1]](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/a/ars/13441566.0045.005/1%3A8/--international-jaina-style-maru-gurjara-temples-under?rgn=div1%3Bview=fulltext) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/pardeshma_jain_dharm_006159_006159)\n\nIn North America, Jain migration accelerated after changes to U.S. immigration law in the late 1960s. Early arrivals formed local associations that gradually evolved into temple-building projects, culminating in large complexes such as the Jain Center of Southern California, the Jain Center of Greater Phoenix, and the Jain Society of Metropolitan Chicago. These institutions typically include one or more temple halls, classrooms for pāṭhaśālā (religious education), libraries, kitchens, and multi-purpose auditoriums. Ritual life in these temples adapts traditional practices—such as daily āratī, Paryuṣaṇa and Daśalakṣaṇa observances, and saṃvatsarī pratikramaṇa—to local work schedules, school calendars, and legal regulations, while maintaining core commitments to ahiṃsā, anekāntavāda, and aparigraha. [[3]](https://www.jainheritagecentres.com/jainism/articles/history-of-jains-in-north-america-qevolutionq/) [[4]](https://zenodo.org/records/18475274)\n\nArchitecturally, diaspora temples often replicate the Māru-Gurjara style associated historically with Śvetāmbara communities in Gujarat and Rajasthan, but in the diaspora this style has become a shared visual language across sectarian lines. Temples in Nairobi, Mombasa, Potters Bar (UK), and Antwerp (Belgium) feature carved sandstone or marble façades, flying arches, and multiple śikhara towers, closely echoing Indian prototypes. At the same time, many diaspora temples occupy repurposed churches, warehouses, or office buildings, reflecting economic constraints and local zoning laws. This combination of replication and adaptation illustrates how diasporic Jains negotiate continuity with inherited forms while responding creatively to new urban and legal contexts. [[1]](https://quod.lib.umich.edu/a/ars/13441566.0045.005/1%3A8/--international-jaina-style-maru-gurjara-temples-under?rgn=div1%3Bview=fulltext) [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/pardeshma_jain_dharm_006159_006159)\n\nSocially, diaspora temples function as hubs for community cohesion, youth and family education, and transnational philanthropy. They host language classes, Jain studies courses, interfaith dialogues, and service projects, enabling Jains to articulate their ethics of nonviolence, environmental concern, and compassion within broader civic frameworks. Leadership roles in these institutions are often held by lay professionals, including women and younger generations, who balance respect for traditional ascetic ideals with the realities of diasporic life. Digital technologies—live-streamed pūjā, online pravacans, and virtual study groups—extend the reach of diaspora temples, connecting scattered communities and linking them back to teachers and institutions in India. [[0]](https://www.arihantainstitute.org/blog/63-the-jain-diaspora) [[4]](https://zenodo.org/records/18475274)\n\nFrom a doctrinal perspective, diaspora temples do not introduce new scriptural authorities but embody long-standing Jain understandings of the temple as a space for venerating the Jina, cultivating detachment, and supporting the fourfold community (monks, nuns, laymen, laywomen). Their distinctiveness lies in context: they translate these ideals into pluralistic societies, navigate legal and ethical questions about religious display, and foster identities that are simultaneously Jain, diasporic, and locally rooted. As such, diaspora temples are key sites for observing how Jainism continues to evolve while remaining anchored in its classical commitments.",

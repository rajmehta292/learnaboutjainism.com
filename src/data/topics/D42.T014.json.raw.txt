Ashtamaṅgala sets appear across both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions, with minor regional variations, but the inclusion of the pair of fish is widely shared. It is not an object of worship in itself; rather, it functions as a visual reminder of specific spiritual qualities and insights on the Jain path. Just as fish move freely through water without obstruction, the symbol invites the practitioner to cultivate courage, clarity, and steady movement toward liberation (mokṣa). Some educational sources emphasize its role as a reminder of the soul’s duty to \"cross the worldly ocean\" by right faith, right knowledge, and right conduct, linking the image to the Three Jewels (ratnatraya) and the broader ethical program of Jain dharma. These symbols sacralize the space and focus the mind, but they are explicitly understood as aids to awareness, not magical talismans promising external success. Contemporary Jain educational materials stress that Ashtamaṅgala are symbolic tools that support right orientation of the mind rather than substitutes for devotion to the Tīrthaṅkaras. Another reading sees the fish couple as representing the flow of divine life in the cosmic ocean, emphasizing the dynamic, living quality of spiritual progress rather than static symbolism. These interpretations belong to later commentarial and educational strata rather than to a single canonical verse, and they should be treated as devotional or pedagogical expansions consistent with Jain non-theistic theology, not as dogmatic doctrine. Minor differences appear in emphasis: some sources highlight the destruction of sorrow and the bringing of happiness into life, others foreground victory over passions and the courage needed for spiritual discipline. These are complementary rather than contradictory, reflecting different pedagogical aims—householder encouragement, ritual focus, or philosophical reflection. No major sectarian controversy is recorded around the symbol itself; debates, where they exist, concern the exact list and ordering of Ashtamaṅgala in particular regional or textual traditions, not the legitimacy of the pair of fish as a Jain emblem. The symbol does not endorse literal fish‑killing or any form of animal exploitation; instead, it uses a familiar natural image to point toward the soul’s journey and the inner work of reducing passions. In modern ethical reflection, especially in diaspora communities, teachers often underline that such symbols are pedagogical metaphors and should never be taken as license for behavior that contradicts Jain vows.

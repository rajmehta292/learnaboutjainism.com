  "overview": "Devavandana (also written Dev Vandana, Deva-vandana) in Jainism refers to the structured act of reverential salutation and devotional praise offered to the Tirthankaras and other spiritually exalted beings, typically performed in front of a Jina image or at a chaitya (shrine). It is both a liturgical sequence of verses and physical prostrations, and an inner discipline of humility, surrender of ego, and cultivation of right faith (samyak-darśana).\n\nIn practice, Devavandana usually combines three dimensions: physical bowing (vandan or praṇāma), vocal recitation of canonical or semi-canonical hymns (stotra, stuti, stavan), and contemplative reflection on the qualities of the Arihants, Siddhas, Ācāryas, Upādhyāyas, and all sādhus and sādhvīs. The most fundamental mantra in many Devavandana sequences is the Navkār (Namokāra) Mantra, which bows to these five categories of spiritually advanced beings and is regarded as a universal salutation that destroys sins and brings auspiciousness.  [[0]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/5016/dev-vadan-shlok)\n\nThe physical component often includes the traditional five-point prostration (pañcāṅga praṇām), in which the devotee touches the floor with two hands, two knees, and the forehead, symbolizing complete surrender of pride and worldly preoccupations. This is accompanied by a devotional attitude (bhāva) of inner purity, non-violence, and aspiration for right knowledge, right faith, and right conduct. Devavandana is not merely a request for worldly favors; rather, it is an offering of reverence to the qualities of the Jinas and an occasion to align one’s own soul with those qualities.  [[1]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/14475/process-of-dev-vandan)\n\nAcross Jain traditions, Devavandana is embedded in daily temple worship and special observances. In Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka practice, a typical Devavandana sequence may begin with the Navkār Mantra, followed by hymns such as Logassa Ujjuyāre, Namutthunam, and Jai Vīyarāya, along with specific vandan to the presiding Tirthankara of the temple, kṣamāpanā (asking forgiveness), and optional stotras like Śāntikaraṇa, Uvāsagga-haraṇa, or Bhaktāmara.  [[0]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/5016/dev-vadan-shlok) Devavandana texts are often collected in liturgical anthologies such as “Devvandanmala” and “Stavan Sangraha Devvandana Sahit,” which organize hymns for daily worship, festivals, and chaityavandana, and provide narrative and doctrinal context for the verses.  [[2]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/devvandanmala_032403_032403) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/stavan_sangraha_devvandana_sahit_008666_008666)\n\nIn Digambara settings, the core intent of Devavandana is similar, though the exact sequence of verses and ritual details may differ. Devotees recite the Navkār Mantra (sometimes in a Sanskritized form) and hymns like “Namostu Vītarāgāya,” as well as selected stotras such as Bhaktāmara or Kalyāṇa Maṇḍira Stotra, often integrated into deva-pūjā and daily worship.  [[0]](https://jainknowledge.com/question/5016/dev-vadan-shlok) The diversity of Devavandana literature—seen in compilations like “Dev Vandana Stuti Stavan Sangrah” by Ācārya Buddhisagar Suri—reflects the Jain recognition that different devotees resonate with different poetic and philosophical styles, while the underlying aim remains the same: to purify the soul, deepen devotion, and orient the practitioner toward liberation (mokṣa).  [[4]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/dev_vandana_stuti_stavan_sangrah_008557_008557)\n\nFrom a doctrinal perspective, Devavandana is closely linked to the Jain understanding of karma and spiritual progress. By repeatedly contemplating and praising the qualities of the Jinas and other advanced beings, the practitioner cultivates śraddhā (trustful faith) in the path of non-violence, restraint, and self-realization, and weakens passions such as pride, anger, and greed. The verses often include universal wishes for the welfare of all living beings, reinforcing the ethic of ahiṁsā and the recognition of the interconnectedness of all jīvas. In this way, Devavandana functions as both a devotional and ethical practice, integrating liturgy, philosophy, and daily conduct into a single, coherent discipline.\n\nHistorically, Devavandana collections have grown as Jain poets, monks, and scholars composed new hymns that respond to the spiritual needs of their communities, while remaining anchored in the core doctrinal framework. Modern compilations frequently include brief biographies of the composers, explanations of the occasions for which each Devavandana is intended (such as Gyan Panchami, Caturmāsa observances, or Maun Ekādaśī), and guidance on correct performance.

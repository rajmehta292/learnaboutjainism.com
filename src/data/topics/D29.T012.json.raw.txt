  "overview": "Prathamanuyoga is one of the four classical anuyogas (expositional streams) of Jain scriptural and scholastic literature, traditionally described as the narrative or historical corpus. In the standard fourfold division—Prathamanuyoga, Karananuyoga, Charananuyoga, and Dravyanuyoga—Prathamanuyoga is associated with itihāsa and purāṇa, that is, accounts of cosmic ages, royal lineages, and especially the exemplary lives of great personages (śalākā-puruṣas). These narratives are not merely stories for entertainment; they function as didactic models that embody Jain ethical ideals, cosmological frameworks, and the dynamics of karma and spiritual progress. [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/gommatsara-by-acharya-nemichandra/d/doc1679466.html)\n\nIn many traditional descriptions, Prathamanuyoga is said to deal with \"the ages and lives of the Great Personages of the World,\" with particular emphasis on the sixty-three śalākā-puruṣas: the twenty-four Tīrthaṅkaras, twelve Cakravartins (universal monarchs), nine Nārāyaṇas, nine Prati-Nārāyaṇas, and nine Balabhadras. Through detailed biographies—birth, auspicious dreams, major turning points, renunciation, spiritual practices, and liberation—these texts present paradigms of both worldly excellence and spiritual perfection. The narratives are carefully integrated into Jain cosmology, showing how beings rise and fall across vast cycles of time, how karmic causes lead to specific destinies, and how right faith, knowledge, and conduct can ultimately culminate in kevalajñāna and mokṣa. [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/gommatsara-by-acharya-nemichandra/d/doc1679466.html)\n\nPrathamanuyoga literature spans both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions and includes works in Prakrit, Sanskrit, and later vernaculars. Digambara authors such as Jinasena and Guṇabhadra composed major purāṇas (e.g., Harivaṃśa Purāṇa, Padma Purāṇa, Uttarapurāṇa) that narrate the careers of Tīrthaṅkaras and other śalākā-puruṣas, embedding Jain perspectives within broader Indian epic and purāṇic motifs. Śvetāmbara authors, most famously Hemacandra in his \"Triṣaṣṭi-śalākā-puruṣa-caritra,\" systematized the lives of the sixty-three great beings into a unified narrative cycle, aligning them with doctrinal themes and monastic discipline. These works are often classified as Prathamanuyoga because they present history and legend in a way that is explicitly subordinated to Jain philosophical and ethical aims.\n\nFrom a pedagogical standpoint, Prathamanuyoga serves several functions. First, it provides accessible entry points into complex doctrines: ideas about karma, guṇasthānas (spiritual stages), cosmological regions, and vows are illustrated through concrete stories rather than abstract argument alone. Second, it offers role models for different kinds of aspirants—householders, kings, warriors, ascetics—showing how Jain values can be lived in diverse social roles. Third, it reinforces communal memory and identity: festivals, temple iconography, and ritual recitations often draw on episodes from Prathamanuyoga texts, making narrative a central vehicle of religious transmission.\n\nWithin the broader hermeneutical framework of the four anuyogas, Prathamanuyoga is not isolated. Its narratives are meant to be read alongside Karananuyoga (cosmology and calculation), Charananuyoga (conduct and discipline), and Dravyanuyoga (philosophy of substances and tattvas). For example, a story of a Tīrthaṅkara’s renunciation may presuppose cosmological details about the era and region (Karananuyoga), illustrate specific vows and rules (Charananuyoga), and implicitly rely on metaphysical claims about soul and karma (Dravyanuyoga). Thus, Prathamanuyoga is best understood as the narrative articulation of Jain doctrine, where history and myth are carefully structured to support the path to liberation rather than to provide neutral chronicle.\n\nModern scholarship often treats Prathamanuyoga texts as crucial sources for reconstructing Jain self-understanding, sectarian developments, and interactions with wider Indian literary traditions. At the same time, traditional communities continue to approach these works primarily as śruta (authoritative teaching) that inspires devotion (bhakti), ethical reflection, and aspiration toward the spiritual ideals embodied by the śalākā-puruṣas. Interpretation therefore moves on multiple levels: historical-critical, doctrinal, ritual, and devotional, all anchored in the recognition that Prathamanuyoga is a narrative gateway into the Jain vision of the cosmos and the soul’s journey.",

  "overview": "In Jain tradition, Rāvaṇa (often called Daśamukha or Daśānana) is not only the antagonist of Rāma’s story but one of the illustrious beings (śalākā-puruṣa) whose life is used to teach karma, non-violence, and the complexity of moral character. Hemacandra’s 12th‑century Sanskrit work *Triṣaṣṭi-śalākā-puruṣa-caritra* (Lives of the Sixty‑Three Illustrious Beings) presents a distinct Jain Ramayana in which Rāvaṇa’s biography is integrated into a larger cosmological and ethical framework. [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc213994.html) [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc213985.html)\n\nIn this Jain retelling, Rāvaṇa belongs to the Rākṣasa lineage (rakṣasavaṃśa) ruling Laṅkā on Rakṣodvīpa in Bharata‑kṣetra. His ancestors, such as Ghanavāhana and Mahārakṣas, are portrayed as kings who eventually renounce power, become mendicants at the feet of Tīrthaṅkaras like Ajita Svāmin, and attain emancipation (mokṣa). This lineage context emphasizes that even powerful rulers are ultimately subject to karma and can turn toward spiritual liberation. [[2]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc213984.html)\n\nRāvaṇa’s birth is described with characteristic Jain symbolism. His mother Kaikasī dreams of a lion tearing an elephant’s boss and entering her mouth, interpreted as a sign that she will bear a son powerful and arrogant toward all. From conception onward, her speech becomes harsh and her demeanor proud, reflecting the karmic qualities of the embryo. The newborn Rāvaṇa, endowed with a life‑span of more than twelve thousand years, immediately lifts and adorns himself with a heavy ancestral necklace of nine rubies—an act that no one else could perform. Because his face is joined with these nine rubies, he receives the name Daśamukha (“Ten‑faced”), and a prophecy is recalled that whoever lifts this necklace will be an Ardhacakrin (a partial Cakravartin, or universal monarch). [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc213985.html)\n\nHemacandra’s narrative presents Rāvaṇa as a powerful king, a Vidyādhara‑like figure endowed with extraordinary abilities, who undertakes an expedition of conquest to reclaim Laṅkā and assert his authority. The text describes his encounter with his cousin Vaiśravaṇa (Kubera), the acquisition of the aerial car Puṣpaka, and his journey to Mt. Sammeta (Sammeta-śikhara), a major Jain pilgrimage site, where he honors the statues of the Arhats. This episode is significant: Rāvaṇa, though a warrior and conqueror, is shown paying reverence to Jinas, indicating that even those immersed in worldly power can recognize and venerate spiritual ideals. [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc213994.html)\n\nThe Jain Ramayana also elaborates Rāvaṇa’s campaigns, his interactions with hell realms, and his concern for vassals and dependents. In one episode, he confronts Yama to free his hereditary servitors who have been imprisoned and subjected to tortures in hell. The narrative uses Rāvaṇa’s anger and sense of responsibility to illustrate how attachment to power and loyalty can lead one into conflict with the cosmic order, yet also how compassion for subordinates can coexist with violent tendencies. [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc213994.html)\n\nCrucially, Jain tradition reframes the ultimate destiny of Rāvaṇa’s soul. After his death and subsequent suffering in the fourth hell, Hemacandra describes a long sequence of future births in which Rāvaṇa’s soul (later named Indrāyudha in a human birth) gradually purifies its karma. Eventually, this soul acquires the family‑determining karma of a Tīrthaṅkara and becomes a future Tīrthanātha, with Rāma’s soul as his gaṇadhara (chief disciple). Both then attain emancipation. This eschatological vision underscores a central Jain teaching: even a figure remembered for violence and arrogance can, through the long arc of rebirth and spiritual effort, become a perfected teacher of dharma. [[3]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/trishashti-shalaka-purusha-caritra/d/doc214126.html)\n\nCompared with the more familiar Hindu Ramayana, Jain narratives downplay or reinterpret certain elements (such as the centrality of divine incarnation) and emphasize non‑violence, karma, and the possibility of spiritual progress for all beings. Rāvaṇa is not a purely demonic figure but a complex soul whose story illustrates how pride, attachment, and violence generate suffering, yet do not permanently bar one from liberation. The Jain portrayal invites readers to see him as a moral lesson in the transformation of consciousness rather than as an eternally condemned villain.\n\nModern Jain scholarship and community education often use Rāvaṇa’s story to discuss themes such as the dangers of ego, the responsibilities of rulers, and the importance of honoring Jinas even while engaged in worldly life. At the same time, teachers caution that narrative embellishments, especially in later retellings or unverified translations, should be checked against primary texts and established commentaries. Where details differ between sects or authors, the emphasis remains on the ethical and karmic lessons rather than on literal historical reconstruction.",

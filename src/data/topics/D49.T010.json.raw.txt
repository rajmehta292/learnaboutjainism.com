  "overview": "Combined-tradition centers in Jainism are contemporary institutions—usually temples or community halls—designed to serve multiple Jain sects under a single organizational and architectural roof. They have emerged most visibly in the global diaspora (North America, UK, parts of Europe), but similar experiments exist in India and elsewhere. Their defining feature is not merely co-location of Jains, but intentional provision of distinct spaces, schedules, and governance structures for different traditions (such as Digambara, Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka, Sthānakavāsī, Terāpanthī, and lineages like Śrīmad Rājacandra), while affirming a shared ethical and philosophical core rooted in the teachings of the Jinas.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_Center_of_America) [[1]](https://southasianherald.com/historic-unity-jain-sects-unite-to-inaugurate-landmark-temple-in-beltsville-maryland/)\n\nHistorically, Jain communities organized themselves along sectarian lines, with separate temples, monastic lineages, and canonical emphases. Archaeological and textual evidence shows that the Digambara–Śvetāmbara schism solidified over centuries, and later reform movements such as Sthānakavāsī and Terāpanth further diversified institutional life. Combined-tradition centers respond to a new social reality: smaller, geographically dispersed Jain populations in cities where building multiple fully staffed temples for each sect is impractical. In these contexts, lay Jains often share language, migration histories, and professional networks, making a unified physical center both economically feasible and socially attractive.  [[2]](https://www.noosaga.com/explore/jainism/jain-history-and-community) [[3]](https://settleinamerica.com/guides/indian/bay-area/jain/)\n\nArchitecturally, many combined-tradition centers allocate separate sanctums or floors for different traditions. The Jain Center of America in New York, for example, houses a Śvetāmbara Mahāvīra shrine, a Digambara Ṛṣabhanātha shrine, an upāśraya-style space for Sthānakavāsīs (who do not use images), and a meditation hall associated with the Śrīmad Rājacandra tradition—all within one building. Each area follows its own iconographic norms, ritual protocols, and festival observances, while common halls host lectures, youth classes (pāṭhaśālā), and community meals. This model allows sectarian integrity and mutual respect to coexist, embodying the Jain principle of anekāntavāda (many-sidedness) at the institutional level.  [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_Center_of_America)\n\nIn North America, centers such as the Jain Center of Northern California (Milpitas) and the Jain Society of Houston explicitly describe themselves as “all-sect” or “multi-sect,” serving Śvetāmbara and Digambara families together and often welcoming members from Sthānakavāsī and Terāpanth backgrounds. These centers typically develop shared educational curricula emphasizing core doctrines—ahiṃsā (non-violence), aparigraha (non-possessiveness), samyag-darśana (right faith), samyag-jñāna (right knowledge), and samyag-cāritra (right conduct)—while acknowledging differences in scriptural canons, monastic discipline, and ritual practice. For many youth and second-generation Jains, combined-tradition centers become the primary lens through which Jain identity is experienced, making them crucial sites for modern religious formation.  [[3]](https://settleinamerica.com/guides/indian/bay-area/jain/) [[4]](https://settleinamerica.com/guides/indian/houston/jain/)\n\nCombined-tradition centers also function as laboratories of intra-Jain dialogue. Planning committees must negotiate issues such as idol placement, shared festival calendars, dietary norms in community kitchens, and the invitation of teachers from different lineages. When done carefully, these negotiations deepen lay understanding of doctrinal nuances and cultivate habits of respectful disagreement. When done hastily, they can generate tensions—for example, over the use of microphones during sermons, the presence or absence of images in certain halls, or the scheduling of Paryuṣaṇa and Daśalakṣaṇa observances. Many centers therefore adopt written constitutions that affirm non-partisanship, specify representation from multiple traditions on boards, and emphasize that no single sect’s practices are normative for all.  [[1]](https://southasianherald.com/historic-unity-jain-sects-unite-to-inaugurate-landmark-temple-in-beltsville-maryland/) [[0]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_Center_of_America)\n\nFrom a doctrinal perspective, combined-tradition centers do not attempt to erase historical differences in canons or monastic codes. Instead, they foreground shared ethical commitments and the common goal of liberation (mokṣa) while leaving questions of scriptural authority and ascetic discipline to the respective lineages. In this sense, they are lay-driven institutions shaped by diaspora realities rather than formal theological mergers. Their existence, however, has practical consequences: children grow up seeing multiple ritual forms as equally Jain; inter-sect marriages become more common; and community projects—such as animal shelters, vegan advocacy, or educational outreach—are framed as collective Jain initiatives rather than sect-specific efforts. These developments raise important questions for scholars and practitioners about how Jain identity will be narrated in coming generations, and how traditional authorities will relate to these hybrid spaces.\n\nFinally, combined-tradition centers highlight a tension between preservation and adaptation. On one hand, they protect minority traditions in cities where a single-sect temple might never be built. On the other, they risk flattening complex doctrinal histories into a generic “Jainism” if educational programs do not carefully distinguish between shared foundations and sect-specific teachings. Responsible centers therefore invest in robust pāṭhaśālā curricula, invite teachers from multiple lineages, and encourage youth to study primary texts and commentaries from their own traditions alongside comparative materials. In doing so, they embody a modern application of anekāntavāda: not as relativism, but as disciplined openness to multiple, well-grounded perspectives within a single liberation path.\n",

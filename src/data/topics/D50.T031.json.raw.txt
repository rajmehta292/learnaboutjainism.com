  "overview": "In Jain studies, \"source diversity\" refers to the plurality of textual, linguistic, sectarian, and historical streams through which Jain doctrine, practice, and ethics are transmitted. Rather than a single universally accepted \"Jain Bible,\" the tradition is structured around multiple canons, commentarial lineages, and regional literatures that together form a complex but coherent scriptural ecosystem. Śvetāmbara communities generally recognize a Prakrit Āgama canon preserved through monastic recitation and later redaction, while Digambara communities deny the full authenticity of that canon and locate authority in other early and scholastic works such as the Ṣaṭkhaṅḍāgama, Kasāyapāhuḍa, and philosophical treatises.  [[0]](https://archania.org/p/the-symbolic-world/symbolic-works/literature/religious-texts/jain-agamas) [[1]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Jaina-canon)\n\nThis diversity is not merely a matter of different book lists; it is embedded in Jain understandings of āgama (\"that which has come down\") and siddhānta (authoritative doctrine). Both terms can refer to bodies of teaching that include sermons, narrative, cosmology, ethical reflection, and ritual manuals, as well as later commentaries and independent works. The Śvetāmbara canon is commonly described as comprising Aṅgas, Upāṅgas, Cheda-sūtras, Prakīrṇakas, Cūlikā-sūtras, and Mūla-sūtras, while Digambara authors emphasize doctrinal continuity through non-Āgama works and philosophical syntheses.  [[0]](https://archania.org/p/the-symbolic-world/symbolic-works/literature/religious-texts/jain-agamas) [[1]](https://www.britannica.com/topic/Jaina-canon)\n\nSource diversity also appears in language and genre. Early canonical materials are primarily in Ardhamāgadhī and related Prakrits, transmitted orally for centuries before being written down. Later Jain literature expands into Sanskrit, regional vernaculars (such as Gujarati, Kannada, Hindi, and Tamil), and modern languages including English. These texts range from ascetic rulebooks and cosmological treatises to narrative epics, hymns, philosophical compendia, and scholastic commentaries. The result is a layered tradition in which doctrinal themes—karma, non-violence, vows, cosmology, and liberation—are articulated differently across time, sect, and audience, yet remain recognizably Jain.  [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_literature) [[3]](https://philosophy.institute/religions-of-the-world/exploring-sacred-texts-jainism-guide/)\n\nWithin this plurality, Jain thinkers have developed strategies for acknowledging and managing disagreement. Medieval ācāryas such as Haribhadra Sūri and Abhayadeva Sūri used Prakrit and Sanskrit commentaries to reconcile sectarian tensions, emphasizing shared soteriological goals while allowing for divergent textual authorities. Phrases like \"nānā-maggehi eko maggo\" (\"through various paths, one path\") and \"savve jīvā piyāyarā\" (\"all souls are beloved\") exemplify a discourse that validates diversity within unity, framing multiple scriptural streams as converging on the same ethical and liberative horizon.  [[4]](https://horizon-jhssr.com/articles/authors/pdf-version-18/JHSSR-1318-2025.pdf)\n\nFor contemporary study, source diversity has several implications. First, any serious engagement with Jainism must recognize that claims about \"what Jainism teaches\" may rest on different textual bases depending on whether one is working in a Śvetāmbara, Digambara, or sub-sectarian context (such as Sthānakavāsī or Terāpanth). Second, translation and edition choices matter: Prakrit and Sanskrit terms often carry technical meanings that can be flattened or distorted in modern languages, especially when drawn from unverified or secondary compilations. Third, responsible guidance requires mapping doctrinal statements to specific textual strata—canonical Āgamas, early Digambara treatises, medieval commentaries, or modern community education—while clearly indicating where evidence is thin, disputed, or dependent on particular lineages.  [[0]](https://archania.org/p/the-symbolic-world/symbolic-works/literature/religious-texts/jain-agamas) [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_literature)\n\nFinally, source diversity is a resource rather than a defect. It allows Jain communities to address new ethical questions, social contexts, and intellectual environments by drawing on a wide range of precedents and interpretive tools. At the same time, it demands humility: no single text, translation, or commentator can exhaust the tradition, and many questions—especially those involving initiation, ascetic discipline, or complex ethical dilemmas—properly belong to living lineages and qualified teachers. A careful, source-grounded approach therefore combines respect for canonical and commentarial diversity with transparent citation, acknowledgment of disagreement, and encouragement to consult experienced human guides when decisions carry spiritual, medical, legal, or familial consequences.\n",

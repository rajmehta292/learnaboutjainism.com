  "overview": "The term \"Saṃgrahaṇī\" (also spelled \"Saṃgrahaṇī, Saṃgrahaṇī-grantha, Sangrahani\") refers to a distinctive Jain textual genre devoted primarily to cosmology and the structure of the universe (loka). These works systematize, summarize, and visually present complex doctrinal material about the three worlds—upper, middle, and lower—along with the classification of beings, the geography of Jambūdvīpa and other continents, the arrangement of heavens and hells, and the measures of time and space. They function as pedagogical compendia, bridging canonical sources and later scholastic systematizations for both ascetics and laypeople. [[0]](https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/cosmology-jain-cosmology)\n\nHistorically, Saṃgrahaṇī texts emerge from the broader Jain cosmological tradition that can be traced back to canonical scriptures such as the Vyākhyāprajñapti (Bhagavatī-sūtra), Sthānāṅga-sūtra, and upāṅga texts like the Jambūdvīpaprajñapti. Medieval authors then distilled and reorganized this material into concise, often metrical treatises. The genre is especially associated with Śvetāmbara scholasticism, though Digambara traditions have parallel cosmological compendia. Saṃgrahaṇīs typically present cosmology in a highly structured way—using verses, tables, diagrams, and sometimes elaborate illustrations—to make abstract doctrine accessible and memorable. [[0]](https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/cosmology-jain-cosmology)\n\nAmong the earliest known works in this genre is the Bṛhatsaṃgrahaṇī attributed to Jinabhadra Gaṇin (6th century CE), which offers a relatively extensive treatment of cosmological topics. Later, shorter and more didactic recensions such as the Laghusaṃgrahaṇī compiled by Candra Sūri (often identified with Śrīcandra, 12th century CE) became highly influential. These texts were frequently copied and illustrated, and manuscripts survive in major collections, showing diagrams of the cosmic man (loka-puruṣa), maps of Jambūdvīpa, and charts of the heavens and hells. [[1]](https://jainpedia.org/manuscript/samgrahani-ratna/) [[2]](https://jainpedia.org/manuscript/table-and-text-3/)\n\nA particularly important and widely transmitted Saṃgrahaṇī text is the Saṃgrahaṇī-ratna (also known in some manuscript traditions as Trailokyadīpikā), which encapsulates cosmological doctrine in verse form and is often accompanied by commentaries, such as that of Dharmameru. Manuscripts of Saṃgrahaṇī-ratna show the close integration of text and image: folios combine metrical descriptions with tables and painted diagrams of palaces in the heavens, the various classes of gods (Bhavanavāsin, Vyantara, Vaimānika), and the tortures of hell. These visual elements are not merely decorative; they are didactic tools that help readers internalize complex spatial and numerical structures. [[1]](https://jainpedia.org/manuscript/samgrahani-ratna/) [[2]](https://jainpedia.org/manuscript/table-and-text-3/) [[3]](https://jainpedia.org/manuscript/more-about-palaces-and-heavens/)\n\nDoctrinally, Saṃgrahaṇī texts draw on and presuppose the authoritative synthesis of Jain philosophy found in works like Umāsvāti/Umāsvāmi’s Tattvārtha-sūtra, but their focus is narrower: they concentrate on the ontology and architecture of the universe rather than the full range of tattvas (fundamental principles). They classify beings according to realms (heavens, middle world, hells), body types, lifespans, and karmic conditions, thereby situating ethical and spiritual practice within a vast cosmological framework. For medieval and early modern Jains, knowledge of this cosmology was considered part of basic religious literacy, and Saṃgrahaṇī manuscripts were used in teaching, recitation, and ritual display. [[0]](https://www.encyclopedia.com/environment/encyclopedias-almanacs-transcripts-and-maps/cosmology-jain-cosmology)\n\nFrom a historical and manuscript perspective, Saṃgrahaṇī texts are crucial witnesses to the development of Jain cosmological thought and visual culture. They show how canonical ideas were reconfigured into didactic compendia, how regional styles of illustration evolved, and how cosmology remained a living part of Jain religious imagination. Modern scholarship uses these texts to reconstruct the transmission of cosmological doctrines, to compare Śvetāmbara and Digambara perspectives, and to understand the interplay between text, image, and ritual. At the same time, there are ongoing debates about the precise dating of some recensions, the authorship of particular works, and the extent to which later Saṃgrahaṇīs incorporate non-canonical or regionally adapted material—issues that require careful philological and historical study.\n\nIt is important to distinguish between the Saṃgrahaṇī genre and other Jain cosmological works such as Trilokasāra or Jambūdvīpaprajñapti. While all deal with cosmology, Saṃgrahaṇīs are characterized by their compendious, often mnemonic structure and their close association with illustrated manuscripts. They occupy a middle position between canonical scripture and later encyclopedic treatises, serving as a practical guide to the structure of the universe for students of Jain doctrine.",

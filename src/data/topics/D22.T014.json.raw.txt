  "overview": "In the Śvetāmbara Jain tradition, “stotras” are metrical hymns of praise, reverence, and remembrance, recited as part of daily sādhanā, temple worship, and personal devotion. They occupy a central place in lived Jain practice, standing alongside scriptural study and ethical discipline. While the term “stotra” itself is Sanskrit, many of the most beloved Śvetāmbara stotras are composed in Prakrit (especially Ardhamāgadhī) or mixed Prakrit-Sanskrit, reflecting the linguistic milieu of the canonical and post-canonical Jain literature.\n\nŚvetāmbara stotras typically praise the qualities (guṇa) of the Tīrthaṅkaras, liberated beings (siddhas), and other exalted spiritual figures, rather than petitioning for worldly favors in a theistic sense. The emphasis is on contemplative remembrance of the Jina’s attributes—omniscience, non-violence, detachment, compassion—and on the transformative effect that such remembrance has on the practitioner’s own karmic dispositions. In this way, stotra-recitation is understood as a supportive practice for right faith (samyag-darśana), right knowledge (samyag-jñāna), and right conduct (samyag-cāritra), rather than a substitute for them.\n\nWithin Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka communities, a widely recognized cluster of nine stotras and mantras, known as “Nava Smaraṇa” or “Nav Smaraṇ,” forms a core devotional cycle: the Namaskāra (Navkār) Mantra, Uvasaggaharam Stotra, Śāntikaram Stotra, Tijaypahutta Stotra, Namiuna Stotra, Ajita-Śānti Stavana, Bhaktāmara Stotra, Kalyāṇa Mandira Stotra, and Bṛhat Śānti Stotra. These nine are recited in various combinations in the morning, during temple visits, and on special occasions, and have been studied in detail by modern scholars such as Vinod Kapashi, who analyzes their meters, historical background, and scriptural bases. [[2]](https://www.storiesbyarpit.com/2025/04/tracing-timeless-historical-journey-of.html) In all Śvetāmbara contexts, however, stotras are understood to be efficacious primarily through the cultivation of inner purity, non-violence, and concentration, rather than through mechanical repetition alone.\n\nHistorically, many Śvetāmbara stotras are linked to specific authors and lineages—such as the Bhaktāmara Stotra traditionally attributed to Ācārya Māntuṅga, or peace hymns associated with later scholastic and ritual specialists—while others, like the Namaskāra Mantra, are treated as anādi (beginningless) or at least extremely ancient, embedded in the early Āgama tradition. Modern critical work traces their appearance in texts such as the Bhagavatī Sūtra and later commentarial literature, noting variant readings and sectarian preferences (for example, the use of “namo” vs. “ṇamo” in different recensions).

  "overview": "In Jain philosophy, the relationship between karma and free agency is framed within a rigorously law‑governed, non‑theistic cosmos in which each individual soul (jīva) is eternally real and personally responsible for its own bondage and liberation. Karma is not merely a moral record or psychological tendency; it is conceived as an extremely subtle material substance (pudgala) that interacts with the soul, obscuring its innate qualities of infinite knowledge, perception, bliss, and energy. Through the soul’s own activities of mind, speech, and body, karmic particles flow toward it (āsrava), bind to it (bandha), and condition its experiences across countless lifetimes. [[0]](https://www.spirituality-studies.org/dp-volume12-issue1-spring2026/files/assets/common/downloads/files/12-1-nishit-shah.pdf) [[1]](https://philopedia.org/traditions/jain-philosophy/)\n\nWithin this framework, free agency is affirmed rather than denied. Because there is no creator‑god who bestows grace or predestines outcomes, Jainism locates the entire burden of moral responsibility in the jīva itself. Liberation (mokṣa) is defined as the complete annihilation of all karmic matter and the permanent cessation of new bondage. The classic Tattvārthasūtra lists seven fundamental realities (tattva): soul (jīva), non‑soul (ajīva), influx (āsrava), bondage (bandha), stoppage (saṃvara), dissociation (nirjarā), and liberation (mokṣa). This sequence shows that while karmic causation is strict, the soul can actively block further influx through ethical restraint and ascetic discipline, and can gradually shed existing karmas through austerities and right conduct. [[2]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084587.html) [[3]](https://philosophy.institute/religions-of-the-world/achieving-liberation-jainism-ultimate-goal/)\n\nJain thinkers therefore reject both fatalism and arbitrary freedom. Every action has precise karmic consequences, yet the soul retains the capacity to initiate new courses of conduct that alter its future karmic profile. The doctrine of the three jewels—right faith (samyagdarśana), right knowledge (samyagjñāna), and right conduct (samyakcāritra)—articulates how free agency operates within karmic law. By gaining correct understanding of reality (especially the tattvas) and cultivating non‑violence, truthfulness, non‑stealing, celibacy, and non‑possessiveness, the agent transforms the quality and quantity of karmic influx. Over time, this disciplined agency leads to higher stages of spiritual development (guṇasthāna), culminating in the complete destruction of deluding, knowledge‑obscuring, perception‑obscuring, and energy‑obstructing karmas. [[3]](https://philosophy.institute/religions-of-the-world/achieving-liberation-jainism-ultimate-goal/) [[4]](https://www.indica.today/long-reads/part-1-karma-in-jainism-bondage-and-liberation/)\n\nPhilosophically, Jain karma theory offers a distinctive account of moral continuity and responsibility without divine intervention. Because karma is material, its operation can be described in terms of dravya–guṇa–paryāya (substance–quality–mode): the soul remains an enduring substance whose essential quality is consciousness, while karmic bondage is a modal obstruction that can be removed without altering the soul’s essence. This allows Jain authors to explain how a beginningless chain of causes can coexist with genuine moral agency: the soul is always free in its core nature, yet conditionally bound by its own past choices. Ethical and ascetic practices do not create freedom from nothing; they uncover an already‑present, but obscured, freedom by dissolving karmic accretions. [[0]](https://www.spirituality-studies.org/dp-volume12-issue1-spring2026/files/assets/common/downloads/files/12-1-nishit-shah.pdf) [[1]](https://philopedia.org/traditions/jain-philosophy/)\n\nAt the same time, Jain texts acknowledge practical constraints on agency. A heavily bound soul, burdened by intense deluding karma, experiences strong passions and ignorance that limit its effective freedom. Still, even in such states, the tradition insists that some minimal capacity for self‑restraint and right orientation remains, making moral responsibility meaningful. Differences among Digambara and Śvetāmbara sub‑traditions concern details of karmic classification and practice, but they converge on the core claim: no external savior can override karmic law, and no karmic law can erase the soul’s potential for self‑directed purification. Modern interpreters have explored how this view relates to contemporary debates on determinism and free will, often highlighting Jainism’s middle path between rigid fatalism and unconstrained spontaneity. [[1]](https://philopedia.org/traditions/jain-philosophy/) [[4]](https://www.indica.today/long-reads/part-1-karma-in-jainism-bondage-and-liberation/)\n\nBecause this account is non‑theistic and deeply individualistic, it has significant ethical implications. Responsibility for harm and care extends to all living beings, and every choice—however small—contributes to the long‑term shaping of one’s karmic condition. The emphasis on ahiṃsā (non‑violence) and careful attention to diet, speech, and movement reflects the conviction that free agency is exercised most authentically when it minimizes harm and gradually loosens karmic bonds. In this sense, Jainism treats freedom not as the ability to do anything whatsoever, but as the hard‑won state in which the soul is no longer compelled by passions, ignorance, or karmic residues, and can abide in its own pure, unobstructed nature.",

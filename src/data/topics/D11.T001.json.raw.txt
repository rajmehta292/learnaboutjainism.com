  "overview": "In Jain philosophy, the guṇasthāna model is the canonical map of the soul’s inner evolution from complete delusion to final liberation. It describes fourteen distinct “quality‑states” (guṇa‑sthāna) of the jīva, each defined by the specific configuration of karmic bondage, the degree of right faith (samyagdarśana), right knowledge (samyagjñāna), and right conduct (samyakcāritra), and the intensity of passions (kaṣāya). Rather than being a mere list of stages, the model functions as a precise diagnostic framework: it tells an aspirant what kind of spiritual condition they are in, which karmas are active or subdued, and what transformations are required to move further toward mokṣa.\n\nThe primary purpose of the guṇasthāna system is pedagogical and practical. Pedagogically, it organizes the otherwise subtle and complex doctrine of karma into a graded path that can be taught, memorized, and contemplated. Texts such as the Tattvārtha Sūtra and later manuals explain how each stage corresponds to specific karmic processes—fruition (udaya), suppression (upaśama), and destruction (kṣaya)—so that students can understand liberation not as a sudden miracle but as a law‑governed progression. Practically, the stages serve as a spiritual “chart” for self‑assessment: by examining one’s faith, ethical discipline, and passions, a practitioner can infer their approximate stage and thus identify appropriate vows, meditations, and restraints to cultivate.\n\nThe guṇasthāna model also safeguards the central Jain insight that ethics is inseparable from metaphysics. As some modern scholars note, Jain ethics is “a means to an end”: the end is the soul’s realization of its innate purity, and the means are the gradual weakening and removal of karmic obscurations. By tying each stage to specific ethical qualities—such as the abandonment of wrong belief, the adoption of vows, the reduction of negligence, and the attenuation of subtle passions—the guṇasthānas show that spiritual progress cannot be reduced to intellectual knowledge alone. Right view without corresponding conduct cannot carry the soul beyond the lower stages; conversely, disciplined conduct without right view remains entangled in delusion.\n\nAnother purpose of the guṇasthāna framework is to integrate diverse types of aspirants—lay and monastic—into a single path while still acknowledging differences in capacity and obligation. Early stages are accessible to laypeople who cultivate basic right faith and partial vows, while higher stages presuppose the rigorous discipline of renunciants, including complete non‑violence, celibacy, and freedom from negligence. Yet the model insists that the same fundamental law of karma applies to all souls: every jīva, regardless of sect, gender, or social status, must traverse the same inner transformations if it is to become a siddha (liberated soul).\n\nThe guṇasthānas also serve a doctrinal purpose in clarifying the relationship between omniscience (kevalajñāna) and liberation. The final stages distinguish between the kevalin who still possesses activity of body, speech, and mind (sayogī kevalī) and the kevalin who has completely ceased all activity (ayogī kevalī), immediately followed by the soul’s ascent to the siddha‑śilā. This fine‑grained distinction prevents confusion between the state of omniscience within saṃsāra and the state of complete disembodied freedom beyond it. By situating kevalajñāna within the sequence of guṇasthānas, Jain thinkers emphasize that omniscience is the culmination of a long karmic process, not an arbitrary gift.\n\nHistorically, the guṇasthāna model has also functioned as a shared conceptual bridge across Jain sects. Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions both accept the fourteen stages, even while differing on certain doctrinal details and practical interpretations. Commentarial literature elaborates the stages with examples, analogies, and technical sub‑classifications, but the core purpose remains stable: to provide a universally applicable, law‑like description of how any soul moves from mithyātva (wrong belief) to the complete destruction of passions and karmas. In modern study, the guṇasthānas continue to be used as a framework for teaching Jain ethics, meditation, and karma theory, offering students a structured way to understand spiritual growth as a gradual, internally coherent process.\n\nFinally, the guṇasthāna model has a contemplative purpose. By repeatedly reflecting on the characteristics of each stage, practitioners cultivate viveka (discernment) about their own inner state and the transient nature of karmic conditions. This reflection can inspire humility—recognizing how far one is from the higher stages—and also hope, since every stage is presented as a real possibility grounded in the nature of the soul. In this way, the fourteen guṇasthānas are not merely a theoretical ladder but a living map for ongoing self‑transformation, guiding the aspirant from the darkness of delusion to the luminous stillness of liberation.",

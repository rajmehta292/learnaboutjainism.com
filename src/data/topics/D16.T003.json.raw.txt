  "overview": "Eśanā-samiti (often written as Eshana-samiti) is one of the five samitis, the regulations of activity or modes of careful conduct, central to Jain monastic discipline and, by extension, an ethical ideal for lay followers. It specifically governs the careful, faultless search for and acquisition of necessities—primarily food, but also water, clothing, shelter, and other requisites—so that this process remains free from violence, deceit, attachment, and other karmically harmful states. [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/eshanasamiti) [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/uttaradhyayana-sutra/d/doc424234.html)\n\nIn classical Jain sources, samiti is the technical term for disciplined, vigilant regulation of actions that cannot be entirely abandoned, such as walking, speaking, eating, handling objects, and disposing of waste. For ascetics who still must engage in these activities, samiti ensures that they are performed with maximum non‑violence (ahiṃsā) and inner purity. Eśanā-samiti, therefore, is not merely about the physical act of begging or obtaining food; it is about the entire ethical field surrounding sustenance—how one searches, from whom one receives, in what manner, at what time, and with what mental attitude. [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/uttaradhyayana-sutra/d/doc424234.html) [[2]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084928.html)\n\nTexts describe Eśanā-samiti in terms of avoiding a detailed list of faults in the search, reception, and use of alms. The Uttarādhyayana-sūtra, for example, speaks of “receiving alms in a manner to avoid the forty-two faults,” indicating that the tradition developed a fine-grained taxonomy of errors such as begging from unsuitable donors, accepting impure or harmful food, showing greed or partiality, or causing distress to householders and other beings. The careful ascetic must examine the giver, the gift, the time, and his own intention, ensuring that the act of obtaining food does not become a source of new karmic bondage. [[1]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/uttaradhyayana-sutra/d/doc424234.html) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/eshna_samiti_229999_229999)\n\nHemacandra and later commentators connect Eśanā-samiti with the broader doctrine of dharmopagraha-dāna, the “gift that supports dharma.” Here, the purity of the receiver is defined in part by his observance of the five samitis, including Eśanā-samiti. A monk who practices faultless begging, free from the forty-two faults, becomes a worthy field of merit for donors, and the interaction between giver and receiver becomes mutually purifying rather than karmically entangling. This relational dimension shows that Eśanā-samiti is not only self-regulation but also a way of protecting laypeople from participating in unwholesome acts. [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/definition/eshanasamiti) [[3]](https://jaingpt.org/knowledge/eshna_samiti_229999_229999)\n\nAcross sectarian lines, the five samitis are acknowledged as a core ethical framework. The Tattvārtha-sūtra, authoritative for both Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions, lists Eśanā alongside īryā (careful walking), bhāṣā (careful speech), ādāna-nikṣepa (careful lifting and laying down), and utsarga (careful disposal of waste). Commentators explain that these regulations are instruments of saṃvara, the stoppage of karmic influx: by practicing Eśanā-samiti, the ascetic minimizes harm to subtle living beings involved in food production and handling, and restrains passions such as greed and attachment that typically arise around sustenance. [[2]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/tattvartha-sutra-with-commentary/d/doc1084928.html) [[4]](https://jainworld.jainworld.com/jainbooks/guideline/61.htm)\n\nWhile originally formulated for monks and nuns, Eśanā-samiti has inspired lay ethics as well. Community-oriented expositions encourage householders to internalize its spirit by choosing non-violent, pure, and modest means of livelihood and consumption, avoiding waste, ostentation, and exploitation. In modern contexts, some teachers extend Eśanā-samiti to questions of ethical sourcing, vegetarian or vegan practice, and ecological responsibility, though such applications are interpretive and should be checked against qualified human guidance within living lineages. What remains constant is the core Jain insight: how one acquires and uses necessities is a crucial arena for spiritual progress, and carefulness (samiti) in this domain is indispensable for the path toward liberation.\n",

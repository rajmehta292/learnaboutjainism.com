  "overview": "Investment ethics in Jainism emerges from the broader framework of right livelihood (samyag ajiva), non‑violence (ahimsa), non‑possessiveness (aparigraha), truthfulness (satya), and non‑stealing (asteya). For a Jain layperson (shravaka), the question is not only whether investment generates profit, but whether the means and consequences of that profit are compatible with the path of spiritual purification and the reduction of karmic bondage. Wealth is treated as a powerful but ambivalent instrument: it can support charity, learning, and social welfare, yet it can also intensify attachment, pride, and harm to living beings if pursued without ethical discipline.\n\nFrom a doctrinal perspective, investment decisions fall under the broader category of economic activity and ownership. Jain texts repeatedly insist that the acquisition, preservation, and use of wealth must avoid direct and indirect forms of himsa. This includes avoiding businesses and financial instruments that rely on slaughter of animals, trafficking in living beings, weapons, intoxicants, or activities that cause large‑scale ecological damage. Modern applications extend this logic to sectors such as meat and dairy industries, armaments, tobacco, gambling, and highly speculative trading that encourages greed and psychological harm. Ethical investment, therefore, is understood as channeling capital toward activities that minimize harm, support sustainable livelihoods, and contribute to the welfare of all sentient beings.\n\nAparigraha—non‑possessiveness—plays a central role in shaping Jain investment ethics. The ideal is not the renunciation of all property for laypeople, but the limitation of desires and the refusal to let wealth become an object of obsession or identity. Investments are to be sized and structured in a way that provides for basic needs, family responsibilities, and charitable giving, without sliding into hoarding or reckless risk‑taking. Excessive diversification purely for accumulation, or leveraging that endangers others’ livelihoods, is seen as spiritually counterproductive even if legally permissible. The emphasis is on stewardship: wealth is temporarily entrusted to the householder, who must manage it with mindfulness, restraint, and a sense of responsibility to the wider community of life.\n\nTruthfulness and non‑stealing further refine the Jain view of investment ethics. Satya requires transparency, honest reporting, and avoidance of deceptive schemes, insider manipulation, or misrepresentation of risk. Asteya forbids gaining through exploitation, unfair contracts, corruption, tax evasion, or labor practices that deprive workers of just compensation. In investment terms, this means screening out companies and funds that rely on exploitative supply chains, predatory lending, or fraudulent accounting, and preferring those that uphold fair wages, accurate disclosures, and lawful compliance. The karmic perspective intensifies these concerns: even indirect participation in such structures through shareholding or lending is understood as morally significant.\n\nJain lay codes also highlight the importance of intention and mental discipline. Speculative trading driven by greed, fear, or thrill‑seeking is discouraged because it strengthens passions (kashayas) such as lobha (greed) and krodha (anger), which bind heavy karmas. Long‑term, value‑aligned investing is more compatible with the Jain path, as it encourages patience, analysis, and alignment with ethical criteria rather than impulsive gain. Many contemporary Jain thinkers connect this with modern concepts like ESG (Environmental, Social, and Governance) investing, arguing that Jain principles provide a deeper, non‑anthropocentric foundation for such frameworks by centering all sentient life rather than only human stakeholders.\n\nFinally, Jain investment ethics integrates charity (dana) and social welfare into the very purpose of wealth. Profits are not solely for private consumption; they are to be shared through support of education, healthcare, animal protection, environmental conservation, and religious or community institutions. This redistribution is not merely philanthropic but soteriological: it is seen as a way to reduce attachment, cultivate compassion, and generate meritorious karmas (punya) that support spiritual progress. At the same time, traditional authorities caution that no textual guideline can replace the nuanced judgment of a conscientious layperson and, where needed, consultation with qualified teachers and professionals—especially when investment decisions intersect with medical, legal, or family obligations.",

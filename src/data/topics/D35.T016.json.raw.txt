  "overview": "In Jainism, the term \"gaṇa\" (Sanskrit/Prakrit: गण / गणा) primarily denotes an organized group or cohort of ascetics under the leadership of a senior disciple, especially in the context of the Tirthankara Mahāvīra’s immediate disciples. In Śvetāmbara narrative and canonical literature, gaṇas are the monastic groups headed by gaṇadharas—the chief disciples who systematized and transmitted the Tirthankara’s teachings. Each gaṇa consists of a substantial number of śramaṇas (monks), forming the basic units of early Jain monastic organization.\n\nThe Kalpa Sūtra, a key Śvetāmbara canonical text, explicitly states that Mahāvīra had nine gaṇas and eleven gaṇadharas, and then proceeds to list the leading elders (sthaviras) and the number of monks instructed by each. This passage is central for understanding how the tradition remembers the earliest structure of the Jain saṅgha: large monastic cohorts, each associated with a particular elder, collectively forming the living community around the Tirthankara. [[0]](https://www.wisdomlib.org/jainism/book/kalpa-sutra/d/doc424489.html) [[1]](https://tibetanbuddhistencyclopedia.com/en/index.php/The_kalpa_sutra_of_bhadrabahu_lives_of_the_ginas_list_of_the_sthaviras)\n\nBeyond this specific historical configuration, the concept of gaṇa also functions more generally as a term for a monastic group or lineage cluster. Over time, as Jain communities diversified into major sects (Digambara, Śvetāmbara Mūrtipūjaka, Sthānakavāsī, Terāpanth, etc.), the detailed memory of Mahāvīra’s nine gaṇas remained especially prominent in Śvetāmbara ritual and scholastic contexts, where the Kalpa Sūtra is recited and studied. In these settings, gaṇas are not merely administrative units; they symbolize the continuity of the teaching from the Tirthankara through his gaṇadharas to the wider saṅgha. [[2]](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Jain_monasticism) [[3]](https://digital.lib.washington.edu/researchworks/items/b5e8df6c-a6f0-44ea-9073-44d910f6952e)\n\nFrom a doctrinal perspective, gaṇas are not independent sources of authority parallel to the Tirthankara; rather, they are vehicles of transmission. The gaṇadharas receive the kevalajñāna-based teaching, organize it into doctrinal clusters, and instruct their respective gaṇas of monks. This structure underlies later Jain understandings of scriptural formation and lineage: the canonical corpus is remembered as emerging from the oral teaching of the Tirthankara, articulated by gaṇadharas, preserved by sthaviras, and embodied in monastic groups (gaṇas). While later sectarian histories and scholastic works elaborate on these lineages, the foundational image of nine gaṇas around Mahāvīra remains a key reference point for discussions of early Jain monasticism and textual transmission. It appears in the section often titled “List of the Sthaviras,” where the text enumerates senior monks and the number of disciples they instruct. The wording emphasizes both the numerical structure (nine gaṇas, eleven gaṇadharas) and the pedagogical function of each elder, thereby illustrating how gaṇas operate as organized monastic cohorts under specific leaders. The traditional attribution of the Kalpa Sūtra to Bhadrabāhu is accepted in Śvetāmbara circles, though modern scholarship discusses its complex textual history and later redactions.

  "overview": "In Jain studies, the phrase \"sectarian sources\" refers to the bodies of scripture, scholastic works, and ritual texts that are recognized as authoritative within particular Jain sects—most prominently the Digambara and Śvetāmbara traditions, and within Śvetāmbara, sub‑traditions such as Mūrtipūjaka, Sthānakavāsī, and Terāpanth. Rather than a single universally accepted canon, Jainism presents a plural landscape of textual authority in which different communities trace their lineages of teaching (āgama, siddhānta) through distinct corpora, transmission histories, and interpretive frameworks.\n\nFrom a historical perspective, sectarian sources emerge out of the shared early oral tradition (śrutajñāna) and the gradual loss, redaction, and re‑organization of teachings after Mahāvīra’s nirvāṇa. Śvetāmbara communities preserve a Prakrit canon known as the Āgamas, structured into Aṅgas, Upāṅgas, Cheda‑sūtras, Mūla‑sūtras, Prakīrṇakas, and Cūlikā‑sūtras, and transmitted through monastic recitation and scholastic cataloguing. Digambara communities, by contrast, deny the full authenticity of the Śvetāmbara Āgamas and locate scriptural authority in other early works such as the Ṣaṭkhaṇḍāgama and Kaṣāya‑pāhuḍa, as well as in systematic treatises (śāstras) and later commentarial traditions. Both sides remember a lost twelfth Aṅga, the Dṛṣṭivāda, as a symbol of the fragmentary nature of preserved scripture.\n\nSectarian sources are not limited to canonical lists. They include commentaries (bhāṣya, ṭīkā, vṛtti), doctrinal compendia, narrative literature, ritual manuals, and philosophical treatises that interpret and extend earlier teachings. Śvetāmbara authors such as Haribhadra Sūri, Abhayadeva Sūri, and Śīlāṅka Sūri produced extensive commentaries on the Āgamas, often using Prakrit and Sanskrit to negotiate doctrinal differences and to articulate reconciliation strategies between sectarian positions. Digambara ācāryas such as Kundakunda and Samantabhadra, and later scholastics, systematized doctrine in works like the Samayasāra and Svayambhū‑stotra, grounding their authority in a different remembered scriptural stream. Modern scholarship emphasizes that these corpora are best understood as overlapping symbolic technologies of memory, discipline, and liberation rather than as mutually exclusive “Bibles.”\n\nThe study of sectarian sources therefore requires careful attention to provenance (which community preserves and uses a text), language (Ardhamāgadhī, other Prakrits, Sanskrit, and vernaculars), and genre (canonical sūtra, commentary, philosophical treatise, ritual manual, narrative). It also demands awareness of disagreement: Digambaras and Śvetāmbaras differ over the status of the Āgamas, the completeness of transmission, and the interpretation of monastic rules, while sharing core soteriological structures such as the three jewels (right faith, right knowledge, right conduct), karma theory, and the path through the guṇasthānas. Contemporary Jain communities and scholars increasingly highlight reconciliation discourses within the tradition, showing how sectarian sources themselves sometimes articulate multi‑perspectival (anekāntavāda) approaches that acknowledge diversity of textual authority while affirming a common goal of liberation.\n\nIn academic and community study, it is crucial to distinguish between primary sectarian sources (canonical texts and early commentaries), later doctrinal and ritual elaborations, and modern educational materials. Primary texts and reviewed commentaries (corpus tiers A and B) provide the most reliable basis for understanding how each sect defines scripture and authority. Peer‑reviewed scholarship (tier C) helps map disagreements, transmission histories, and linguistic features, while community education (tier D) often simplifies or harmonizes complex sectarian debates. A responsible guide to Jain sectarian sources will therefore present multiple viewpoints, indicate where evidence is missing or contested, and encourage consultation with qualified teachers within living lineages for questions of practice and discipline.",

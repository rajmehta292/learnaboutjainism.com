  "overview": "In Jain studies, the phrase \"colonial encounters\" refers to the dense web of interactions between Jain communities and the agents of European colonial expansion—administrators, missionaries, Orientalist scholars, and commercial networks—roughly between 1750 and 1950. These encounters did not simply impose an external gaze on a passive community; they generated new forms of self-description, legal and economic negotiation, religious contestation, and scholarly collaboration that continue to shape how Jainism is understood today. [[0]](https://www.soas.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2022-06/The%20Jains%20in%20the%20Colonial%20World%20A%20Review%20IJJS%20Vol%2017%20No2%202021%201-13%20file156570.pdf) [[1]](https://books.google.com/books/about/Cooperation_Contribution_and_Contestatio.html?id=Ub_rzQEACAAJ)\n\nEarly colonial references to Jains appear in travelogues and administrative reports where they are often subsumed under broader categories such as \"bania\" or \"mercantile castes.\" In regions like Gujarat and the Coromandel Coast, British officers and missionaries gradually recognized that some of these trading groups followed distinctive religious practices—strict vegetarianism, temple worship of Jinas, and elaborate rules of non-violence—that did not fit neatly into existing Christian or Islamic taxonomies. Alexander Walker of Bowland’s early nineteenth‑century \"Account of the Jeyn\" is a key example: he struggled to classify Jains, yet acknowledged that their \"religious tenets are different\" from those of surrounding communities. [[2]](https://www.ioa.uni-bonn.de/aik/de/forschung/publikationen/medien/leseprobe_saac_6.pdf) [[3]](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/48679297.pdf)\n\nOrientalist scholarship in the nineteenth century marked a second major phase of colonial encounters. European scholars, often working with Jain pandits and monks, began editing and translating canonical texts in Ardhamāgadhī Prakrit and Sanskrit, such as the Kalpa‑sūtra and Tattvārthasūtra. These projects helped crystallize \"Jainism\" as a distinct religion in the emerging comparative study of religions, even though Jains themselves had long maintained a clear sense of their identity. The so‑called \"Jacobi moment\"—Hermann Jacobi’s 1879 edition of the Kalpa‑sūtra—has often been cited as the point at which Western academia formally recognized Jainism as separate from Buddhism and Hinduism, but recent scholarship shows that colonial officials and missionaries were already grappling with Jain distinctiveness decades earlier, drawing heavily on information provided by Jain interlocutors. [[0]](https://www.soas.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2022-06/The%20Jains%20in%20the%20Colonial%20World%20A%20Review%20IJJS%20Vol%2017%20No2%202021%201-13%20file156570.pdf) [[3]](https://www.jstor.org/stable/pdf/48679297.pdf)\n\nMissionaries formed another crucial strand of colonial encounters. In South India, for example, Protestant missionaries on the Coromandel Coast wrote polemical accounts of Jain temple worship and ascetic practices, sometimes portraying Jains as overly attached to ritual and wealth, sometimes admiring their ethical rigor. These missionary narratives circulated in Europe and influenced Orientalist understandings of Jainism, creating a feedback loop in which European scholarship and missionary critique reinforced each other. At the same time, Jains responded with apologetic writings, public debates, and philanthropic projects that defended their tradition against Christian critiques, while selectively adopting modern educational and print technologies introduced under colonial rule. [[2]](https://www.ioa.uni-bonn.de/aik/de/forschung/publikationen/medien/leseprobe_saac_6.pdf) [[4]](https://networks.h-net.org/node/22055/reviews/10579423/maes-luithle-hardenberg-and-cort-and-orr-cooperation-contribution)\n\nEconomically, many Jain groups were deeply embedded in the colonial economy as bankers, traders, and industrialists. Their commercial success brought them into close contact with colonial administrators and legal systems, leading to new forms of corporate organization, philanthropy, and public representation. Jain merchants funded schools, hospitals, libraries, and temple renovations, often framing these projects as both religious merit and civic contribution to the colonial city. Legal disputes over temple property, image worship, and sectarian control—especially between Digambara and Śvetāmbara sub‑traditions—were sometimes adjudicated in colonial courts, forcing Jain leaders to articulate doctrinal positions and historical claims in the language of modern law and evidence. [[0]](https://www.soas.ac.uk/sites/default/files/2022-06/The%20Jains%20in%20the%20Colonial%20World%20A%20Review%20IJJS%20Vol%2017%20No2%202021%201-13%20file156570.pdf) [[1]](https://books.google.com/books/about/Cooperation_Contribution_and_Contestatio.html?id=Ub_rzQEACAAJ)\n\nThese encounters also reshaped internal Jain identities. Under colonial scrutiny, distinctions between Digambara and Śvetāmbara, and within Śvetāmbara between Mūrtipūjaka, Sthānakavāsī, and Terāpanthī, were increasingly described in print, census categories, and legal documents. Reform movements emphasized scriptural authority, rational ethics, and social respectability, sometimes downplaying practices that seemed vulnerable to missionary criticism, such as elaborate image rituals or certain forms of ascetic extremity. At the same time, Jain intellectuals collaborated with European scholars to produce grammars, catalogues, and critical editions, thereby contributing directly to the academic field of Jainology while asserting their own interpretive authority. [[1]](https://books.google.com/books/about/Cooperation_Contribution_and_Contestatio.html?id=Ub_rzQEACAAJ) [[4]](https://networks.h-net.org/node/22055/reviews/10579423/maes-luithle-hardenberg-and-cort-and-orr-cooperation-contribution)\n\nBy the late colonial period, Jain participation in nationalist politics, social reform, and global networks added further layers to these encounters. Some Jains supported anti‑colonial movements and used the language of ahimsa to critique imperial violence; others focused on protecting religious institutions and economic interests within the colonial framework. Diasporic Jain communities that began to form in East Africa, Burma, and later in Europe and North America carried with them memories of colonial rule and its legal, educational, and commercial structures. The legacy of colonial encounters is thus visible today in the categories used in censuses and scholarship, the prominence of certain texts in global Jain self‑presentation, and ongoing debates about how to balance scriptural tradition, modern ethics, and historical consciousness.\n\nFrom a Jain perspective, these colonial encounters are not merely a chapter of external history; they are part of the karmic and social conditions (paryāya) through which the community has navigated its commitment to non‑violence, truth, and non‑possessiveness. Modern Jain reflection on colonialism often emphasizes both the harms of imperial domination and the opportunities that arose for articulating Jain values in new idioms—legal, academic, and political. A careful study of this period therefore requires attention to primary documents produced by colonial agents and Jain authors alike, as well as sensitivity to the ways in which later scholarship has interpreted, and sometimes misinterpreted, these complex encounters.",

In Jain studies, these commentaries are indispensable for understanding not only the doctrinal meaning of earlier Prakrit and Sanskrit scriptures, but also the social, sectarian, and intellectual worlds in which commentators lived. Because commentators often situate themselves within lineages, mention patrons, describe controversies, and respond to rival interpretations, their works preserve historical data that is absent or only implicit in the root texts they explain. Śvetāmbara canonical commentaries in Sanskrit from roughly the 8th–13th centuries—by figures such as Śīlaṅka, Abhayadeva, and Malayagiri—demonstrate how exegetes embedded doctrinal clarification within narratives of sectarian identity, monastic discipline, and canon formation. These texts can be read both \"vertically\" (as interpretations of specific sūtras) and \"horizontally\" (as witnesses to broader debates, such as disputes over authority between gacchas or sects). [[0]](https://www.researchgate.net/profile/Royce_Wiles/publication/236682580_Svetambara_Jain_Canonical_Commentators_Writing_in_Sanskrit/links/00b7d519037a9afaad000000/Svetambara-Jain-Canonical-Commentators-Writing-in-Sanskrit.pdf) [[1]](https://brill.com/view/journals/nu/72/5-6/article-p545_5.xml)\n\nCommentaries also function as evidence for linguistic and literary history. Medieval Jain ācāryas employed Prakrit and Sanskrit in sophisticated ways to negotiate sectarian tensions, using semantic neutralization, paradoxical formulations, and hierarchical reframing to reconcile divergent positions. For example, studies of Haribhadra Sūri, Abhayadeva Sūri, and Śīlaṅka Sūri show how commentators used inclusive formulas like \"savve jīvā piyāyarā\" (all souls are beloved) and \"nānāmaggehi eko maggo\" (through various paths, one path) to emphasize shared soteriological foundations while acknowledging doctrinal diversity. Such rhetorical strategies reveal how language itself became a tool of peace-building and identity construction, making commentaries crucial evidence for the history of sectarian relations. Commentators write from particular institutional locations—gacchas, lineages, or courts—and their accounts of rivals, reforms, or controversies are shaped by polemical aims. Adaptations of earlier narratives, such as different Dharmaparīkṣā versions produced within the Śvetāmbara Tapā Gaccha, show how authors rework inherited stories to assert exclusive identities and claim canonical authority for their own group. These adaptations are historically revealing precisely because they are not neutral: they expose the strategies by which communities construct literary heritage to overcome internal crises and consolidate power. [[1]](https://brill.com/view/journals/nu/72/5-6/article-p545_5.xml)\n\nTherefore, commentary literature occupies a dual role. It is derivative in the sense that it depends on prior scriptures, yet it is original as a witness to the historical moment of its composition. When used as historical evidence, commentaries must be cross-checked against inscriptions, manuscripts, colophons, and non-Jain sources, and their genre conventions—such as hyperbolic praise, sectarian polemic, or idealized narratives of lineage—must be carefully distinguished from verifiable data.

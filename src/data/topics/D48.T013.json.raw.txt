  "overview": "In Jain traditions, “modern retelling” is best understood within a much older and deeply rooted practice of narrative adaptation, transcreation, and vernacularization. Jain authors have long engaged with authoritative stories—about tīrthaṅkaras, exemplary kings, lay ethics, and polemical debates—and reshaped them for new audiences, languages, and social contexts. Contemporary retellings in print, digital media, theatre, film, and children’s literature continue this lineage rather than breaking from it.\n\nScholars now often use the term “literary transcreation” to describe how Jain storytellers rework inherited narratives while preserving their ethical and doctrinal core. Recent research highlights that Jain communities have produced multiple versions of the same story in Prakrit, Sanskrit, Apabhraṃśa, Old Hindi, Kannada, and modern vernaculars, each calibrated to its audience’s expectations and the religious concerns of its time. These processes include expansion or condensation of episodes, shifts in narrative voice, insertion of didactic passages, and re-framing of characters to emphasize nonviolence, restraint, and right faith. [[0]](https://www.cbs.ugent.be/publications/publication-highlights-q2-20254-literary-transcreation-as-a-jain-practice/) [[1]](https://biblio.ugent.be/publication/01JJKW7XG73BBFBMHHDTAA21GS)\n\nIn the present day, modern retellings appear in several recognizable forms. There are prose and verse adaptations of classical works for lay readers, often simplifying technical vocabulary while retaining key doctrinal motifs such as karma, saṃvara (stoppage), and nirjarā (shedding). There are children’s books and comics that recast canonical stories—like those of King Kumārapāla, the Pāṇḍavas, or Nemi—to foreground moral choices in everyday life. There are sectarian and community publications that re-present older śrāvakācāra (lay conduct) manuals in contemporary idiom, addressing issues such as migration, professional ethics, and environmental responsibility. Academic and community collaborations also produce translations with commentary, which then inspire popular retellings in blogs, podcasts, and social media posts.\n\nModern retelling is not merely aesthetic; it is a mode of resilience and ethical negotiation. By adapting narratives, Jain communities respond to changing social realities—urbanization, diaspora life, interreligious contact, and new media technologies—while reaffirming core commitments to ahiṃsā, anekāntavāda, and self-discipline. Recent studies show how narrative traditions like the Dharmaparīkṣā, or biographies of figures such as Kumārapāla, are repeatedly reconfigured to address new controversies, internal debates, and questions of identity. In this sense, modern retelling is both conservative and creative: it conserves a recognizable frame of meaning while creatively re-inscribing it in new historical moments. [[2]](https://brill.com/display/title/74066) [[3]](https://brill.com/abstract/journals/nu/72/5-6/nu.72.issue-5-6.xml)\n\nFrom a Jain perspective, the legitimacy of a modern retelling depends less on literal fidelity to a single manuscript and more on whether the retelling supports right view (samyagdarśana), encourages ethical practice, and respects the authority of the broader scriptural and commentarial tradition. Many contemporary authors explicitly situate their work as an adaptation of a known text or lineage, acknowledging both continuity and change. This self-awareness helps distinguish responsible retellings—grounded in primary sources and living traditions—from purely commercial or sensationalized uses of Jain motifs. For serious study or practice, modern retellings are best read alongside primary texts and qualified commentaries, with attention to where the narrative has been simplified, re-framed, or updated for modern concerns.\n\nBecause Jainism places great value on careful speech and truthful representation, modern retellings carry ethical responsibilities. They can open doors for youth and newcomers, making complex doctrines accessible and inspiring. Yet they can also unintentionally flatten doctrinal nuance or sectarian diversity if they present one version as universally authoritative. A reflective approach treats modern retellings as gateways: they invite interest and moral reflection, but they are complemented by deeper engagement with canonical scriptures, classical narrative literature, and guidance from knowledgeable teachers within recognized lineages.",
